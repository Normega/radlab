# Auto-advance, back navigation, progress indicators, items-per-screen guidance, and inattentive responding by presentation format

Scope note: these notes cover (a) navigation features in item-by-item web questionnaires (auto-advance / auto-forward, back button, progress indicators) and (b) published guidance and experimental evidence on items per screen for rating scales, plus speeding / careless-responding evidence relevant to online panels (Prolific, MTurk, student pools). Every finding carries a link to the page it was read from. Where a full text could not be opened, that is stated in Gaps. Bibliographic details marked "(unverified)" come from secondary reference lists or search metadata, not from the article itself.

---

## 1. What do experiments show about auto-advance (automatic forward navigation)?

### Takeaway
Across six-plus experiments, auto-advance reliably shortens completion time a little on PCs (roughly 7% per page up to about 1/3 of total time), but the saving is small or absent on smartphones and can be eaten up by extra navigation clicks. It does not measurably hurt missing data, reliability, mean scores, "don't know" use or breakoff. Respondents change answers less often (roughly half as often in one large study) and usually rate auto-advance as easier, although a sizeable minority wrongly believe they cannot go back. No study found a data-quality *cost* for simple single-choice rating items. The one caution is a possible quality loss on cognitively demanding questions.

### Cited Findings

**Hays et al. (2010), US health-survey panel, the original experiment**
- Citation: Hays, R. D., Bode, R., Rothrock, N., Riley, W., Cella, D., & Gershon, R. (2010). The impact of next and back buttons on time to complete and measurement reliability in computer-based surveys. *Quality of Life Research*, 19(8), 1181–1184 (Bakker et al.'s reference list gives 1181–1184; one abstract page gave 1181–1191, so the end page is unverified). https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-010-9682-9 — [abstract via BSL](https://erasmusmc.mijn.bsl.nl/the-impact-of-next-and-back-buttons-on-time-to-complete-and-meas/271924); [Bakker et al. 2022 reference list](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/view/2021.10)
- Design: 807 Polimetrix/PollingPlace panelists (mean age 53, 64% female, 87% White, 81% some college or more) were randomized to a 2×2 design: automatic advance vs. a "next" button after each response, crossed with back navigation allowed vs. not allowed. — [Hays ISOQOL slides (UCLA)](https://labs.dgsom.ucla.edu/file/48668/Hays-Oct31-ISOQOL-PROMIS.pdf); [search summary of same](https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20552282/)
- Completion time was about 50% longer when a next button was required, which is roughly 2 extra seconds per item. — [abstract via BSL](https://erasmusmc.mijn.bsl.nl/the-impact-of-next-and-back-buttons-on-time-to-complete-and-meas/271924)
- Missing data, reliability and mean scale scores were similar with and without the next button. — [abstract via BSL](https://erasmusmc.mijn.bsl.nl/the-impact-of-next-and-back-buttons-on-time-to-complete-and-meas/271924)
- Allowing back navigation reduced the number of respondents answering suspiciously quickly (under 2 seconds per item). — [abstract via BSL](https://erasmusmc.mijn.bsl.nl/the-impact-of-next-and-back-buttons-on-time-to-complete-and-meas/271924)
- The authors recommended automatic advancement to the next item with the option to go back to the previous item. — [Hays ISOQOL slides](https://labs.dgsom.ucla.edu/file/48668/Hays-Oct31-ISOQOL-PROMIS.pdf)

**Selkälä & Couper (2018), Finnish students, two experiments**
- Citation: Selkälä, A., & Couper, M. P. (2018). Automatic versus manual forwarding in web surveys. *Social Science Computer Review*, 36(6), 669–689. https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317736831 (volume and pages from Bakker et al.'s reference list) — [U. Lapland record](https://research.ulapland.fi/fi/publications/automatic-versus-manual-forwarding-in-web-surveys/); [ISR record](https://api.isr.umich.edu/?p=196436)
- Two experiments among students in Finland compared auto-forwarding (AF) with manual forwarding (MF) under varied cognitive effort (information accessibility, consistency requirements). — [U. Lapland record](https://research.ulapland.fi/fi/publications/automatic-versus-manual-forwarding-in-web-surveys/)
- AF was faster only on PCs and tablets. There was **no time advantage for smartphone users**. — [U. Lapland record](https://research.ulapland.fi/fi/publications/automatic-versus-manual-forwarding-in-web-surveys/)
- MF respondents changed answers more often than AF respondents. AF slightly reduced straightlining. — [U. Lapland record](https://research.ulapland.fi/fi/publications/automatic-versus-manual-forwarding-in-web-surveys/)
- Respondents reported more positive experiences with AF, but AF "may decrease the quality of responses to cognitively demanding questions." — [U. Lapland record](https://research.ulapland.fi/fi/publications/automatic-versus-manual-forwarding-in-web-surveys/)

**Selkälä, Callegaro & Couper (2020), follow-up analysis**
- Citation: Selkälä, A., Callegaro, M., & Couper, M. P. (2020). Automatic versus manual forwarding in web surveys: A cognitive load perspective on satisficing responding. In G. Meiselwitz (Ed.), *Social Computing and Social Media* (HCII 2020, LNCS 12194, pp. 130–155). Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-49570-1_10 — [Google Research page](https://research.google/pubs/automatic-versus-manual-forwarding-in-web-surveys-a-cognitive-load-perspective-on-satisficing-responding/)
- They report MF "reducing both average item level response times as well as the standard deviation" relative to AF, which they read as satisficing. Their argument is that navigation buttons add extraneous cognitive load, while a simpler AF interface frees resources for the response task. The sample was university students and the survey was brief. — [Google Research page](https://research.google/pubs/automatic-versus-manual-forwarding-in-web-surveys-a-cognitive-load-perspective-on-satisficing-responding/)
- Bakker et al. summarize this paper as showing that "auto-forwarding seems to decrease satisficing behavior." — [Bakker et al. 2022](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/view/2021.10)

**Giroux, Tharp & Wietelman (2019), law students, very large randomized test**
- Citation: Giroux, S., Tharp, K., & Wietelman, D. (2019). Impacts of implementing an automatic advancement feature in mobile and web surveys. *Survey Practice*, 12(1). https://doi.org/10.29115/SP-2018-0034 — [Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)
- Sample: 39,350 law students invited at 77 US/Canadian schools, 20,574 responded (52.28%), 18.1% on mobile. Here "auto-advance" meant **auto-scrolling to the next question on the same page**, not page turning. — [Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)
- Completion time showed mixed results across six pages, with no consistent, practically meaningful difference. — [Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)
- Item nonresponse: no difference on mobile. Desktop *manual* users left more items unanswered (1.06 vs. 0.83). — [Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)
- Straightlining did not differ (0.57 vs. 0.55 grid sets). Breakoff was 13.99% (auto) vs. 14.68% (manual), not significant. — [Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)
- **Answer changes were about 45–48% lower with auto-advance**, consistently across pages. This was the largest effect in the study. — [Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)
- Auto-advance was rated easier to use (6.49 vs. 6.36 on a 7-point scale) and better in visual design (1.54 vs. 1.64 on a 4-point scale, where lower is better). — [Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)
- **More than one-third of auto-advance users said they had difficulty changing answers, believing the feature prevented revision, which it did not.** The authors recommend clear instructions or a warning telling respondents that they can change answers. — [Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)

**Bakker et al. (2022), Statistics Netherlands general-population health survey**
- Citation: Bakker, J., Haan, M., Schouten, B., Struminskaya, B., Lugtig, P., Toepoel, V., Giesen, D., & Meertens, V. (2022). Testing the effects of automated navigation in a general population web survey. *methods, data, analyses*, 16(1), 107–128. https://doi.org/10.12758/mda.2021.10 — [mda article page](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/view/2021.10); [full-text PDF](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)
- Design: 2,098 invited, 1,535 started, 1,461 completed (RR1 69.6%), with a €5 unconditional incentive. The survey was long: 409 questions on 220 pages, median 29.2 min. Respondents were randomized to AF or MF. **The AF version kept visible previous and next buttons.** AF applied to 75.5% of pages. Pages with check-all-that-apply or open questions stayed manual. Respondents were not told about AF beforehand. — [full-text PDF](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)
- Time: AF saved 0.65 s per page on average (10.97 vs. 11.61 s), or about 68.9 s over roughly 107 pages. On AF-eligible pages (single-choice or matrix) the saving was 0.72 s, or 7.1% (10.16 → 9.44 s, p < .001). Manual pages showed no difference. — [full-text PDF](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)
- Navigation: AF increased "previous" clicks (mean 2.9 vs. 1.9) and unnecessary "next" clicks (28.1 vs. 13.5), largely cancelling the time saving. Unnecessary next clicks were most frequent on **pages that held multiple single-choice questions**. Previous-button use under AF peaked on cognitively demanding questions, at new topics, and where wording resembled an earlier item. — [full-text PDF](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)
- Satisficing proxies did not differ. "I'd rather not say" was used at least once by 80.1% (AF) vs. 79.0% (MF), and "don't know" by 33.3% vs. 30.6% (both n.s.). — [full-text PDF](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)
- Breakoff: 74 respondents (4.8%) broke off, 45.9% of them in AF and 54.1% in MF. Breakoff varied little by device. — [full-text PDF](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)
- Recommendation: AF can reduce completion time, but think carefully before mixing manual and automatic forwarding in one survey. Navigation should be predictable within a survey and across devices (citing Antoun et al. 2018), and respondents should be told their navigation options (citing Giroux et al. 2019). — [full-text PDF](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)
- Bakker et al.'s literature summary: most studies find AF shortens response times (Hays et al. 2010; Roberts et al. 2012; Selkälä & Couper 2018 for PCs only; Lugtig et al. 2019). Some find no time effect (Arn et al. 2015; de Bruijne 2015; Selkälä & Couper 2018 for smartphones), and one finds longer times under AF (Roberts et al. 2013). Respondents rate AF surveys as more enjoyable, more interesting, less difficult and less lengthy (Roberts, de Leeuw, Hox, Klausch & de Jongh 2012). Possible downsides include confusion, accidental skipping that raises item nonresponse (de Bruijne 2016), and discouraged answer revision. — [full-text PDF](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)

**Roßmann, Gummer et al., German smartphone experiment**
- In an experiment with a probability-based online panel recruitment, smartphone respondents needed **20% less time** when the questionnaire used autoforwarding. They did not rate the survey more favourably, and they were not more likely to sign up for the panel. — [SSOAR record 63138](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/handle/document/63138)

**Arn, Klug & Kołodziejski (2015), DemoSCOPE panel**
- Citation: Arn, B., Klug, S., & Kolodziejski, J. (2015). Evaluation of an adapted design in a multi-device online panel: A DemoSCOPE case study. *methods, data, analyses*, 9(2), 185–212. https://doi.org/10.12758/mda.2015.011 — [mda article page](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/view/2015.011)
- This was a before/after comparison (not randomized) of a mobile-adapted design, looking at completion rate, item nonresponse, straightlining, question timing and interview length. — [mda article page](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/view/2015.011)
- Bakker et al. list it among studies finding **no completion-time effect** of auto-forwarding. — [Bakker et al. full text](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)

### Inferences
- For short single-response rating items (Likert or VAS-style radio buttons), auto-advance has a consistent small benefit (time, perceived ease) and no documented quality cost in missing data, reliability, means, straightlining, DK use or breakoff. The quality caution from Selkälä & Couper (2018) concerns *cognitively demanding* questions, which most standard rating-scale items are not.
- The 45–48% drop in answer changes (Giroux) and the "MF changes answers more" result (Selkälä & Couper) can be read two ways: fewer fixes of mis-taps, or fewer second thoughts. No study separates these, so a lab adopting auto-advance should keep a visible Back control and log every answer event, including revisions.
- The time saving is smallest on phones (none in Selkälä & Couper; 20% in Roßmann et al.), so time is a weak argument for auto-advance on a phone-heavy sample. Consistency and lower tap count are the stronger arguments.
- Bakker's extra "next" clicks came mostly from *multi-question* pages with AF. Auto-advance therefore fits a strict one-item-per-screen format and behaves worst when mixed into pages with several items. This suggests one predictable rule per instrument: either one item per screen with AF, or several items per page with a manual Next, not a hybrid.
- Several sources recommend telling respondents up front that answers can be changed (Giroux; Bakker), because a large minority otherwise assume they cannot go back.

### Gaps
- I could not open the full text of Selkälä & Couper (2018), so effect sizes for time, answer changes and straightlining are not given here, only the direction from the abstract.
- I could not open the full text of Arn et al. (2015), so its numbers and whether its design used auto-advance specifically are unconfirmed. The "no time effect" claim rests on Bakker et al.'s summary.
- The Roßmann/Gummer study's exact authors, title, venue and year could not be confirmed from the SSOAR record snippet. Retrieve it before citing formally.
- Roberts et al. (2012, 2013), de Bruijne (2015/2016) and Lugtig et al. (2019) are known only through Bakker et al.'s summary. Their primary sources were not checked.
- **No study found measures "accidental/erroneous responses" (mis-taps that auto-advance commits) directly.** Answer changes are the only proxy. No study was found on Prolific or MTurk samples specifically, or on auto-advance with sliders or VAS (where release-to-advance raises a separate mis-touch risk).

## 2. Back navigation and answer review: effects on data quality

### Takeaway
The evidence is thin but consistent. Making a back button available costs nothing in completion time, missing data or reliability. It reduced the number of ultra-fast (<2 s/item) responders in the one experiment that crossed it with auto-advance. Respondents use it rarely, and mostly on demanding or repetitive items.

### Cited Findings
- In Hays et al.'s 2×2 design, the back-button option reduced the number of respondents answering in under 2 s per item. Missing data, reliability and scale means were similar across conditions. The authors recommended auto-advance **with** back navigation. — [abstract via BSL](https://erasmusmc.mijn.bsl.nl/the-impact-of-next-and-back-buttons-on-time-to-complete-and-meas/271924); [Hays slides](https://labs.dgsom.ucla.edu/file/48668/Hays-Oct31-ISOQOL-PROMIS.pdf)
- Back use is infrequent: about 2–3 previous-page clicks per respondent across a 220-page survey (AF mean 2.9, MF mean 1.9). It concentrates on cognitively demanding questions, topic transitions and items that resemble earlier ones. — [Bakker et al. full text](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)
- Under AF, back use did not differ between auto-forwarded and manual pages, suggesting respondents were not confused by the switch. — [Bakker et al. full text](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)
- More than one-third of auto-advance users believed they could not change answers. Auto-advance can therefore suppress *perceived* reviewability unless back/revision is signalled. — [Giroux et al. 2019](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)

### Inferences
- A visible Back control is cheap insurance with auto-advance. It gives a way to recover from a mis-tap that auto-advance would otherwise commit, and in Hays et al. it coincided with fewer speeders.
- Because back use is rare and clustered, a platform that logs back events per item gets a usable paradata signal of item difficulty or confusion at little cost.
- Allowing back navigation means a participant can revise an earlier answer. If the platform is append-only (a re-answered item is a new row, not an overwrite), the revision is preserved as data. This fits the lab's "flag, don't drop" rule but needs an analysis rule for which answer counts.

### Gaps
- No experiment was found that isolates back-button availability in a manual-next design on modern mobile devices or crowdsourced samples.
- No study was found on whether a "review all answers before submit" screen improves or harms data quality for rating scales.

## 3. Progress indicators

### Takeaway
A meta-analysis of 32 experiments finds that a constant-speed progress bar does **not** reduce breakoff. Fast-to-slow bars reduce it and slow-to-fast bars increase it, and a constant bar *increased* breakoff where a small incentive was promised. Do not add a progress bar expecting to cut dropout.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Villar, A., Callegaro, M., & Yang, Y. (2013). Where am I? A meta-analysis of experiments on the effects of progress indicators for web surveys. *Social Science Computer Review*, 31(6), 744–762. https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439313497468 (DOI unverified) — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/14427/); [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/where-am-i-a-meta-analysis-of-experiments-on-the-effects-of-progress-indicators-for-web-surveys/)
- It covered 32 randomized experiments comparing drop-off with and without a progress indicator, classifying indicators as constant, fast-to-slow or slow-to-fast. — [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/where-am-i-a-meta-analysis-of-experiments-on-the-effects-of-progress-indicators-for-web-surveys/)
- A constant indicator did not significantly reduce drop-off. Fast-to-slow reduced it and slow-to-fast increased it. Among studies promising a small incentive, a constant indicator increased drop-off. — [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/where-am-i-a-meta-analysis-of-experiments-on-the-effects-of-progress-indicators-for-web-surveys/); [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/14427)

### Inferences
- Prolific participants and course-credit students are paid or credited samples, which is the subgroup where a constant bar *raised* drop-off. That argues against a progress bar as a retention device on this platform.
- If progress is shown for orientation (e.g., "item 4 of 7" within a short instrument), it should be accurate. Deliberately fast-to-slow indicators reduce breakoff but misrepresent the remaining length, which raises an ethics question. This is an inference, not a finding from the sources.

### Gaps
- I found no study on progress indicators in **one-item-per-screen** formats specifically, and none on data-quality outcomes (as opposed to breakoff) from progress indicators.
- The Villar et al. effect sizes (odds ratios) were not retrieved.

## 4. Published best-practice guidance on items per screen (web and mobile), especially for multi-item rating scales

### Takeaway
Guidance has converged on three points: avoid conventional grids/matrices on smartphones, prefer item-by-item formats for comparability across devices, and put the same thing on screen for every device. Two sources openly accept *several* related items on one scrolling page rather than strictly one per screen, mainly to limit page loads (Pew 2015). Experiments show that more items per screen shortens completion but raises item nonresponse and lowers respondents' evaluation of the layout (Toepoel et al. 2009). Large grids with long scales (9–11 points) break measurement equivalence (Liu & Cernat 2018).

### Cited Findings

**Design heuristics and organizational guidance**
- Antoun, C., Katz, J., Argueta, J., & Wang, L. (2018). Design heuristics for effective smartphone questionnaires. *Social Science Computer Review*, 36(5), 557–574. This systematic review of 2007–2016 smartphone design studies recommends "optimizing" for smartphones, fitting content to screen width to prevent horizontal scrolling, and choosing simpler question types (single-choice, multiple-choice, text entry) over large grids, drop boxes and sliders. — [MPRC record / search summary](https://www.popcenter.umd.edu/mprc-associates/antoun/christopher-antoun-publications/articlereference.2019-03-29.9422701861)
- Its five heuristics are readability, ease of selection, visibility across the page, simplicity of design elements, and **predictability across devices**. — [MPRC record / search summary](https://www.popcenter.umd.edu/mprc-associates/antoun/christopher-antoun-publications/articlereference.2019-03-29.9422701861)
- Pew Research Center (McGeeney, K., 2015, June 11; updated July 1). *Tips for creating web surveys for completion on a mobile device*. Recommendation 4 is to "avoid" grids, because they encourage straightlining and require vertical plus horizontal scrolling on phones, which raises abandonment. Recommendation 5 says "it's okay to put multiple questions on the same screen," grouped by topic, because one question per page means slower mobile load times. Navigation buttons should sit at the bottom so users scroll past all content. Sliders and spin wheels should be avoided. 27% of American Trends Panel respondents then answered on smartphones and 8% on tablets. — [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/methods/2015/06/11/tips-for-creating-web-surveys-for-completion-on-a-mobile-device/)
- AAPOR Task Force: Link, M. W., Murphy, J., Schober, M. F., Buskirk, T. D., Childs, J. H., Tesfaye, C. L., Callegaro, M., Cohen, J., Dean, E., Harwood, P., Pasek, J., & Stern, M. (2014). *Mobile technologies for conducting, augmenting and potentially replacing surveys* (AAPOR Task Force report). One of its themes is that online surveys must be treated as mixed-device surveys. — [Google Research record](https://research.google/pubs/mobile-technologies-for-conducting-augmenting-and-potentially-replacing-surveys/); [RTI record](https://www.rti.org/publication/mobile-technologies-conducting-augmenting-and-potentially-replacing-surveys-0)
- In mobile-optimized questionnaires, survey software commonly decomposes grids into item-by-item format on small screens. — [Revilla et al., UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/63708937-1d64-4ea1-a98f-78faa1a3017b/full)
- The US Census Bureau has published a usability evaluation of grid redesign for the mobile-optimized National Survey of College Graduates (RSM2017-14). — [Census working paper](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2017/adrm/rsm2017-14.html)

**Experimental evidence on number of items per screen**
- Toepoel, V., Das, M., & van Soest, A. (2009). Design of web questionnaires: The effects of the number of items per screen. *Field Methods*, 21(2), 200–213. The same items were shown as 1, 4, 10 or 40 per screen. Format had no effect on the substantive (arousal) index. **Item nonresponse increased with the number of items per screen.** More items per screen shortened the interview but **worsened respondents' evaluation of the layout**. — [Tilburg University record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/design-of-web-questionnaires-the-effects-of-the-number-of-items-p/); [CentERdata](https://www.centerdata.nl/publicaties/design-of-web-questionnaires-the-effects-of-the-number-of-items-per-screen)
- Couper, M. P., Traugott, M., & Lamias, M. (2001). Web survey design and administration. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 65(2), 230–253. Among 1,602 University of Michigan students, one experiment compared several related items on one screen with one question per screen. — [ISR record](https://api.isr.umich.edu/?p=187434); [search summary](https://chnm.gmu.edu/digitalhistory/links/pdf/chapter6/6.26.pdf)

**Grid vs. item-by-item, PC and mobile**
- Couper, M. P., Tourangeau, R., Conrad, F. G., & Zhang, C. (2013). The design of grids in web surveys. *Social Science Computer Review*, 31(3), 322–345 (volume and pages unverified). https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439312469865. In two experiments, dynamic feedback within a grid reduced missing data, and **splitting grids into component questions further reduced missing data and motivated underreporting**. — [ISR record](https://api.isr.umich.edu/?p=187431); [search summary](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1212903573627412480)
- Mavletova, A., Couper, M. P., & Lebedev, D. (2018). Grid and item-by-item formats in PC and mobile web surveys. *Social Science Computer Review*, 36(6) (pages unverified). This was a two-wave experiment in a Russian opt-in panel (wave 1 N = 1,678, wave 2 N = 1,079), crossing format with device. Straightlining was higher in the grid. Measurement error was somewhat higher and concurrent validity lower for grids on both PC and mobile. Grids also increased reported technical difficulties and lowered survey evaluations. — [HSE publications](https://publications.hse.ru/en/view/211416329); [ISR record](https://api.isr.umich.edu/?p=196428)
- Liu, M., & Cernat, A. (2018). Item-by-item versus matrix questions: A web survey experiment. *Social Science Computer Review*, 36(6), 690–706. https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439316674459. Scales of 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9 and 11 points were crossed with format. Straightlining and response time were similar across formats at all scale lengths. **Item nonresponse was higher for matrices, especially on mobile.** The two formats were measurement-equivalent below 7 options, but **9- and 11-option matrices differed substantially** from item-by-item. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0894439316674459); [MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/95842-item-by-item-versus-matrix-questions-a-web-survey-experiment)
- Revilla, M., Toninelli, D., & Ochoa, C. An experiment comparing grids and item-by-item formats in web surveys completed through PC and smartphones (Spain, fielded Feb–Mar 2015; *Telematics and Informatics*, 2017, volume and pages unverified). Respondents were randomized to PC, non-optimized smartphone, or optimized smartphone. Inter-item correlations were similar. Grids took longer on smartphones. Splitting grids reduced nondifferentiation on smartphones. **Item-by-item for both PC and smartphone was judged the best way to make devices comparable.** — [UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/63708937-1d64-4ea1-a98f-78faa1a3017b/full)
- DeBell, M., Wilson, C., Jackman, S., & Figueroa, L. (2021). Optimal response formats for online surveys: Branch, grid, or single item? *Journal of Survey Statistics and Methodology*, 9(1), 1–24 (citation from search metadata; findings not retrieved). — [search result](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0894439318773733)
- An eye-tracking result cited in a 2023 *Journal of Official Statistics* paper: respondents processed the question stem and scale labels significantly more in item-by-item designs than in large grids, while in grids they often referred to surrounding items when judging. — [JOS 2023 PDF (search summary)](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/jos-2023-0004.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a phone-heavy sample, the evidence points away from matrix/grid presentation of rating scales: worse straightlining (Mavletova), more missing data (Liu & Cernat; Couper et al. 2013; Toepoel), and non-equivalence with long scales (Liu & Cernat). The real choice is between **(i) one item per screen** and **(ii) several stacked items on one scrolling page** (each item with its own full response scale, no grid).
- Among published sources, one item per screen is the format best supported for *cross-device comparability* (Revilla et al.; Antoun's "predictability across devices"), and it gives each item its own unambiguous timestamp. The main cost named in guidance is page-load overhead (Pew), which hardly applies to a single-page app that swaps items client-side without a server round-trip. This is an inference from the Pew rationale.
- Toepoel's result that nonresponse rises with items per screen is less relevant when every item is required. On a platform that enforces responses, the comparable risk moves to random or straightlined answers on long pages.

### Gaps
- **Textbook guidance not verified.** I could not access Dillman, Smyth & Christian (2014), *Internet, Phone, Mail, and Mixed-Mode Surveys: The Tailored Design Method* (4th ed., Wiley); Couper (2008), *Designing Effective Web Surveys* (Cambridge UP); Callegaro, Lozar Manfreda & Vehovar (2015), *Web Survey Methodology* (Sage); or Tourangeau, Conrad & Couper (2013), *The Science of Web Surveys* (Oxford UP). Their specific recommendations on items per screen, grids and auto-advance are therefore not reported here. Check them against the books before quoting.
- **GESIS Survey Guidelines:** I did not locate a GESIS guideline page specifically on grids or items per screen, so no GESIS recommendation is reported.
- **Couper, Traugott & Lamias (2001) results** (commonly cited as higher inter-item correlations and faster completion when items share a screen) and Tourangeau, Couper & Conrad (2004, *POQ* 68(3)), which is often cited for the same "items on the same screen correlate more" effect, were not verified against the articles.
- DeBell et al. (2021) findings were not retrieved.
- Gallup Panel and LISS-specific written guidance was not found.

## 5. Inattentive responding / speeding in online panels by presentation format, and how timing-based screens behave per page vs. per item

### Takeaway
Prolific (and CloudResearch) produce markedly better attention and quality metrics than MTurk or general opt-in panels, and student pools sit in between. Speeding is a stable, person-level trait concentrated in younger respondents and strongly linked to straightlining in grids. The standard time screen (about 2 s per item, applied **per page**) has been validated as a page-level measure. Item-per-screen formats measure time per item directly, but auto-advance removes the ~2 s per item that a Next click adds, so cutoffs borrowed from paged/manual designs will flag more people unless recalibrated. No experiment was found that directly compares careless-responding rates between one-item-per-screen and multi-item pages on Prolific/MTurk.

### Cited Findings

**Platform-level data quality**
- Douglas, B. D., Ewell, P. J., & Brauer, M. (2023). Data quality in online human-subjects research: Comparisons between MTurk, Prolific, CloudResearch, Qualtrics, and SONA. *PLOS ONE*, 18(3), e0279720. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0279720 — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0279720)
  - The 106-item Qualtrics survey (about 8 min) contained 5 attention checks, a 50-item personality inventory and a video-recall task. Samples: MTurk 500, CloudResearch 505, Prolific 496, Qualtrics 575, SONA undergraduates 555. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0279720)
  - The speed criterion was finishing in more than 3 minutes, matching roughly 1 second per question plus a 75-second video. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0279720)
  - Prolific and CloudResearch participants were more likely than MTurk, Qualtrics or SONA participants to pass attention checks, follow instructions, remember information, have unique IP/geolocation, and work slowly enough to read the items. — [Paperity abstract](https://paperity.org/p/305672321/data-quality-in-online-human-subjects-research-comparisons-between-mturk-prolific)
  - **Conflicting figures:** one summary gives the "high-quality" share as Prolific 67.9%, CloudResearch 62.0%, Qualtrics 53.2% and MTurk 26.4% ([search summary of abstract](https://paperity.org/p/305672321/data-quality-in-online-human-subjects-research-comparisons-between-mturk-prolific)). The automated read of the full article gave approximate figures of Prolific ~82%, CloudResearch ~80%, SONA ~68%, Qualtrics ~65% and MTurk ~50% ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0279720)). The two sets probably reflect different definitions (strict all-criteria vs. a looser metric), but I could not confirm which is which. Check the paper before quoting.
  - Cost per high-quality respondent was Prolific $1.90, CloudResearch $2.00, MTurk $4.36 and Qualtrics $8.17. SONA was $0 but took 14 weeks to recruit. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0279720)
- Peer, E., Rothschild, D., Gordon, A., Evernden, Z., & Damer, E. (2022). Data quality of platforms and panels for online behavioral research. *Behavior Research Methods*, 54, 1643–1662. Two studies with about 4,000 participants measured attention, comprehension, honesty and reliability. Without reputation filters, only Prolific gave high quality on all measures. With filters, CloudResearch and Prolific were both high, and MTurk stayed low even with approval-rating filters. — [HUJI CRIS](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/data-quality-of-platforms-and-panels-for-online-behavioral-resear-2/); [Peer lab page](https://eyalpeer.huji.ac.il/ODQ)
- Careless-responding prevalence: about 10–12% in undergraduate samples (Meade & Craig, 2012), with published estimates ranging from about 3.5% to 30–40% depending on the instrument (Curran, 2016). These are secondary citations in later papers. — [Frontiers/PMC secondary review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8725053); [Metapsychology secondary](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2637/3070)

**Speeding and its relation to straightlining**
- Zhang, C., & Conrad, F. G. (2014). Speeding in web surveys: The tendency to answer very fast and its association with straightlining. *Survey Research Methods*, 8(2), 127–135. https://doi.org/10.18148/srm/2014.v8i2.5453 — [SRM](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/5453)
  - The sample was the LISS probability panel, N = 5,523 with response times on 54 questions. Speeding was defined **per question** as faster than 300 ms per word of question text (versus about 200 ms/word comprehension reading speed for college students). — [SRM full text](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/5453/5344)
  - Respondents sped on a mean of about 15 of 54 questions, and the top quartile on 21 or more. Speeding was **stable across the questionnaire**: 66.9% of the lowest-quartile and 65.0% of the highest-quartile speeders stayed in the same quartile in the second half. — [SRM full text](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/5453/5344)
  - "Persistent speeders" were over 40% of 18–34-year-olds but under 5% of those 65 and older. — [SRM full text](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/5453/5344)
  - More speeding went with more straightlining in grids regardless of demographics. Among low-education respondents speeding was associated with a "drastic increase" in straightlining. — [SRM abstract](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/5453)

**Per-page vs. per-item time screens**
- Huang, J. L., Curran, P. G., Keeney, J., Poposki, E. M., & DeShon, R. P. (2012). Detecting and deterring insufficient effort responding to surveys. *Journal of Business and Psychology*, 27(1), 99–114 (volume and pages unverified). Its **page-time index** counts the questionnaire pages a participant completes faster than 2 seconds per item. — [secondary description (Frontiers)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.784375/full); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/226222354_Detecting_and_Deterring_Insufficient_Effort_Responding_to_Surveys)
- Bowling, N. A., Huang, J. L., Brower, C. K., & Bragg, C. B. (2023). The quick and the careless: The construct validity of page time as a measure of insufficient effort responding to surveys. *Organizational Research Methods*, 26(2), 323–352. Page time had better validity than total completion time, and the 2-seconds-per-item rule gave a construct-valid page-time score across items of different word lengths. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356814910_The_Quick_and_the_Careless_The_Construct_Validity_of_Page_Time_as_a_Measure_of_Insufficient_Effort_Responding_to_Surveys); [search summary](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.784375/full)
- Even 5–10% careless respondents can bias results, and bias grows with their share. — [Frontiers (2021) secondary](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.784375/full)
- Requiring a Next click adds about 2 s per item (Hays et al. 2010). AF pages ran about 0.7 s (7%) faster per page in Bakker et al. — [Hays abstract](https://erasmusmc.mijn.bsl.nl/the-impact-of-next-and-back-buttons-on-time-to-complete-and-meas/271924); [Bakker et al.](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)

**Format and straightlining (overlaps with section 4)**
- Grids produced more straightlining than item-by-item formats (Mavletova et al. 2018), while Liu & Cernat (2018) found similar straightlining. The evidence is mixed, but no study shows item-by-item formats *increasing* straightlining. — [HSE](https://publications.hse.ru/en/view/211416329); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0894439316674459)
- Splitting grids reduced nondifferentiation on smartphones (Revilla et al.). — [UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/63708937-1d64-4ea1-a98f-78faa1a3017b/full)

### Inferences
- **Timing screens depend on format.** The 2 s/item page-time rule (Huang; Bowling) was developed and validated on *multi-item pages with manual navigation*. In a one-item-per-screen, auto-advance format, the measured time per item excludes the ~2 s Next click (Hays) and the scroll-and-scan time of a multi-item page. A 2 s/item cutoff applied to item-level latencies from an auto-advance format would therefore be stricter than its validated use and would flag more people. The lab should either recalibrate (e.g., a words-based threshold such as 300 ms/word, as in Zhang & Conrad, or a cutoff tied to the platform's own latency distribution) or apply the rule to a *summed* instrument time (total time across the scale's items ÷ number of items).
- One item per screen produces per-item latencies, which allow item-level speeding flags and person-level "persistent speeder" classification as in Zhang & Conrad. Per-page timing cannot do this, so the item-level format adds analytic capability.
- Because speeding is a stable trait that skews young (Zhang & Conrad), samples of university students and young Prolific workers are the high-risk groups. The format choice should be paired with a pre-registered timing screen, not relied on alone.
- Platform choice (Prolific over MTurk) matters more for data quality than any presentation-format effect documented here, judging by the size of the effects in Douglas et al. and Peer et al. compared with the small format effects in sections 1 and 4.

### Gaps
- **No experiment was found that compares careless responding or attention-check failure between one-item-per-screen and multi-item-page formats on Prolific, MTurk or student pools.** This is the main evidence gap for the lab's decision.
- I could not retrieve Meade & Craig (2012) or Curran (2016) directly. Their prevalence figures come from secondary citations. The usual citations, unverified here, are: Meade, A. W., & Craig, S. B. (2012). Identifying careless responses in survey data. *Psychological Methods*, 17(3), 437–455; and Curran, P. G. (2016). Methods for the detection of carelessly invalid responses in survey data. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 66, 4–19.
- Douglas et al.'s headline percentages conflict between sources, as noted above.
- No study was found that validates the 2 s/item rule on item-level latencies from auto-advance designs.

## 6. Respondent burden and perceived length / satisfaction by format

### Takeaway
Respondents tend to rate item-by-item and auto-advance designs more favourably on ease and layout, even though multi-item pages finish faster. Grids lower survey evaluations and raise reported technical difficulty. The satisfaction gains from auto-advance are small, and in one smartphone study they were absent.

### Cited Findings
- Multiple items per screen shortened the interview but **worsened respondents' evaluation of the questionnaire layout**. — [Toepoel et al. 2009, Tilburg record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/design-of-web-questionnaires-the-effects-of-the-number-of-items-p/)
- Grid format increased reported technical difficulties and lowered subjective evaluation of the survey on PC and mobile. — [Mavletova et al. 2018, HSE](https://publications.hse.ru/en/view/211416329)
- Grids took longer on smartphones. — [Revilla et al., UPF](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/63708937-1d64-4ea1-a98f-78faa1a3017b/full)
- Auto-advance was rated easier to use (6.49 vs. 6.36 / 7) and better in visual design. — [Giroux et al. 2019](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)
- AF respondents reported more positive experiences. — [Selkälä & Couper 2018](https://research.ulapland.fi/fi/publications/automatic-versus-manual-forwarding-in-web-surveys/)
- AF surveys were rated more enjoyable, more interesting, less difficult and less lengthy (Roberts et al. 2012, as summarized by Bakker et al.). — [Bakker et al. full text](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)
- Smartphone respondents with autoforwarding were 20% faster but did not evaluate the survey better and were not more willing to join the panel. — [SSOAR 63138](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/handle/document/63138)
- Mobile respondents are more likely to abandon a survey when frustrated by grids and scrolling. — [Pew 2015](https://www.pewresearch.org/methods/2015/06/11/tips-for-creating-web-surveys-for-completion-on-a-mobile-device/)
- Respondents are less willing to complete longer surveys on smartphones (Hintze et al. 2014, cited by Bakker et al.), and surveys still take longer on smartphones even when mobile-friendly (Couper, Antoun & Mavletova 2017; Couper & Peterson 2017, cited by Bakker et al.). — [Bakker et al. full text](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)

### Inferences
- For a phone-heavy population, one item per screen with auto-advance plus a visible Back control is the format best aligned with the satisfaction evidence. The time cost of item-by-item presentation is partly offset by auto-advance on PCs and is small on phones in either case.
- Perceived length matters for paid samples: Prolific participants see the estimated duration up front. Because multi-item pages are objectively faster (Toepoel), the lab should benchmark actual median durations by format before setting Prolific time estimates and pay. This is an inference, not a sourced finding.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures perceived burden or satisfaction by format on Prolific or MTurk specifically, or in repeated-measures / EMA-style daily use where the same scale is answered many times. Habituation to auto-advance over repeated sessions is untested (Giroux et al. list it as future work).
- No breakoff comparison between one-item-per-screen and stacked-items-per-page formats on phones was found.
