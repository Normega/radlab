# Items per screen in PC web surveys: experiments on item-by-item vs. stacked multi-item pages vs. grids

Scope: randomized experiments in desktop/PC web administration (device findings are noted where a PC paper also reports them). Three formats are distinguished throughout:
- **(a) item-by-item / paging**: one item per screen.
- **(b) stacked / scrolling multi-item page**: several items on one page, *each with its own repeated response options* (no shared header). Literature labels: "expanded" (Bell 2001), "scrolling" or "vertical scrolling" item-by-item (Vehovar 2023; Mavletova 2018; Revilla 2017), "multiple headers" (Toepoel 2005/2009), "all-at-once" with per-item dropdowns (Nosek 2012), "single screen but separated, not in a grid" (Yan 2005).
- **(c) grid/matrix**: shared response header, items as rows.

**Caution on terminology for the report writer:** "item-by-item" is used inconsistently in this literature. In Couper 2001, Tourangeau 2004, Toepoel 2009, Callegaro 2009, Roßmann 2018, DeBell 2021, Neuert 2023 and the GSS it means **one item per page (a)**. In Mavletova 2018, Revilla 2017, Revilla & Couper 2018 and Vehovar's "scrolling" layout it means **items stacked on one page with repeated response options (b)**. Comparisons labelled "grid vs. item-by-item" are therefore not all the same contrast.

## Q1. How do formats affect inter-item correlations and internal consistency (alpha)? Is there a "near means related" proximity effect, and how large?

### Takeaway
Putting items together on one screen, especially in a grid, tends to raise inter-item correlations and alpha a little. The effect is not consistent. Large effects come from short scales with uniform keying (Tourangeau 2004: alpha .62 in one grid vs .51 one item per screen; Roßmann 2018 Exp 2: .60 vs .41). Long or mixed-keyed scales show near-zero differences (Toepoel: .879–.887 across 1/4/10/40 per screen; Nosek: .880 vs .878), and some studies show the reverse, with single-item pages having *higher* alpha (Callegaro 2009 n.s.; Nosek 2012 Exp 2; Garland 2009). Several authors read the grid's higher alpha as partly artifactual, driven by straightlining and missed reverse-keyed items. Construct and criterion validity is equal or better in separated formats.

### Cited Findings
**Proximity / "near means related" (grids vs. separate screens)**
- Tourangeau, Couper & Conrad (2004), "Spacing, Position, and Order: Interpretive Heuristics for Visual Features of Survey Questions," *Public Opinion Quarterly* 68(3):368–393, doi:10.1093/poq/nfh035. Their Experiment 6 put a battery on one screen, on two screens, or one item per screen. The abstract says: "The intercorrelations among the items were highest when they were all on the same screen," and that respondents "expect items that are physically close to be related to each other conceptually." — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/68/3/368/1846396)
- Numbers for Tourangeau 2004 Exp 6, as reported secondhand by Callegaro et al. 2009: N = 2,568 opt-in panel members, 8 items (4 diet attitudes, 4 eating behaviours), 7-point agree–disagree scale labelled at the endpoints only. Cronbach's alpha was **.621 for a single grid, .562 for two screens with 4-item grids, and .511 for one item per screen**, a significant difference. Mean nondifferentiation was .436, .422 and .412 respectively, also significant. — [Callegaro et al. 2009, AAPOR/JSM proceedings](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf). MeasuringU's secondary summary gives the same alphas (.62 vs .51) and says separate pages took about 50% longer. — [MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/grids-responses/)
- Tourangeau 2004 also looked at the two reverse-worded items. Their part-whole correlations were weaker in the single grid (r = −.331 and −.097) than on two screens (−.395 and −.151) or eight screens (−.427 and −.187). The authors read this as respondents using proximity as a cue "perhaps at the expense of reading each item carefully." This is reported secondhand in Toepoel et al. 2005. — [Toepoel, Das & van Soest 2005, CentER DP 2005-114](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download)
- Peytchev (2006) reanalysed the Tourangeau 2004 data with SEM. The standardized validity coefficients for the two latent constructs rose "consistently ... as questions were separated on different screens," **from 0.82 to 0.94**. This is reported secondhand in Callegaro 2009. — [Callegaro et al. 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf). Vehovar et al. similarly cite Peytchev & Tourangeau (2005): "construct validity was higher in item-by-item layouts than in grids, despite higher interitem consistency in grids." — [Vehovar, Couper & Čehovin 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)
- Couper, Traugott & Lamias (2001), "Web Survey Design and Administration," *POQ* 65(2):230–253, doi:10.1086/322199. The sample was 1,602 University of Michigan students, and one experiment varied "several related items on one screen" against "one question per screen." — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/65/2/230/1890069). Secondary accounts of the results: correlations were "consistently higher among items appearing together on a screen," but "the overall effect is not large, and none of the differences between each pair of correlations reach statistical significance," and factor structures were similar. — [Toepoel et al. 2005](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download). Callegaro 2009 describes the design as a 5-item knowledge measure in a grid vs. 5 single pages, plus an 11-item Likert measure on 11 screens vs. 3 screens of 4/4/3-item grids, with slightly higher correlations in the grid that were not significant. — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
  - *Conflict:* Callegaro 2009 gives the Couper 2001 sample as "8,747 University of Michigan students." The POQ abstract says "A sample of 1,602 students was sent an e-mail invitation." 8,747 may be the sampling frame. Use 1,602 as the invited sample.
- Silber, Roßmann & Gummer (2018), "When near means related: evidence from three web survey experiments on inter-item correlations in grid questions," *International Journal of Social Research Methodology* 21(3):275–288, doi:10.1080/13645579.2017.1381478. The title and later citations indicate it found grids raise inter-item correlations. Neuert et al. 2023 cite it for "artificially high inter-item correlations (Silber et al. 2018) due to respondents consistently giving the same answer." — [Neuert, Roßmann & Silber 2023, JOS](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/jos-2023-0004.pdf). **I could not obtain the effect sizes.**
- Roßmann, Gummer & Silber (2018), "Mitigating Satisficing in Cognitively Demanding Grid Questions," *JSSAM* 6(3):376–400, doi:10.1093/jssam/smx020. Alpha was higher in the grid than on single-item pages: **Exp 1 (5 items, 11-point scale, N = 2,503 German opt-in panel) .68 vs .64 (p = .026); Exp 2 (7 items, N = 529 GESIS probability pilot panel) .60 vs .41 (p = .001)**. In the same experiments the grid also had more straightlining (see Q2), so the higher alpha comes alongside lower differentiation. — [OUP full text](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/6/3/376/4562352)

**Studies finding little or no difference, or the reverse**
- Toepoel, Das & van Soest (2009), "Design of Web Questionnaires: The Effects of the Number of Items per Screen," *Field Methods* 21(2):200–213, doi:10.1177/1525822X08330261. The 40-item Arousal Seeking Tendency scale was given at 1, 4, 10 or 40 items per screen. Alpha was **.8801 (1 per screen), .8849 (4), .8871 (10), .8788 (40)**. Variance explained by components with eigenvalue >1 was 58%, 54%, 57% and 55%. Reverse-worded items related more weakly to the total when items shared a screen, but "none of the differences between each pair of correlations reach statistical significance." — [Toepoel et al. 2005 working paper](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download); published abstract: "no effect of format on the arousal index" — [Crossref/Field Methods](https://doi.org/10.1177/1525822X08330261)
- Callegaro, Shand-Lubbers & Dennis (2009), "Presentation of a Single Item versus a Grid: Effects on the Vitality and Mental Health Scales of the SF-36v2 Health Survey," AAPOR 2009 / JSM Proceedings pp. 5887–5897. The sample was KnowledgePanel (probability), 2,500 sampled, 1,419 analysed after excluding MSNTV and iPhone/PDA users, five conditions of about 280 each. Pooled alpha for Vitality was **.823 grid vs .851 single-item** and for Mental Health **.849 grid vs .868 single-item**; neither difference was significant. Spearman correlations between reversed and straight item sums were also slightly higher for single-item (MH .535 vs .593; Vitality .614 vs .652; n.s.). This is the opposite direction to Tourangeau 2004, and both scales mix item keying. — [Callegaro 2009 PDF](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- Nosek, Sriram & Umansky (2012), "Presenting Survey Items One at a Time Compared to All at Once Decreases Missing Data without Sacrificing Validity in Research with Internet Volunteers," *PLoS ONE* 7(5):e36771, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0036771. Exp 1 used the 12-item SDO: **alpha .880 all-at-once vs .878 one-at-a-time**. Exp 2 used the 30-item MFQ: average subscale alpha was **.679 all-at-once vs .705 one-at-a-time**, higher for one-at-a-time on all five subscales by bootstrap (p < .001): Harm .650/.660, Fairness .581/.622, Ingroup .679/.721, Authority .686/.715, Purity .801/.807. — [PMC3355147](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3355147/)
- Bell, Mangione & Kahn (2001), "Randomized testing of alternative survey formats using anonymous volunteers on the World Wide Web," *JAMIA* 8(6):616–620 (PMC130072). The SF-36 was presented as a "matrix" vs. an "expanded" format. "Neither health status scores nor alpha reliability coefficients were substantially influenced by the survey format." — [PMC130072 (abstract via search)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC130072); Callegaro: the multi-item expanded format "did not yield lower Cronbach's alpha scores for the eight SF-36 scales" — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- Yan (2005), PhD dissertation, University of Maryland. Six risky-behaviour items, N = 2,587 opt-in panel, three conditions: single screens, one scrolling screen with separate items not in a grid, and a grid. Alpha rose from single-item to grid, but the difference was only marginally significant. Callegaro prints "p=0.98", which is presumably a typo for .098. — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf). Vehovar et al. list Yan 2005 among studies with "little or no difference." — [Vehovar et al. 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)
- Garland (2009), SSI industry report, SurveySpot panel, conditions grid / multiple items per screen / single item per screen. The single-item version "yielded double the variance explained" in factor analysis, recovered the intended number of factors, and had a **higher** alpha than the grid versions. This is a non-peer-reviewed industry study, reported via Callegaro 2009. — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- Mavletova, Couper & Lebedev (2018), "Grid and Item-by-Item Formats in PC and Mobile Web Surveys," *Social Science Computer Review* 36(6):647–668, doi:10.1177/0894439317735307. The design was two waves, a Russian opt-in panel, 1,678 respondents in wave 1 and 1,079 in wave 2. Concurrent validity was **lower in the grid** on both PC and smartphone. There was "almost no significant effect of the question format on test–retest correlations," and measurement equivalence was partial, with differences on the longer (7-point) scale. — [Crossref abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317735307); slide figures show grid vs. item-by-item validity correlations such as .47 vs .60 and .43 vs .57 (Z = 3.74, p < .001) — [ESRA 2017 slides](https://hse.ru/data/2017/10/15/1159283029/Mavletova%20et%20al_Grids_item-by-item_ESRA_2017.pdf)
- Vehovar, Couper & Čehovin (2023), "Alternative Layouts for Grid Questions in PC and Mobile Web Surveys," *SSCR* 41(6):2122–2144, doi:10.1177/08944393221132644, n = 4,644. "For interitem consistency, grids had no advantage on PCs, while they actually underperformed on SPs for neuroticism (22%)." The study used Big Five items with alternating keying, and the authors conclude "the supposed advantage of higher consistency disappeared with alternating item scale directions," which "confirms warnings about artificially high item correlations in grids." — [Vehovar et al. 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)
- Liu & Cernat (2018), "Item-by-item Versus Matrix Questions: A Web Survey Experiment," *SSCR* 36(6):690–706, doi:10.1177/0894439316674459. Matrix and item-by-item formats were measurement-equivalent with fewer than 7 response options. "For matrices with 9 or 11 response options, analyses reveal substantial differences compared to item-by-item questions." — [Crossref abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439316674459)
- Grady, Greenspan & Liu (2019), "What Is the Best Size for Matrix-Style Questions in Online Surveys?" *SSCR* 37(3):435–445, doi:10.1177/0894439318773733. With grids of 5/10/20 rows by 3/5/7 columns, "three columns produced lower scale reliability." — [Crossref abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439318773733)
- Gottfried, Michele & Ježek (2022), "Proximity of Items Does Not Necessarily Affect Their Correlation," *European Journal of Psychological Assessment* 38(4):282–292, doi:10.1027/1015-5759/a000661. This tested sequential proximity (adjacent items in randomized order), not screen layout. Personality inventories, N = 742 and 205 mostly undergraduates: "we found no evidence of such effect ... we failed to conceptually replicate the findings of previous studies." — [Crossref abstract](https://doi.org/10.1027/1015-5759/a000661)

**Mechanism evidence (eye-tracking)**
- Neuert, Roßmann & Silber (2023), "Using Eye-Tracking Methodology to Study Grid Question Designs in Web Surveys," *Journal of Official Statistics* 39(1):79–101, doi:10.2478/JOS-2023-0004. Lab study, N = 132 recruited (103 to 125 analysed), three 10-item questions shown as a large grid (10 per page), two small grids (5 per page), or item-by-item (10 pages). Of grid respondents, **16–25% changed at least one answer after reading later items** (Trust 21% small vs 28% large; Rights 19% vs 29%; BFI-10 12% vs 19%). The authors read this as the "near means related" heuristic at work. Differentiation did not differ (Pd e.g. .55/.51/.56, F = 0.94, p = .39). Fixation on response-scale labels was highest in item-by-item; for Q2 the means were large grid 4.7 s, small grid 6.4 s, single 10.0 s (F = 19.1, p < .001). — [JOS PDF](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/jos-2023-0004.pdf)

### Inferences
- Where the grid raises alpha, the gain runs from about 0 to .19. It is largest for short, uniformly keyed sets (4–8 items). It is near zero for long scales (40-item ASTS, 12-item SDO) and tends to vanish or reverse with mixed keying (SF-36 Vitality/MH, Big Five, MFQ).
- Higher alpha in grids comes with more straightlining and weaker reverse-item correlations in Tourangeau 2004 and Roßmann 2018. Validity results run the other way: the Peytchev SEM 0.82→0.94, Mavletova's lower concurrent validity, and Garland. Together these support reading grid alpha as partly method variance rather than better measurement. Alpha is therefore a poor outcome for comparing formats; validity coefficients and reverse-item consistency are more informative.
- The "near means related" heuristic is well supported for **layout** proximity, through correlations and eye-tracking answer changes. Pure sequential adjacency in randomized order does not show it (Gottfried 2022).

### Gaps
- Effect sizes from Silber, Roßmann & Gummer (2018), which has three experiments, could not be retrieved because the paper is paywalled and SSOAR blocked automated access.
- Couper 2001 correlations and the Tourangeau 2004 numbers are known only through secondary sources (Callegaro 2009, Toepoel 2005, MeasuringU). They are consistent with each other but not checked against the original tables.
- No meta-analysis pooling alpha or correlation differences by format was found (see Q3).

## Q2. Effects on straightlining/nondifferentiation, item nonresponse, breakoff, completion time, and other satisficing indicators

### Takeaway
On PCs, grids are the fastest format only when compared with one-item-per-page paging. They are not faster than stacked or scrolling item-by-item pages (Vehovar 2023; Bell 2001; Nosek 2012). Grids usually produce more straightlining, roughly 1.3–1.6× (e.g., 16% vs 10% and 10% vs 4% in Roßmann 2018; OR about 1.34–1.38 in Mavletova 2018), though some well-powered studies find none (Liu & Cernat 2018; Callegaro 2009; Neuert 2023). Item nonresponse rises with the number of items on a page whether or not a grid is used (Toepoel: 10% → 19% of respondents with any missing item from 1 to 40 per screen; Nosek: missing items roughly halved with one-at-a-time; Lozar Manfreda: higher on one scrolling page). Breakoff rarely differs by format on PCs, except with very tall grids (Grady 2019) or long paging surveys (Vehovar 2023).

### Cited Findings
**Completion time**
- Toepoel 2005/2009 (CentERpanel, a probability-based Dutch panel; N = 2,565 at T1, repeated on N = 2,350 at T2). Mean seconds were **456 (1 per screen), 400 (4), 367 (10), 392 (40)**, F = 4.20, p = .01. The difference was significant only between 1 and 10 per screen. At T2 the relation was linear (more items per screen, shorter interview). The authors attribute the upturn at 40 items to scrolling back to see the header. — [Toepoel 2005 WP](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download)
- Callegaro 2009: median **44–46 s for the grid groups vs 69–72 s for single-item groups** on 9 SF-36 items. — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- Roßmann 2018: **Exp 1 grid 29.1 s vs single 38.6 s; Exp 2 37.2 s vs 48.1 s** (mouse clicks 8.3 vs 14.9; processing time 35.0 vs 42.6 s), all p < .001. — [Roßmann 2018](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/6/3/376/4562352)
- DeBell, Wilson, Jackman & Figueroa (2021), "Optimal Response Formats for Online Surveys: Branch, Grid, or Single Item?" *JSSAM* 9(1):1–24, doi:10.1093/jssam/smz039. KnowledgePanel 2011, N = 1,209 completes, 95.5% desktop/laptop. Grids (2–6 items per page) were faster than single-item pages in 7 of 8 sections: racial resentment, 4 items, median 45 vs 52 s; emotions about country, 6 items, 30 vs 59 s. Taking branching as 1.0, single-item time ranged 0.6–1.0 and grid 0.4–0.8. — [OUP full text](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/9/1/1/5678715)
- Lozar Manfreda, Batagelj & Vehovar (2002), "Design of Web Survey Questionnaires: Three Basic Experiments," *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication* 7(3), doi:10.1111/j.1083-6101.2002.tb00149.x. RIS 1996 survey, one scrolling page (n = 644) vs one question per page (n = 672). **Multiple pages took 466 s vs 368 s, about 30% longer (p < .0005).** — [JCMC](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/7/3/JCMC731/4584218)
- Bell 2001: matrix 5.07 min vs expanded (stacked) 5.22 min. — [PMC130072 abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC130072)
- Nosek 2012 Exp 1: all-at-once 149 s vs one-at-a-time 154 s (d = .05). In Exp 2, where all surveys in the session had the same format, one-at-a-time was *faster* (study time 866 vs 912 s with the MFQ). The one-at-a-time interface auto-advanced on a confirming click. — [Nosek 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3355147/)
- Vehovar 2023: grids were significantly faster than paging but **not** faster than scrolling or unfolding. Overall median was 29.1 min for paging vs 19.6 min for the non-paging layouts. — [Vehovar 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)
- Liu & Cernat 2018: response time was "similar between the two question types across all response lengths." — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439316674459)
- Mavletova 2018 (wave 1 slides): grid about 18.8 min vs item-by-item (scrolling) about 20.9 min. All format and device differences were significant (t = 4.48, p < .001). — [ESRA slides](https://hse.ru/data/2017/10/15/1159283029/Mavletova%20et%20al_Grids_item-by-item_ESRA_2017.pdf)
- Neuert 2023: the trend was large grid fastest and item-by-item slowest, not significant (lab, small n). — [JOS](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/jos-2023-0004.pdf)

**Straightlining / nondifferentiation**
- Roßmann 2018: straightlining **Exp 1 grid 16% vs single 10% (p < .001); Exp 2 10% vs 4% (p = .003)**. Pd was .56 vs .61 and .64 vs .69. Nonsubstantive ("don't know") answers were **30% vs 19%** and **6% vs 1%**. The grid effects were concentrated among low-ability respondents, with no format difference among high-ability respondents. — [Roßmann 2018](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/6/3/376/4562352)
- Tourangeau 2004 Exp 6: nondifferentiation .436 (one grid) vs .422 (two grids) vs .412 (separate screens), significant (via Callegaro). — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- DeBell 2021: straightlining **grid 2.6% vs single-item 1.8%** in the grid-vs-SI comparison (logit difference 0.35, 95% CI −0.00 to 0.71, p < .10). In the three-way comparison it was grid 4.1%, SI 2.4%, branch 3.0%. — [DeBell 2021](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/9/1/1/5678715)
- Mavletova 2018: straightlining was higher in grids, with negative-binomial OR **1.34 (wave 1) and 1.38 (wave 2)** and no format×device interaction. — [ESRA slides](https://hse.ru/data/2017/10/15/1159283029/Mavletova%20et%20al_Grids_item-by-item_ESRA_2017.pdf); [abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317735307)
- Vehovar 2023: on PC, the grid straightlining index was **.460 vs a five-layout average of .388 (19% worse)**, and 25% worse on smartphones. Paging (one per page) had the least straightlining, 30% better than average on PC. — [Vehovar 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)
- **Null results:** Liu & Cernat 2018 found straightlining "similar between the two question types across all response lengths" (2–11 options). — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439316674459). Neuert 2023 found no Pd or CV differences. — [JOS](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/jos-2023-0004.pdf). MeasuringU's SUS studies found 19% vs 13% and 8% vs 11%, both n.s. — [MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/grids-responses/). Stefkovics & Kmetty 2022 found "little evidence of stronger satisficing on grids." — [MISS 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z)

**Item nonresponse**
- Toepoel: share of respondents with ≥1 missing item was **.10 (1 per screen), .13 (4), .15 (10), .19 (40)**, F = 7.35, p < .01. Mean missing items were .14, .17, .22 and .25 (F = 2.92, p = .03). The pattern replicated at T2. — [Toepoel 2005 WP](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download)
- Nosek 2012: Exp 1 (17 items) averaged **2.23 missing items all-at-once vs 1.09 one-at-a-time** (d = .17). Exp 2 had **13.8% vs 7.2%** of data missing without the MFQ and **14.2% vs 7.0%** with it (d ≈ .26–.27). Skipping the whole MFQ: 12.4% vs 5.0%. — [Nosek 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3355147/)
- Lozar Manfreda 2002: the one-page scrolling design had higher item nonresponse, e.g. grid questions 40.3% vs 35.0% (p = .013) and 40.2% vs 35.3% (p = .021). — [JCMC](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/7/3/JCMC731/4584218)
- Liu & Cernat 2018: "item nonresponse tends to be higher for matrix than item-by-item question, especially among mobile respondents." — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439316674459)
- Couper, Tourangeau, Conrad & Zhang (2013), "The Design of Grids in Web Surveys," *SSCR* 31(3):322–345, doi:10.1177/0894439312469865. Dynamic feedback in grids reduced missing data, and "splitting the grids into component questions further reduces missing data and motivated underreporting"; visual complexity had little effect. — [Crossref abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439312469865)
- DeBell 2021: item nonresponse averaged SI 0.5%, grid 0.8%, branch 1.0%, with no significant difference on matched items (Grid−SI 0.3 pp, s.e. 0.24). — [DeBell 2021](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/9/1/1/5678715)
- Callegaro 2009: item nonresponse was "extremely low" in every group, so it could not be tested. — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- Vehovar 2023: paging had 48% less item nonresponse than average on PC, "similar to scrolling and unfolding." Horizontal-scrolling widgets had 90% more nonresponse than average on PC. — [Vehovar 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)
- Paper (not web), Iglesias, Birks & Torgerson (2001), *QJM* 94:695–698: SF-12 for women aged 70+ (n = 422). Item missingness was **26.6% in grids vs 8.5%** with one question at a time (via Callegaro 2009). — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf). *Warning:* a search-engine summary wrongly attributed these 26.6%/8.5% numbers to Couper et al. 2013. They belong to Iglesias 2001.

**Breakoff**
- Lozar Manfreda 2002: 14.6% (one page) vs 16.5% (multi-page), p = .36. — [JCMC](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/7/3/JCMC731/4584218)
- Peytchev, Couper, McCabe & Crawford (2006), "Web Survey Design: Paging versus Scrolling," *POQ* 70(4):596–607, doi:10.1093/poq/nfl028. More than 21,000 undergraduates were sampled, about 10,000 responded, and 10% got the scrolling version. The survey had up to 268 questions in 5 sections with skip patterns. — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/70/4/596/1878498). Secondary accounts: "no differences in participation rates and breakoff rates between the two designs"; scrolling "increases the likelihood of inadvertently missing an item"; and with complex skip logic, "a paging design with automatic navigation takes less time." — [Mavletova & Couper 2014, JSSAM 2:498–518](https://publications.hse.ru/mirror/pubs/share/folder/nssh3n1258/direct/164995078)
- Toepoel: too few incomplete cases to analyse (16 of 2,565 at T1). — [Toepoel 2005 WP](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download)
- Nosek 2012: attrition was the same: 86.1% vs 85.6% finished (Exp 1), and 67.8% vs 68.3% with the MFQ (Exp 2, p = .48). — [Nosek 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3355147/)
- DeBell 2021: 57 of 1,266 broke off (4%), with no format difference. — [DeBell 2021](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/9/1/1/5678715)
- Mavletova 2018: wave 1 breakoff was grid 12.4% vs item-by-item 12.5%, no difference (PC 10.0% vs smartphone 15.2%). — [ESRA slides](https://hse.ru/data/2017/10/15/1159283029/Mavletova%20et%20al_Grids_item-by-item_ESRA_2017.pdf)
- Grady 2019 (Study 1 N = 2,492; Study 2 N = 2,570): "dropout rate and reported survey difficulty increased as row size increased" (5 vs 10 vs 20 rows per page). Seven columns increased completion time, and 5×5 was best overall. — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439318773733)
- Vehovar 2023: grids showed "no disadvantages for breakoffs." Paging was worse on breakoffs (+9% PC, +25% SP vs average), driven by its longer duration. — [Vehovar 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)

**Respondent evaluation / burden**
- Toepoel: layout rating (1–10) was **7.67 (1 per screen), 7.57 (4), 7.37 (10), 7.06 (40)** (F = 17.13, p < .001). More items per screen also lowered ratings of duration and ease. — [Toepoel 2005 WP](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download)
- Thorndike et al. (2009), "Web-based measurement: Effect of completing single or multiple items per webpage," *Computers in Human Behavior* 25(2):393–401, doi:10.1016/j.chb.2008.05.006. 710 Swedish internet self-help seekers (depression, social phobia, panic) completed BDI, BAI, QOLI and MADRS twice (1–4 h apart, counterbalanced): one questionnaire per page vs one item per page. "The majority of participants in each diagnostic group preferred the single item per page format, even though this format required more time." — [abstract via search](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/111/paper-detail/72164); design details via [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- Mavletova 2018: grids "increased reported technical difficulties and decreased subjective evaluation." — [abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317735307)
- Callegaro 2009: no difference in difficulty or enjoyment. — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)

### Inferences
- Grids' speed advantage comes mostly from fewer page turns. Against a stacked page with no extra clicks, the time saving largely disappears (Vehovar, Bell). Some of the time saved may also be shallower processing: Neuert's eye-tracking showed less fixation on scale labels in grids, and Roßmann's effects were concentrated in low-ability respondents.
- Item nonresponse is mainly a function of **how many items share a page**, grid or not, when answers are not required. Toepoel's per-item-header conditions were folded into the same format groups, and Nosek's all-at-once page used per-item dropdowns, yet both showed the gradient. One-per-page designs that force an explicit "decline" (Nosek) or show one item at a time almost eliminate accidental skips. With hard or soft reminders, the gap mostly closes (Vehovar used soft reminders).
- Breakoff is mostly insensitive to format on PCs. The exceptions are very tall grids (20 rows) and total survey length, which hurts paging in long surveys.

### Gaps
- No PC study found reports straightlining for format (b) vs (a) directly with a large probability sample, apart from Vehovar 2023 (paging beat scrolling on straightlining, magnitudes given as % deviation from average).
- Peytchev 2006 primary numbers (item nonresponse, timings by section) could not be read; only secondary characterizations are available.
- Grady 2019 and Liu & Cernat 2018 effect sizes (e.g., dropout % by row count) are available only at abstract level. MeasuringU gives Liu & Cernat's N as 5,644 SurveyMonkey panelists, but MeasuringU's summary also misstates Liu & Cernat's straightlining result as "higher straightlining in grids," which contradicts the abstract. Treat MeasuringU details as unreliable unless corroborated.

## Q3. What did the named papers find? (one-line study cards, plus reviews and meta-analyses)

### Takeaway
The named studies are summarized below with design, N and headline effects. No formal meta-analysis of grid vs. item-by-item effects on PC data quality was found. The closest syntheses are Callegaro et al. 2009's literature table, MeasuringU's 2019 vote-count table, the literature reviews in Vehovar et al. 2023 and Neuert et al. 2023, and the book chapters (Couper 2008; Callegaro, Lozar Manfreda & Vehovar 2015; Tourangeau, Conrad & Couper 2013), which I could not access. "DeBell et al. 2021 on grids" is a primary experiment, not a meta-analysis.

### Cited Findings
- **Toepoel, Das & van Soest (2009)** *Field Methods* 21(2):200–213. CentERpanel (Dutch probability panel), N = 2,565, 40 ASTS items, 5-point scale fully labelled, at 1, 4, 10 or 40 items per screen. The multi-item formats were run in two variants: one header at the top, or a header repeated at every item. The study was repeated six months later (N = 2,350; 2,027 did both). Results: no format effect on the arousal index mean (T1 117.95) or on variances; alpha .879–.887; respondents with any missing item rose from 10% to 19%; duration was lowest at 10 per screen; layout rating fell from 7.67 to 7.06. — [WP](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download); [published abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/1525822X08330261)
- **Peytchev, Couper, McCabe & Crawford (2006)** *POQ* 70(4):596–607. About 10,000 undergraduate respondents (10% scrolling, 90% paging), up to 268 questions with skips. Secondary accounts report no difference in breakoff, more item nonresponse when scrolling, and paging faster where skips are complex. — [abstract](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/70/4/596/1878498); [Mavletova & Couper 2014](https://publications.hse.ru/mirror/pubs/share/folder/nssh3n1258/direct/164995078)
- **Callegaro, Shand-Lubbers & Dennis (2009)** AAPOR/JSM proceedings. KnowledgePanel, 1,419 analysed, five arms (standard grid, shaded grid, single-item horizontal, single-item vertical, single-item vertical shaded) on SF-36v2 Vitality (4 items) and Mental Health (5 items). Alpha was slightly higher for single-item (n.s.), times were 44–46 s vs 69–72 s, item nonresponse was negligible, and difficulty and enjoyment did not differ. — [PDF](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- **Liu & Cernat (2018)** *SSCR* 36(6):690–706. Web experiment crossing matrix vs. item-by-item with 2/3/4/5/7/9/11 response options, plus device analysis. Straightlining and time were similar; item nonresponse was higher in the matrix, especially on mobile; the formats were measurement-equivalent below 7 options and diverged at 9–11. — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439316674459)
- **Roßmann, Gummer & Silber (2018)** *JSSAM* 6(3):376–400. Two experiments, N = 2,503 (opt-in) and N = 529 (probability), 5- and 7-item sets on 11-point scales, grid vs. one item per page. The grid produced more straightlining (16 vs 10%; 10 vs 4%), more "don't know" answers (30 vs 19%; 6 vs 1%), shorter time, and higher alpha (.68 vs .64; .60 vs .41), with effects concentrated among low-ability respondents. — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/6/3/376/4562352)
- **Grady, Greenspan & Liu (2019)** *SSCR* 37(3):435–445. N = 2,492 and 2,570. More rows per page meant more dropout and difficulty; 7 columns meant longer completion; 3 columns meant lower reliability; 5×5 was best. Grids were converted to item-by-item on phones. — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439318773733)
- **Bell, Mangione & Kahn (2001)** *JAMIA* 8(6):616–620. SF-36 shown to website volunteers as a matrix vs. an "expanded" format; 1,615 of 4,208 visitors consented and completed. Times were 5.07 vs 5.22 min; scores and alphas were "not substantially influenced." — [PMC130072](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC130072). *Conflict:* Callegaro 2009 reports "4,876 consenting visitors" randomized. MeasuringU repeats 4,876 and calls the comparison "grid vs. one-at-a-time," whereas Callegaro says the non-grid arm was "multiple items per screen with response options laid out vertically." Prefer the JAMIA abstract figures and Callegaro's design description.
- **Thorndike et al. (2009)** *CHB* 25(2):393–401. 710 Swedish help-seekers, within-person crossover (each questionnaire on one page vs one item per page). Factor structure and factor means were equivalent across format and time; most participants preferred one item per page despite the longer time. — [abstract](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/111/paper-detail/72164). MeasuringU's summary calling this "grid vs one-at-a-time" for "quality-of-life questions" misdescribes it. The multi-item format was a whole questionnaire per page, and BDI-type items each carry unique response options, so this is format (b).
- **DeBell, Wilson, Jackman & Figueroa (2021)** *JSSAM* 9(1):1–24. Covered in Q2 and Q4. N = 1,209, probability panel, 95.5% PC. Grid was fastest; SI had the least item nonresponse (n.s.); straightlining was 2.6% vs 1.8% (p < .10); criterion validity of grid and SI was "effectively the same" (SI−Grid +0.0014, p = .59); branching was worst. — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/9/1/1/5678715). *Note:* the extraction gave the response rate as about 2% (AAPOR RR3 cumulative for the panel). I did not verify this.
- **Mavletova, Couper & Lebedev (2018)** *SSCR* 36(6):647–668. Crossover of device × format, Russian opt-in panel, N = 1,678/1,079, 49 items in 7 sets, answers required. Grid had more straightlining, lower concurrent validity and worse burden ratings; breakoff and test–retest did not differ. Here "item-by-item" means stacked on one scrolling page. — [abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317735307); [slides](https://hse.ru/data/2017/10/15/1159283029/Mavletova%20et%20al_Grids_item-by-item_ESRA_2017.pdf)
- **Couper, Tourangeau, Conrad & Zhang (2013)** *SSCR* 31(3):322–345. Dynamic feedback and splitting grids into component questions reduced missing data; visual complexity had little effect. — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439312469865)
- **Revilla, Toninelli & Ochoa (2017)** *Telematics and Informatics* 34(1):30–42, doi:10.1016/j.tele.2016.04.002. Grids vs. item-by-item on PC and smartphones. Mavletova's slides summarize it as similar alpha, with grids taking longer than item-by-item among mobile respondents. — [slides](https://hse.ru/data/2017/10/15/1159283029/Mavletova%20et%20al_Grids_item-by-item_ESRA_2017.pdf). Primary not read.
- **Revilla & Couper (2018)** *SSCR* 36(3):349–368, doi:10.1177/0894439317715626. n = 1,476, seven groups, Netquest Spain: grids vs. vertical and horizontal item-by-item on PC and smartphone. "The most striking difference" was the smartphone "next"-button placement, not grid vs. item-by-item. — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317715626)
- **Vehovar, Couper & Čehovin (2023)** *SSCR* 41(6):2122–2144. n = 4,644 (54% PC), Slovenian panel, five randomized layouts across 15 grids and 158 items in a 20-minute survey. Grids underperformed the stacked layouts on item nonresponse, straightlining and outliers on PC, without a time advantage over stacked layouts. The authors recommend replacing grids "not only on mobile devices but also on PCs." — [SSCR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)
- **Neuert, Roßmann & Silber (2023)** *JOS* 39(1):79–101. Eye-tracking (see Q1). — [JOS](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/jos-2023-0004.pdf)
- **Nosek, Sriram & Umansky (2012)** *PLoS ONE* 7(5):e36771. Covered in Q1, Q2 and Q5. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3355147/)
- **GSS Methodological Report #141** (Sparkman, Wells, Norling-Ruggles, Schapiro & Bautista, NORC, Sept 2024). GSS 2021/2022 web cases, grids of 3–4 Yes/No items vs. sequential screens. Two items were significant (ABANY 60.1% vs 54.7% "yes" in 2021; ABRAPE 92.1% vs 86.2% in 2022), and **neither survived multiple-comparison correction**. "These simplified grids make little difference in terms of measurement." — [NORC PDF](https://gss.norc.org/content/dam/gss/get-documentation/pdf/reports/methodological-reports/GSS%20MR141%20Grid%20And%20Volunteered%20Response%20Experiments.pdf)
- **Stefkovics & Kmetty (2022)** "A comparison of question order effects on item-by-item and grid formats: visual layout matters," *Measurement Instruments for the Social Sciences* 4:9, doi:10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z. Hungarian opt-in panel, 2019, ESS welfare items. A priming question shifted attitudes "only when questions were presented on separate pages"; "the grid format was robust against question order." — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z)

**Reviews and vote-counts (not meta-analyses)**
- Callegaro 2009 §2.2 summary: "in most cases placing multiple items per screen increases inter-item correlations. Grids seem to increase item nonresponse ... respondents view items on grid as belonging together but that does not mean that the answers are more valid." — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- MeasuringU (Sauro, Feb 2019) vote-count across about 11 prior studies plus its own. More straightlining: grid 4, alone 1. Higher reliability or variance explained: grid 4, alone 4, no difference 1. Higher nonresponse: grid 6. Longer time: alone 5, no difference 2. Preference: grid 4, alone 2. This is an industry blog with **some misdescribed studies** (see above), so use it with care. — [MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/grids-responses/)
- Neuert 2023 literature summary: grids have been linked to more missing or nonsubstantive answers (Mavletova & Couper 2015; Roßmann 2018; Toepoel 2009), more straightlining (DeBell 2021; Roßmann 2018; Tourangeau 2004) and more breakoff (Couper 2013; Liu & Cernat 2018; Tourangeau 2004). The authors also note that less differentiated grid answers could sometimes be "closer to 'truth'." — [JOS](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/jos-2023-0004.pdf)

### Inferences
- The evidence base is about 15 randomized PC or mixed-device experiments. Most are on opt-in panels or students; the probability-based ones are Toepoel/CentERpanel, Callegaro and DeBell/KnowledgePanel, GSS, and Roßmann Exp 2. Results agree on time (grid faster than paging) and roughly agree on item nonresponse and straightlining (grid worse). They disagree on alpha.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis was found. The "DeBell et al. 2021 grid review" named in the brief appears to be the DeBell, Wilson, Jackman & Figueroa primary experiment. A 2015–2025 systematic review is not evident. Antoun et al. (2018, SSCR 36(5):557–574) is a systematic review of *smartphone* design heuristics, outside PC scope. Čehovin & Berzelak (2020) is cited by Vehovar as a review of grid layouts but was not retrieved.
- The book chapters (Couper 2008, ch. 4; Callegaro, Lozar Manfreda & Vehovar 2015; Tourangeau, Conrad & Couper 2013) were not accessed.

## Q4. Do formats differ in substantive means or response distributions?

### Takeaway
Mostly no. Mean scale scores are generally equivalent across 1/4/10/40 items per screen and across grid vs. separate formats, with occasional item-level shifts of about 2–7 points on 0–100 scales or 5–6 pp on proportions. Isolated differences rarely survive multiple-comparison correction. Distributional differences show up mainly as more extreme or midpoint choices in grids, and as context or order effects that differ by layout.

### Cited Findings
- Toepoel: "no statistically significant difference in the mean scores" of the arousal index across 4 formats, and no difference in item variances. Formats also showed "the same deviation of item scores between repeated experiments" (test–retest, 6 months). — [Toepoel 2005 WP](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download)
- Thorndike 2009: "factor means for all questionnaires were equivalent across presentation method and time." — [abstract](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/111/paper-detail/72164)
- Bell 2001: health status scores were not substantially influenced. — [PMC130072](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC130072)
- DeBell 2021: midpoint ("moderate") responses were somewhat more common in single-item than grid format (logit 0.18, 95% CI 0.07–0.31). Extreme responses did not differ between SI and grid (0.01, CI −0.12 to 0.13). Branching differed strongly from both. — [DeBell 2021](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/9/1/1/5678715)
- Vehovar 2023: across 20 estimates × 2 devices, scrolling (stacked) gave 0 significant deviations from the five-layout average, unfolding 1, grid 2, horizontal scrolling 4 and paging 5. The grid had 1 relative difference >5% on PC data. — [Vehovar 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644); [ESRA slides](https://www.europeansurveyresearch.org/conf2023/uploads/320/2081/4/ESRA2023_cehovin_final.pdf)
- GSS 2021/22 web: 2 of 11 items differed (grid +5.4 pp and +5.9 pp "yes"), not significant after correction. — [NORC MR141](https://gss.norc.org/content/dam/gss/get-documentation/pdf/reports/methodological-reports/GSS%20MR141%20Grid%20And%20Volunteered%20Response%20Experiments.pdf)
- Stefkovics & Kmetty 2022: a question-order (priming) effect appeared only in item-by-item paging, not in grids. Layout therefore changes the *context sensitivity* of answers even when it leaves the means alone. — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z)
- Garland 2009 (industry): "the distribution of answers significantly changed across conditions" (grid / multiple per screen / single). — via [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- MeasuringU 2019 (industry, n = 319–612). SUS means were about 1 point lower in grids for software products, and 72.1 (separate) vs 67.4 (grid) for Facebook. NPS-type brand ratings were 0.38 points (3%) higher on separate pages, with only 1 of 10 brands significant. — [MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/grids-responses/)
- Roßmann 2018: grids increased nonsubstantive ("don't know") answers (30% vs 19%; 6% vs 1%), which changes the effective analytic sample. — [Roßmann 2018](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/6/3/376/4562352)
- Vehovar 2023: grids underperformed on extreme responses on PC. — [Vehovar 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)

### Inferences
- For multi-item scale scores, format effects on means are small relative to typical between-group effects. The larger practical risks are (i) differential missingness or "don't know" answers and (ii) mixing formats across waves, arms or devices, which several authors warn can introduce non-equivalence (Stefkovics 2022; Liu & Cernat at 9+ options; Mavletova on the 7-point set).

### Gaps
- Few studies test means with pre-specified equivalence margins. Most rely on non-significance.

## Q5. Evidence on multiple items per page where EACH item keeps its own labelled response options (no shared header), vs. grids and vs. item-by-item

### Takeaway
Some direct evidence exists, and it is fairly favourable to this format on PCs. Compared with grids, stacked items with repeated options generally match or beat the grid on data quality: less straightlining and item nonresponse, no loss of consistency or validity, and no meaningful time penalty (Vehovar 2023; Mavletova 2018; Bell 2001; Toepoel's per-item-header arms). Compared with one item per page, stacked pages are faster and less burdensome. They carry somewhat more item nonresponse when answers are optional (Toepoel; Nosek; Lozar Manfreda; Vehovar 2022 follow-up) and slightly more straightlining (Vehovar 2023: paging best). Means are equivalent (Thorndike; Vehovar).

### Cited Findings
- **Vehovar, Couper & Čehovin (2023)** is the most direct test. The "scrolling" layout "displays items sequentially on the same page," with response categories "repeated for each item," and was randomized against grid, unfolding/accordion, horizontal-scrolling and paging (one item per page) layouts. n = 4,644, 54% on PC. Scrolling "behaved very similarly" to unfolding, which "did not underperform in any RQI." Scrolling had only slight, non-significant lags vs unfolding in item nonresponse and attention checks, and it was the best layout for survey estimates (0 significant deviations in 40 cells). Grids were not faster than scrolling or unfolding. Paging beat scrolling on straightlining but took about 50% longer (29.1 vs 19.6 min), with more breakoffs and burden. The authors conclude "Grids can be safely replaced by scrolling or unfolding" on PCs and smartphones. — [SSCR 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644); [ESRA 2023 slides](https://www.europeansurveyresearch.org/conf2023/uploads/320/2081/4/ESRA2023_cehovin_final.pdf)
  - Their 2022 follow-up (n = 1,546; 37% PC; 40% of items from grids) compared paging vs. scrolling. Paging took about 10% longer with more breakoffs and burden (n.s.), and scrolling had **higher item nonresponse (p < .001)**. — [ESRA 2023 slides](https://www.europeansurveyresearch.org/conf2023/uploads/320/2081/4/ESRA2023_cehovin_final.pdf) (slides only; not peer-reviewed)
- **Mavletova, Couper & Lebedev (2018)**: the item-by-item arm was a *scrolling* format (items stacked with their own options). Their research question was "What is the effect of an item-by-item scrolling format relative to grids for both PC and smartphone respondents?" On PC as well as mobile, the grid had more straightlining (OR about 1.34–1.38), lower concurrent validity and worse burden ratings. Breakoff and test–retest were equal, and the grid was about 2 minutes faster over 111 items. Answers were obligatory. — [ESRA 2017 slides](https://hse.ru/data/2017/10/15/1159283029/Mavletova%20et%20al_Grids_item-by-item_ESRA_2017.pdf); [abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317735307)
- **Bell, Mangione & Kahn (2001)**: SF-36 "expanded" format (multiple items per screen, each with vertically laid-out options) vs. matrix. Alpha and scores were essentially unchanged, and times were about equal (5.22 vs 5.07 min). — [PMC130072](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC130072); [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- **Toepoel et al. (2005/2009)**: at 4, 10 and 40 items per screen, half the respondents saw the response header repeated at **every item** (n = 374, 398, 359) and half saw one header at the top (n = 353, 370, 359). The authors pooled the variants because "preliminary analyses show no differences in the visibility of the header." Where scrolling was needed, per-item headers reduced completion time, but not significantly. So in this study repeating labels on each item vs. a shared header made little difference; the number of items per screen drove nonresponse and evaluations. — [Toepoel 2005 WP](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download)
- **Yan (2005)**: arm 2 was "a single screen but separated (not in a grid) in a scrolling design," between one-per-screen and grid. Alpha increased from single-item to grid, marginally significant. Callegaro does not report where the separated-scrolling arm fell. — [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- **Nosek et al. (2012)**: the all-at-once page showed every item with its own drop-down list, not a grid. Against one-at-a-time it had about twice the missing data (13.8% vs 7.2%), more people skipping whole questionnaires (MFQ 12.4% vs 5.0%), equal attrition, slightly lower alpha in Exp 2 (.679 vs .705), essentially equal criterion validity, and was slower in Exp 2. *Confounds:* the one-at-a-time interface required an explicit "Decline to answer" click to skip, and drop-downs differ from radio buttons. — [Nosek 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3355147/)
- **Thorndike et al. (2009)**: a whole clinical questionnaire per page (BDI, BAI, QOLI, MADRS, where items carry their own option sets) vs one item per page. Measurement was equivalent and most respondents preferred one per page. — [abstract](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/111/paper-detail/72164)
- **Lozar Manfreda et al. (2002)**: a single scrolling page (all questions, mixed types) vs one question per page. The one page was about 30% faster (368 vs 466 s), with equal breakoff (14.6% vs 16.5%) and more item nonresponse. — [JCMC](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/7/3/JCMC731/4584218)
- **Garland (2009)** (industry) included a "multiple items per screen" non-grid arm. The single-item arm outperformed both the grid and the multiple-items arm on factor recovery and alpha. Satisfaction did not differ. — via [Callegaro 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)
- **Revilla & Couper (2018)**: grids vs vertical and horizontal item-by-item on PC and smartphone (n = 1,476). The main reported difference was smartphone next-button placement, implying small format differences on PC. — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317715626)
- **Neuert 2023 (mechanism)**: with items co-present on a page (grids of 5 or 10), 16–29% of respondents revised an earlier answer after reading later items. This "look-around" behaviour presumably also applies to stacked non-grid pages. — [JOS](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/jos-2023-0004.pdf)

### Inferences
- Taken together, the stacked/scrolling format behaves like the grid on speed and like item-by-item on straightlining and validity. The two PC/mixed-device studies that test it head-to-head against a grid (Vehovar 2023; Mavletova 2018), plus Bell 2001, favour stacked over grid or find parity.
- Against one item per page, stacked pages trade a modest rise in accidental item nonresponse, and slightly more nondifferentiation per Vehovar, for large savings in time, breakoff risk in long surveys, and burden ratings. Requiring or prompting answers (Mavletova used obligatory answers; Vehovar soft reminders) and keeping pages short (about 5 items, by analogy with Grady's row findings and Toepoel's gradient) are the levers the literature points to.
- The proximity heuristic is a property of co-presence on the screen, not of the shared header (Toepoel's header manipulation had no effect; Neuert's answer-revision data). Stacked pages should therefore still show some inter-item assimilation relative to one per page, probably less than grids. Toepoel's per-item-header arm and Yan's separated arm are the only data points, and neither reports a separate estimate.

### Gaps
- No study isolates "shared header vs. repeated labels" with everything else held constant and reports inter-item correlations separately. Toepoel ran the manipulation but pooled the arms after finding no differences, and the per-arm numbers were not published.
- Yan (2005) and Garland (2009) results for the non-grid multi-item arm are not available in detail. One is an unpublished dissertation, the other an industry release.
- Vehovar 2023's per-cell PC values for scrolling vs paging (e.g., exact straightlining indices) are in an online supplement I did not retrieve. Only signs and relative-difference percentages are given here.
