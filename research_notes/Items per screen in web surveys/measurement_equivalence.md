# Measurement equivalence and psychometric consequences of questionnaire presentation format (items per screen, grids, item order, paper vs computer)

Scope note: these notes cover (1) tests of factor structure, invariance, reliability and score comparability across presentation formats and administration modes; (2) context, proximity and item-order effects that format can create or amplify, and how randomization interacts with them; (3) authoritative guidance on standardizing and documenting administration format; (4) evidence on mixing formats inside one study. Verification level is marked where it matters: "[abstract]" = verified against the published abstract (via OpenAlex/Europe PMC/Semantic Scholar metadata); "[full text]" = verified against the full text; "[search summary]" = only seen in a search-engine summary or secondary citation, not checked against the source itself.

## 1. Are there studies testing configural/metric/scalar invariance of a multi-item scale between item-by-item and multiple-items-per-screen (or grid) presentations? What did they find?

### Takeaway
Yes, but there are few of them and almost all come from survey methodology (attitude batteries on web panels), not psychometric scale validation. Clinical-scale studies (Thorndike et al. 2009, Turner-Bowker et al. 2013) find the same constructs and equivalent means between one-item-per-page and all-items-on-a-page layouts. The survey-methods experiments find equivalence for short response scales (fewer than 7 points) and *non*-equivalence for longer scales (9 or 11 points), longer batteries, and smartphones. Grids reliably raise inter-item correlations: a format artifact that inflates alpha and factor loadings without any change in the construct.

### Cited Findings

**Direct invariance and equivalence tests: grid or multi-item page vs item-by-item**
- Liu, M., & Cernat, A. (2018). Item-by-item versus matrix questions: A web survey experiment. *Social Science Computer Review, 36*(6), 690–706. https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439316674459 [abstract]. This web experiment crossed matrix vs item-by-item format with 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9 and 11 response options, plus device. "Measurement models reveal measurement equivalence between the two question types when there are fewer than seven response options. For matrices with 9 or 11 response options, analyses reveal substantial differences compared to item-by-item questions." Straightlining and response time were similar across formats. Item nonresponse was higher for the matrix, especially on mobile. — [Liu & Cernat 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439316674459)
- Mavletova, A., Couper, M. P., & Lebedev, D. (2018). Grid and item-by-item formats in PC and mobile web surveys. *Social Science Computer Review, 36*(6), 647–668. https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317735307 [abstract]. This was a two-wave experiment in a Russian opt-in panel (N = 1,678 in wave 1, 1,079 in wave 2) crossing format with device. It found "somewhat higher measurement error in the grid format in both mobile and PC web conditions". There was "almost no significant effect of the question format on test–retest correlations between the latent scores". The multigroup comparison "showed some measurement equivalence between the question formats. However … a longer scale [produced] some differences in the measurement equivalence." Straightlining was higher in grids, and "concurrent validity was lower in the grid format in both PC and mobile web conditions." — [Mavletova et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317735307)
- Thorndike, F. P., Carlbring, P., Smyth, F. L., Magee, J. C., Gonder-Frederick, L., Öst, L.-G., & Ritterband, L. M. (2009). Web-based measurement: Effect of completing single or multiple items per webpage. *Computers in Human Behavior, 25*(2), 393–401. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2008.05.006 [search summary; the abstract text could not be retrieved directly]. N = 710 internet self-help seekers (depression, social phobia, panic) completed the BDI, BAI, QOLI and MADRS-S twice, once with all items on one page and once with one item per page, in counterbalanced order. Reported result: the questionnaires "measure the same construct across … presentation type (single versus multiple items per page), and order of presentation", and "factor means for all questionnaires were equivalent across presentation method and time." Most participants preferred one item per page even though it took longer. This is the closest match to a clinical-scale invariance test of items per page. — [Thorndike et al. 2009 (search summary via datalearner index)](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/111/paper-detail/72164)
- Turner-Bowker, D. M., Saris-Baglama, R. N., & DeRosa, M. A. (2013). Single-item electronic administration of the SF-36v2 Health Survey. *Quality of Life Research, 22*(3), 485–490. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-012-0169-8 [abstract]. Headache patients (N = 180) completed the SF-36v2 one item per page instead of the paper grid. Internal consistency ranged from 0.81 to 0.95 and principal components "confirmed the 2-factor structure", so the single-item version was judged reliable and valid. **Caveat:** this is a single-format study compared against published norms and structure. It is not a randomized format comparison or a formal invariance test. — [Turner-Bowker et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-012-0169-8)
- Smith, K. J., & Kosinski, M. (2011). Psychometric comparability of single item and grid form administration of the SF-36v2 Health Survey (Abstract PRM25). *Value in Health, 14*(3), A150. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2011.02.833. This is a conference abstract on the same question. I could not retrieve its results. — [Smith & Kosinski 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2011.02.833)
- Callegaro, M., Shand-Lubbers, J., & Dennis, J. M. (2009). Presentation of a single item versus a grid: Effects on the Vitality and Mental Health scales of the SF-36v2 Health Survey (conference paper, AAPOR). This was a KnowledgePanel experiment (N = 2,500) randomized to one of five layouts. The abstract frames grids as posing "higher item non-response, higher item non-differentiation, and some-times higher measurement error", but the retrievable abstract is truncated before the results. — [Callegaro et al. 2009 (ResearchGate listing)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/253454379_Presentation_of_a_Single_Item_versus_a_Grid_Effects_on_the_Vitality_and_Mental_Health_Scales_of_the_SF36v2_Health_Survey)
- Toepoel, V., Das, M., & van Soest, A. (2009). Design of web questionnaires: The effects of the number of items per screen. *Field Methods, 21*(2), 200–213. https://doi.org/10.1177/1525822X08330261 [abstract]. A 40-item arousal measure was shown at 1, 4, 10 or 40 items per screen. The authors "find no effect of format on the arousal index". Nonresponse rose with more items per screen. More items per screen shortened completion time but worsened respondents' evaluation of the layout. — [Toepoel et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/1525822X08330261)
- Revilla, M., Toninelli, D., & Ochoa, C. (2017). An experiment comparing grids and item-by-item formats in web surveys completed through PCs and smartphones. *Telematics and Informatics, 34*(1), 30–42. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tele.2016.04.002. The Semantic Scholar auto-summary (not the abstract) reports "similar levels of interitem correlations" across PCs and smartphones (optimized and not), longer grid completion times on smartphones, and sometimes less non-differentiation on PCs. **Conflict:** Stefkovics & Kmetty (2022) list Revilla et al. 2017 among studies that "observed measurement inequivalence between the two formats." I could not reach the full text to resolve this. — [Revilla et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tele.2016.04.002); [Stefkovics & Kmetty 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z)
- Revilla, M., & Couper, M. P. (2018). Comparing grids with vertical and horizontal item-by-item formats for PCs and smartphones. *Social Science Computer Review, 36*(3), 349–368. https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317715626 [abstract]. Seven groups (N = 1,476) were compared on behavior and data-quality outcomes, but not on invariance. The most striking finding was that in smartphone item-by-item conditions with an always-visible "next" button, item missing data were substantially higher. — [Revilla & Couper 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317715626)

**Grids raise inter-item correlations ("near means related")**
- Tourangeau, R., Couper, M. P., & Conrad, F. (2004). Spacing, position, and order: Interpretive heuristics for visual features of survey questions. *Public Opinion Quarterly, 68*(3), 368–393. https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfh035 [abstract]. Experiment 6 "varied whether a battery of related items was administered on a single screen, across two screens, or with each item on its own screen. The intercorrelations among the items were highest when they were all on the same screen." This is the origin of the "near means related" heuristic. — [Tourangeau et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfh035)
- Couper, M. P., Traugott, M. W., & Lamias, M. J. (2001). Web survey design and administration. *Public Opinion Quarterly, 65*(2), 230–253. https://doi.org/10.1086/322199. One of the experiments put several related items on one screen and compared that with one question per screen. The abstract does not state the result. Stefkovics & Kmetty (2022) summarize it as finding "higher intra-item correlations in the grid format with lower completion time." — [Couper et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1086/322199); [secondary summary in Stefkovics & Kmetty 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z)
- Silber, H., Roßmann, J., & Gummer, T. (2018). When near means related: Evidence from three web survey experiments on inter-item correlations in grid questions. *International Journal of Social Research Methodology, 21*(3), 275–288. https://doi.org/10.1080/13645579.2017.1381478 [abstract]. "Inter-item correlations in grid designs were higher for both the set of five and seven items than for questions in a single-item design. Additionally, the inter-item correlations of the set of follow-up questions were higher if the preceding set of questions was presented in a grid." In other words, the format of one block carried over into the correlations of the *next* block. — [Silber et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/13645579.2017.1381478)
- Roßmann, J., Gummer, T., & Silber, H. (2018). Mitigating satisficing in cognitively demanding grid questions: Evidence from two web-based experiments. *Journal of Survey Statistics and Methodology, 6*(3), 376–400. https://doi.org/10.1093/jssam/smx020 [abstract]. Splitting grids into single items increased response times but "significantly reduced the amount of response nondifferentiation and nonsubstantive responses." Question design moderated how much respondent ability and motivation predicted satisficing. — [Roßmann et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/jssam/smx020)
- Couper, M. P., Tourangeau, R., Conrad, F. G., & Zhang, C. (2013). The design of grids in web surveys. *Social Science Computer Review, 31*(3), 322–345. https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439312469865 [abstract]. Splitting grids into component questions reduced missing data and "motivated underreporting". — [Couper et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439312469865)
- Stefkovics & Kmetty (2022) summarize the literature this way: "most of the previous evidence suggests that the two formats do not lend the same results … Respondents … tend to spend less time answering questions and yield more correlated responses in a grid format." They also note that item nonresponse, straightlining and nondifferentiation "is typically higher in grid formats" [full text]. — [Stefkovics & Kmetty 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z)

### Inferences
- For EFA/CFA the main mechanical risk is that grid or multi-item-per-page layouts inflate inter-item correlations (Tourangeau et al. 2004; Silber et al. 2018). That would raise loadings and alpha and could flatten discriminant structure, especially between adjacent subscales in the same grid. A scale developed and validated in one layout and then given in another could show configural invariance but differ in loadings, residual covariances, or both. This is an inference from correlation-level findings. Only Liu & Cernat (2018) and Mavletova et al. (2018) tested this formally, and both found non-equivalence that grew with scale length and response-scale length.
- For short clinical scales with 4- or 5-point response options, the evidence (Thorndike et al. 2009; Liu & Cernat 2018 below 7 options; Toepoel et al. 2009 index means) points to practical equivalence of *means*. The more robust risk is to correlational structure and data quality, not to mean scores.
- Format effects are larger on smartphones (grids do not render, partial response scales are visible), so device mix is a moderator to record.

### Gaps
- I found no study in *Psychological Assessment*, *Assessment*, *Journal of Personality Assessment* or *Educational and Psychological Measurement* that ran a full configural/metric/scalar MGCFA on a multi-item psychological scale comparing one-item-per-screen against several-items-per-screen. Thorndike et al. (2009) is closest, but I could verify its results only via a search summary, not the abstract or full text.
- I could not obtain the specific fit or ΔCFI values from Liu & Cernat (2018) or Mavletova et al. (2018). Publisher pages returned 403.
- The full results of Callegaro et al. (2009) and Smith & Kosinski (2011) on SF-36v2 grid vs single item were not retrievable.

## 2. Paper vs computerized administration: meta-analyses, and what they imply for format changes *within* computer administration (ISPOR "minor" vs "moderate" modifications)

### Takeaway
Paper-to-electronic migration of self-report scales produces near-identical scores when it is done faithfully. Four PRO meta-analyses or reviews and the general-psychology mode studies converge on this. ISPOR's 2009 framework explicitly classes "going from multiple items per page to one item per screen" as a **minor** modification needing only cognitive interviewing and usability testing. Changing item order, splitting an item across screens, or requiring scrolling are classed as **moderate**. ISPOR's 2023 update concludes that further comparability testing is unnecessary when evidence exists and faithful-migration best practice is followed. One review (Muehlhausen et al. 2015) explicitly calls for standardizing "number of items per screen" across vendors.

### Cited Findings

**Meta-analyses: PROs (paper vs electronic)**
- Gwaltney, C. J., Shields, A. L., & Shiffman, S. (2008). Equivalence of electronic and paper-and-pencil administration of patient-reported outcome measures: A meta-analytic review. *Value in Health, 11*(2), 322–333. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1524-4733.2007.00231.x. This review covered 65 studies, with 46 studies and 278 scales usable for quantitative synthesis. The mean difference between modes averaged 0.2% of the scale range, and 93% of 233 comparisons fell within ±5% of the range. The weighted mean cross-mode correlation was 0.90 (207 coefficients, mostly ICCs), and 94% were at least 0.75. Conclusion: "paper- and computer-administered PROs are equivalent." [search summary of the abstract on ISPOR's site] — [ISPOR abstract page](https://www.ispor.org/publications/journals/value-in-health/abstract/Volume-11--Issue-2/Equivalence-of-Electronic-and-Paper-and-Pencil-Administration-of-Patient-Reported-Outcome-Measures--A-Meta-Analytic-Review)
- Muehlhausen, W., Doll, H., Quadri, N., Fordham, B., O'Donohoe, P., Dogar, N., & Wild, D. J. (2015). Equivalence of electronic and paper administration of patient-reported outcome measures: A systematic review and meta-analysis of studies conducted between 2007 and 2013. *Health and Quality of Life Outcomes, 13*, 167. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12955-015-0362-x [full text]. The review extracted 435 correlations, with ICCs ranging from 0.65 to 0.99 and a pooled correlation of 0.88 (95% CI 0.87–0.88; I² = 93.8). The pooled standardized mean difference was 0.037 (95% CI 0.031–0.042; I² = 33.5) across 307 studies. Platform-specific pooled ICC was 0.90 (39 studies). The authors "propose that equivalence studies should not be necessary to demonstrate the equivalence of validity of a measure that has been migrated to an electronic platform following best practices … with minor changes." They also "recommend that common best practices are established among the vendor community … to standardize migration principles (i.e. number of items per screen, scrolling through answer options)." — [Muehlhausen et al. 2015 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4597451/)
- Rutherford, C., Costa, D., Mercieca-Bebber, R., Rice, H., Gabb, L., & King, M. (2016). Mode of administration does not cause bias in patient-reported outcome results: A meta-analysis. *Quality of Life Research, 25*(3), 559–574. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-015-1110-8 [abstract]. Of 5,100 papers screened, 56 were eligible. "No evidence of bias was found for: (1) paper versus electronic self-complete; and (2) self-complete versus assisted MOA." Heterogeneity in the paper-vs-electronic comparison "was explained by type of construct (i.e. physical vs. psychological)". The largest bias arose when clinic-assisted completion was followed by home self-completion. The authors conclude the results "support the use of mixed MOAs within a research study." — [Rutherford et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-015-1110-8)
- Alfonsson, S., Maathz, P., & Hursti, T. (2014). Interformat reliability of digital psychiatric self-report questionnaires: A systematic review. *Journal of Medical Internet Research, 16*(12), e268. https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.3395 [abstract]. The review covered 33 studies and 40 symptom scales. Mean total scores differed significantly between formats in 10 of 62 analyses. Interformat reliability ranged from r = .35 to .99, and "some instruments … consistently show low interformat reliability, suggesting that these conclusions cannot be generalized to all questionnaires." — [Alfonsson et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.3395)

**General psychology: paper vs internet, including invariance tests**
- Weigold, A., Weigold, I. K., & Russell, E. J. (2013). Examination of the equivalence of self-report survey-based paper-and-pencil and internet data collection methods. *Psychological Methods, 18*(1), 53–70. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031607 [abstract]. Two studies of personality, social desirability, computer self-efficacy and personal growth initiative scales assessed quantitative equivalence (means), qualitative equivalence (internal consistency, intercorrelations) and auxiliary equivalence. The methods were "generally equivalent, particularly for quantitative and qualitative equivalence," with nonequivalence only in auxiliary aspects (missing data, completion time, response rate). — [Weigold et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031607)
- Campos, J. A. D. B., Zucoloto, M. L., Bonafé, F. S. S., Jordani, P. C., & Marôco, J. (2011). Reliability and validity of self-reported burnout in college students: A cross randomized comparison of paper-and-pencil vs. online administration. *Computers in Human Behavior, 27*(5), 1875–1883. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2011.04.011 [abstract]. This was a within-subject, counterbalanced comparison of the MBI-SS, OBI and CBI. "The factorial structure was invariant in the three burnout inventories." Reliability was good in both formats, but "concordance between answers was generally higher in the paper-and-pencil format than on the internet." — [Campos et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2011.04.011)
- Vleeschouwer, M., Schubart, C. D., Henquet, C., et al. (2014). Does assessment type matter? A measurement invariance analysis of online and paper and pencil assessment of the Community Assessment of Psychic Experiences (CAPE). *PLoS ONE, 9*(1), e84011 [abstract]. Paper (N = 796) was compared with internet (N = 21,590). Metric invariance was rejected (Δχ²(23) = 152.75, p < .001), but the effect sizes were small (mean SIDS −0.11). A person 2 SD above the mean was expected to score 4.80 points lower online on a 42–114 scale. The authors concluded the difference "can be neglected without major consequ[ences]". This shows that a formal invariance failure and practical equivalence can coexist. — [Vleeschouwer et al. 2014 (PLoS ONE)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0084011)
- Davidov, E., & Depner, F. (2011). Testing for measurement equivalence of human values across online and paper-and-pencil surveys. *Quality & Quantity, 45*(2), 375–390. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-009-9297-9. This study exists and is relevant, but I did not retrieve its results. — [Davidov & Depner](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-009-9297-9)

**ISPOR task-force guidance on modification levels**
- Coons, S. J., Gwaltney, C. J., Hays, R. D., Lundy, J. J., Sloan, J. A., Revicki, D. A., Lenderking, W. R., Cella, D., & Basch, E. (2009). Recommendations on evidence needed to support measurement equivalence between electronic and paper-based patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures: ISPOR ePRO Good Research Practices Task Force report. *Value in Health, 12*(4), 419–429. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1524-4733.2008.00470.x. As summarized in Muehlhausen et al. 2015 [full text of the secondary source]:
  - **Minor** modification: placing a paper questionnaire into a screen format "without significantly altering item content, recall period or response options". "Minor levels of modification also include going from multiple items per page to one item per screen, for example on a handheld device." The evidence required is "cognitive interviewing and usability testing."
  - **Moderate** modification: "may result in changes to the (perceived) meaning of the assessment items". Examples are "splitting an item into multiple screens (e.g., having a question and its responses on different screens), using a scroll bar to view all item text or responses, and changing the order of item presentation." Moderate changes call for a quantitative equivalence study. Paper to IVRS is also classed as moderate.
  - **Substantial** modification: changes to wording or response options. The migrated instrument is to be "treated as a brand new instrument requiring full psychometric testing."
  - Sources: [Muehlhausen et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4597451/); [ISPOR report page](https://www.ispor.org/heor-resources/good-practices/article/recommendations-on-evidence-needed-to-support-measurement-equivalence-between-electronic-and-paper-based-patient-reported-outcome-%28pro%29-measures)
- Eremenco, S., Coons, S. J., Paty, J., Coyne, K., Bennett, A. V., & McEntegart, D. (2014). PRO data collection in clinical trials using mixed modes: Report of the ISPOR PRO Mixed Modes Good Research Practices Task Force. *Value in Health, 17*(5), 501–516. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2014.06.005. The machine-generated summary (Semantic Scholar TLDR, **not** the abstract) says a successful mixed-modes trial requires "a 'faithful migration,' measurement equivalence established between modes, and carefully planned implementation to minimize the risk of increased measurement error impacting the power of the trial to detect a treatment effect." — [Eremenco et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2014.06.005)
- O'Donohoe, P., Reasner, D. S., Kovacs, S. M., Byrom, B., Eremenco, S., Barsdorf, A. I., Arnera, V., & Coons, S. J. (2023). Updated recommendations on evidence needed to support measurement comparability among modes of data collection for patient-reported outcome measures: A good practices report of an ISPOR Task Force. *Value in Health, 26*(5), 623–633. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2023.01.001 [abstract]. This report updates the 2009 and 2014 reports and recommends "an evidence-based approach to determine whether new research is needed". "In cases where sufficient evidence of measurement comparability exists and best practices for faithful migration are followed, this task force concludes that further testing of measurement comparability among the data collection modes is unnecessary, including cases of 'mixing modes' within clinical trials such as bring your own device designs." — [O'Donohoe et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2023.01.001)

### Inferences
- The PRO-migration literature treats items per screen as a low-risk change for *mean scores and test–retest agreement*. Those are the outcomes it measures: ICCs and mean differences. It mostly does not test factor structure or inter-item covariance, which is where the survey-methods literature finds grid and proximity effects (Q1). The two literatures are not in conflict. They measure different properties. For scale development, where EFA/CFA depend on the covariance matrix, "minor" by ISPOR's mean-score standard does not guarantee identical covariance structure.
- ISPOR classes *changing item order* as moderate, one step more serious than changing items per screen. That matters if a platform randomizes item order while the reference version used a fixed order.
- Rutherford et al. (2016) found that heterogeneity in paper-vs-electronic comparisons was explained by construct type (physical vs psychological). Psychological constructs may therefore be more mode-sensitive. I did not verify the direction of that effect.

### Gaps
- I could not read Coons et al. (2009) or O'Donohoe et al. (2023) in full; publisher PDFs returned 403. The minor/moderate examples above come from Muehlhausen et al.'s (2015) verbatim summary of Coons 2009. I could not confirm whether the 2023 update reclassifies items-per-screen or scrolling changes.
- Eremenco et al. (2014) is characterized only from a machine-generated summary.
- Gwaltney et al. (2008) figures come from a search-engine rendering of the ISPOR abstract page, not the PDF.
- No meta-analysis isolates "items per screen" as a moderator of paper-vs-electronic equivalence. Muehlhausen et al. (2015) call for standardizing it but do not analyze it.

## 3. Item-order and context effects: grouped vs randomized order, carryover, consistency, "near means related", and randomization

### Takeaway
Grouping items by subscale tends to raise alpha slightly and can raise convergent-discriminant separation. Part of that rise is artifact: positive sequential dependency between consecutive answers inflates alpha when same-scale items are adjacent (Shimada & Katahira 2023). The evidence on whether grouping changes the factor structure is mixed. Several personality studies (Schell & Oswald 2013; Buchanan et al. 2018; Gottfried et al. 2022; Blötner & Grüning 2025 preprint; Hunt 2007) find little or no effect. Older symptom-inventory and organizational studies (Franke 1997; Schriesheim et al. 1989) find real effects on means, reliability and validity. Context effects on prevalence and means can be large (Huang & Cornell 2016: 45% higher bullying prevalence). Randomizing order removes systematic order bias but spreads order-dependent variance across people, and it is itself a protocol change that ISPOR classes as "moderate".

### Cited Findings

**Grouped vs randomized order: reliability and validity**
- Schriesheim, C. A., Kopelman, R. E., & Solomon, E. (1989). The effect of grouped versus randomized questionnaire format on scale reliability and validity: A three-study investigation. *Educational and Psychological Measurement, 49*(3), 487–508. https://doi.org/10.1177/001316448904900301 [abstract]. "Neither format is clearly superior to the other on any of the examined psychometric properties, although a weak trend showed that the grouped item format produced slightly superior measurement properties." — [Schriesheim et al. 1989a](https://doi.org/10.1177/001316448904900301)
- Schriesheim, C. A., Solomon, E., & Kopelman, R. E. (1989). Grouped versus randomized format: An investigation of scale convergent and discriminant validity using LISREL confirmatory factor analysis. *Applied Psychological Measurement, 13*(1), 19–32. https://doi.org/10.1177/014662168901300103 [abstract]. With N = 80 per group, CFA showed "the grouped format was superior to the random format, particularly with respect to the weaker measures (the job characteristics scales)." — [Schriesheim et al. 1989b](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662168901300103)
- Schriesheim, C. A. (1981). The effect of grouping or randomizing items on leniency response bias. *Educational and Psychological Measurement, 41*(2), 401–411. https://doi.org/10.1177/001316448104100219 [abstract]. "The random relative to the grouped mode showed substantially less leniency response bias," though the bias was "still considerable for the random mode." — [Schriesheim 1981](https://doi.org/10.1177/001316448104100219)
- Franke, G. H. (1997). "The whole is more than the sum of its parts": The effects of grouping and randomizing items on the reliability and validity of questionnaires. *European Journal of Psychological Assessment, 13*(2), 67–74. https://doi.org/10.1027/1015-5759.13.2.67 [abstract]. Two SCL-90-R studies (N = 130 and 134) compared the standard mixed order with homogeneous item blocks. Item-blocking had "significant effects … on the mean values, on the reliability, and the validity of the questionnaire." The findings "seriously call into question the admissibility of subscale-extraction for self-report inventories." This is directly relevant to administering subscales or short forms in a different arrangement from the validated full instrument. — [Franke 1997](https://doi.org/10.1027/1015-5759.13.2.67)
- Schell, K. L., & Oswald, F. L. (2013). Item grouping and item randomization in personality measurement. *Personality and Individual Differences, 55*(3), 317–321. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2013.03.008 [search summary only; abstract elided in the APIs]. The IPIP Big Five was administered sorted at random, sorted by factor, or cycled through factors. "The underlying measurement model and the internal consistency of the scale was unaffected by differences in item order." — [ScienceDirect listing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886913001281)
- Buchanan, E. M., Foreman, R. E., Johnson, B. N., Pavlacic, J. M., Swadley, R. L., & Schulenberg, S. E. (2018). Does the delivery matter? Examining randomization at the item level. *Behaviormetrika, 45*(2), 295–316. https://doi.org/10.1007/s41237-018-0055-y [abstract]. Two meaning-in-life scales containing suicidality and depression items (chosen as candidates for item reactivity) were compared under randomized vs fixed computer delivery. "Item randomization does not alter scale psychometrics for meaning in life scales." The abstract's framing is apt: scales "are customarily evaluated based on a set modality (i.e., computer or paper) and administration (fixed-item order). Deviating from an established administration profile could result in non-equivalent response patterns." — [Buchanan et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41237-018-0055-y)
- Weinberg, M. K., Seton, C., & Cameron, N. (2018). The measurement of subjective wellbeing: Item-order effects in the Personal Wellbeing Index—Adult. *Journal of Happiness Studies, 19*(1), 315–332. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-016-9822-1 [abstract]. Randomizing the order of PWI domains "revealed lower mean scores and more variation in scores." A single-factor solution held regardless of order. The authors recommend "that all new scales be subject to [item-order] investigation." — [Weinberg et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-016-9822-1)
- Huang, F. L., & Cornell, D. G. (2016). Question order affects the measurement of bullying victimization among middle school students. *Educational and Psychological Measurement, 76*(5), 724–740. https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164415622664 [abstract]. In a randomized experiment (n = 5,951), "changing the sequence of questions can result in 45% higher prevalence rates." — [Huang & Cornell 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164415622664)
- Hunt, J. (2007). *An examination of the effects of item grouping on internal consistency and scale intercorrelation* (Master's thesis, Western Kentucky University) [abstract]. N = 204, measuring conscientiousness and extraversion. Grouping did not raise alpha (.80 ungrouped vs .76 grouped) or lower the scale intercorrelation (.31 grouped vs .27 ungrouped), and neither difference was significant. — [Hunt 2007](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/theses/500)
- Etzel, J. M., Holland, J., & Nagy, G. (2021). The internal and external validity of the latent vocational interest circumplex: Structure, relationships with self-concepts, and robustness against item-order effects. *Journal of Vocational Behavior, 124*, 103520. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2020.103520 [abstract]. Item orders designed to elicit cross-domain or within-domain comparisons gave a circumplex structure that "was remarkably similar (although not fully invariant) across conditions." — [Etzel et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2020.103520)

**Sequential dependency and carryover between adjacent items**
- Shimada, D., & Katahira, K. (2023). Sequential dependencies of responses in a questionnaire survey and their effects on the reliability and validity of measurement. *Behavior Research Methods, 55*(6), 3241–3259. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-022-01943-z (preprint: https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/s8dea) [abstract]. Study 1: "each respondent's previous response had a positive effect on his or her current response." Study 2 was a theoretical investigation: positive lag-1 dependency "reduced the reliability, coefficient α, and validity of the subscales". However, "sequential dependencies were found to make these indicators higher when the items were grouped than when they were randomized and cause an overestimation of reliability by the coefficient α." Study 3 confirmed this in real data. — [Shimada & Katahira 2023](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-022-01943-z)
- Weijters, B., Geuens, M., & Schillewaert, N. (2009). The proximity effect: The role of inter-item distance on reverse-item bias. *International Journal of Research in Marketing, 26*(1), 2–12. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2008.09.003 [search summary]. "The (positive) correlation between a nonreversed item pair decreases with increasing inter-item distance," and the negative correlation between reversed pairs becomes stronger with distance. The authors recommend dispersing reversed items and modelling a response-style factor in CFA. — [IDEAS/RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ijrema/v26y2009i1p2-12.html)
- **Contrary evidence:** Gottfried, J., Michele [sic; spelling as indexed by OpenAlex, unverified], L., & Ježek, S. (2022). Proximity of items does not necessarily affect their correlation. *European Journal of Psychological Assessment, 38*(4), 282–292. https://doi.org/10.1027/1015-5759/a000661 [abstract]. Two studies (N = 742 and 205) used per-respondent randomized order. "We expected to see a strong item proximity effect … but we found no evidence of such effect … a general item proximity effect does not need to be considered a major factor when employing … common psychological inventories with randomized item order." — [Gottfried et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1027/1015-5759/a000661)
- Knowles, E. S. (1988). Item context effects on personality scales: Measuring changes the measure. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 55*(2), 312–320. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.55.2.312 [search summary only]. Items appearing later in a questionnaire are more strongly related to the total score than items appearing earlier, a serial-position "consistency" effect. — [Knowles 1988](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.55.2.312)
- Blötner, C., & Grüning, D. J. (2025). The item context effect: Do previously answered items influence psychometric properties of self-report personality scales? PsyArXiv preprint. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/wsr6j_v1 [abstract; not peer reviewed]. Four experiments (N = 4,895) with BFI-10 and HEXACO-18 found "little variation … in the responses and subscale-specific inter-item correlations". The authors conclude researchers "need not worry too much about the order in which they administer their target measures." **Flag:** the abstract also says "measurement invariance did not hold across conditions", which reads as inconsistent with that conclusion. It may be a wording error in the abstract. Check the full text before citing. — [Blötner & Grüning 2025](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/wsr6j_v1)

**How layout interacts with order (format × context)**
- Stefkovics, Á., & Kmetty, Z. (2022). A comparison of question order effects on item-by-item and grid formats: Visual layout matters. *Measurement Instruments for the Social Sciences, 4*, 8. https://doi.org/10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z [full text]. This was a preregistered 3×2 experiment in a Hungarian online panel using ESS welfare items. A priming item placed before the target shifted attitudes negatively, "but the effect was significant only when questions were presented on separate pages" (e.g., B = 0.38 for priming before the target on separate pages vs −0.00 in the grid). The authors suggest that item-by-item presentation produces "deeper cognition", while in grids the "near means related" heuristic produces higher inter-item correlations instead of priming. — [Stefkovics & Kmetty 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z)

### Inferences
- The two layouts produce *different kinds* of context dependence. Grids or multi-item pages raise correlations among co-displayed items (proximity and "near means related"). One item per screen can make each item more open to priming by the content of the preceding item (Stefkovics & Kmetty 2022). Neither layout is free of context. They differ in which artifact they carry. So switching layout between development and validation, or between a study and its comparison norms, can change the covariance structure even when means look similar.
- Grouping same-scale items, whether on one page or in sequence, inflates alpha through sequential dependency (Shimada & Katahira 2023). Combined with grid proximity effects, a scale developed with all its items on one page may show higher alpha and cleaner EFA loadings than the same scale given one item per screen or interleaved. This is inferred by combining the cited mechanisms. I found no single study that crossed items-per-screen × grouping × EFA.
- Randomization turns systematic order effects into person-level noise. That is appropriate for unbiased means across conditions, but it departs from a fixed-order reference version. Buchanan et al. (2018) and Schell & Oswald (2013) suggest this matters little for well-established personality scales. Franke (1997), Weinberg et al. (2018) and Huang & Cornell (2016) show it can shift means and prevalence for symptom, wellbeing and victimization measures.

### Gaps
- I could verify Schell & Oswald (2013), Knowles (1988) and Weijters et al. (2009) only through search summaries. Their abstracts are elided in the open indexes.
- I did not locate a "Wilson et al." item-order study matching the prompt and cannot attribute findings to it.
- Melnick, S. A. (1993), "The effects of item grouping on the reliability and scale scores of an affective measure", *Educational and Psychological Measurement, 53*(1), https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164493053001023, is relevant, but I did not retrieve its findings.

## 4. What do the Standards (AERA/APA/NCME 2014), the ITC computer-based testing guidelines, and scale-development guides say about standardizing and documenting administration format?

### Takeaway
The *Standards* (2014) require test specifications to define item formats and "the ordering of items and sections" (4.2). They require administration directions clear enough to replicate the conditions under which reliability, validity and norm data were obtained (4.15). Any permitted variation must be identified and justified (4.5), and the mode of administration in validation data must be reported (1.10). Those who allow non-standard administration bear the burden of showing it "did not affect … the comparability of the scores" (Ch. 6). The ITC guidelines require evidence that "the method of item presentation ensures that the results … are equivalent", and evidence of equal means and SDs before paper-based norms are used for a computerized version. Boateng et al.'s (2018) primer does not address layout or items per screen.

### Cited Findings

**AERA, APA, & NCME. (2014). *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*. Washington, DC: American Educational Research Association.** [full text verified from the open PDF] — [Standards 2014 PDF](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)
- **Standard 4.2:** "the test specifications should define the content of the test, the proposed test length, the item formats, the desired psychometric properties of the test items and the test, and the ordering of items and sections."
- Chapter 4 text on administration specifications: "Administration procedures include mode of test delivery (e.g., paper-and-pencil or computer based) … For tests administered by computer, administration specifications will also include a description of any hardware and software requirements."
- **Standard 4.15:** "The directions for test administration should be presented with sufficient clarity so that it is possible for others to replicate the administration conditions under which the data on reliability, validity, and (where appropriate) norms were obtained. Allowable variations in administration procedures should be clearly described."
- **Standard 4.5:** "If the test developer indicates that the conditions of administration are permitted to vary from one test taker or group to another, permissible variation in conditions for administration should be identified. A rationale for permitting the different conditions and any requirements for permitting the different conditions should be documented." The Comment gives "computer administration of a test form in some locations and paper-and-pencil administration of the same form in other locations" as the example. It adds: "Test developers should specify procedures for monitoring variations and for collecting evidence to show that the target construct is or is not altered by allowable variations."
- **Standard 1.10:** "When validity evidence includes statistical analyses … the conditions under which the data were collected should be described in enough detail that users can judge the relevance of the statistical findings to local conditions. ### Inferences
- Applied to a web platform, the Standards' logic is as follows. Items per screen, item order and response-format rendering are part of the "administration conditions under which the data on reliability, validity, and … norms were obtained" (4.15) and of the test specifications (4.2). Changing them is a "variation" that should be documented and justified (4.5). If scores are to be compared with published norms or other studies, the burden of showing comparability falls on whoever introduced the variation (Ch. 6).
- The ITC requirement of comparable means, SDs, reliabilities and external correlations, checked "for appropriate subpopulations", is a practical minimum evidence set for treating a re-formatted version as interchangeable with a reference version.

- The ITC's 2022 *Guidelines for Technology-Based Assessment* (a later document) were not retrieved. They may supersede the 2005 CBT guidelines on some points.

## 5. Does using different formats ACROSS instruments within one study (some paged, some scrolling or grid) cause problems?

### Takeaway
Direct evidence is thin but points the same way. Format effects carry over from one block to the next: a grid raises inter-item correlations in the *following* set of questions (Silber et al. 2018). Layout changes how strongly earlier items prime later ones (Stefkovics & Kmetty 2022). Survey methodologists explicitly warn that mixing item-by-item and grid formats "may introduce measurement inequivalence". I found no study that experimentally varied format consistency *across* instruments and measured respondent confusion or cross-scale correlations.

### Cited Findings
- Stefkovics & Kmetty (2022): "The findings highlight that mixing item-by-item and grids formats in online surveys may introduce measurement inequivalence, especially when question order effects are expected." In the discussion: "we observed measurement inequivalence between the two formats, which raises concerns about their simultaneous use in one survey design. In mixed-device web surveys, the visual layout is routinely optimized to screen size, for instance, some respondents receive an item in an item-by-item format, while others receive it in a grid … in such designs, the order of the questions will cause measurement error in some cases, but not in others." [full text] — [Stefkovics & Kmetty 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z)
- Silber et al. (2018): "the inter-item correlations of the set of follow-up questions were higher if the preceding set of questions was presented in a grid … respondents seem to apply the interpretative near means related heuristic … also when answering follow-up questions preceded by grid questions." This is evidence that one instrument's layout can alter the measurement properties of the next instrument. [abstract] — [Silber et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/13645579.2017.1381478)
- Roßmann et al. (2018): question design moderated the effect of respondent ability and motivation on satisficing. Grids raised nondifferentiation and nonsubstantive answers. Satisficing therefore depends on format and could differ across instruments formatted differently within one session. [abstract] — [Roßmann et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/jssam/smx020)
- Revilla & Couper (2018): small interface details within item-by-item formats on smartphones (an always-visible "next" button) substantially raised item missing data. Navigation conventions that vary between pages within a survey are a plausible source of missingness. [abstract] — [Revilla & Couper 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317715626)
- Toepoel et al. (2009): more items per screen raised nonresponse and worsened respondents' evaluation of the layout. Mavletova et al. (2018): grids increased reported technical difficulties and lowered evaluation of the survey. [abstracts] — [Toepoel et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/1525822X08330261); [Mavletova et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317735307)
- ITC CBT Guidelines (Guideline 7): "using consistent screen locations and colour for instructional text and prompts, using consistent screen design, layout and colours." This is a usability standard in favour of within-test interface consistency, not an empirical finding. [full text] — [ITC CBT Guidelines](https://www.intestcom.org/files/guideline_computer_based_testing.pdf)
- Rutherford et al. (2016) found mixing paper and electronic modes *across occasions* did not bias PRO scores. Their largest bias came from mixing *assisted clinic* with *self-completed home* administration. So some kinds of mixing are benign for means. [abstract] — [Rutherford et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-015-1110-8)

### Inferences
- In a multi-instrument battery, cross-scale correlations (convergent and discriminant validity, and common-method variance) can be inflated when instruments share a grid or page. They can also be affected by the layout of the preceding block (Silber et al. 2018). If some instruments are paged and others scroll, between-instrument correlations would carry a format-dependent component that differs by pairing. This is inferred from the correlation-level findings; it has not been tested directly.
- A within-study inconsistency also becomes a between-study comparability problem. If instrument A was normed item-by-item but administered here in a scrolling grid, and instrument B the reverse, then A–B correlations and each instrument's alpha are not directly comparable with the published values. Standards 1.10 and 4.15 call for documenting this.

### Gaps
- I found no experiment that manipulated *consistency* of layout across instruments within a session (all paged vs all scrolling vs mixed) and measured confusion, satisficing, or cross-scale correlations. This appears to be a genuine gap in the literature, not just a search failure, but I cannot rule out a study I missed.
- I found no evidence specific to clinical or psychological instruments, as opposed to attitude batteries, on mixing layouts within a session.
