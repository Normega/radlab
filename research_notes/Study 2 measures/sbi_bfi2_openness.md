### Instructions (Source B, Appendix B)

> For each statement listed below, please circle the one number that best indicates how true the
> particular statement is for you. There are no right or wrong answers. Please be as honest as you can.

The printed form also has `Gender:` (and, per Source A, age) fields at the top. Source D's online
adaptation reads "please pick the one number" in place of "please circle the one number".

### Response scale

- **7 points, 1 to 7.**
- Endpoint anchors as printed above the number columns in Appendix B: **1 = strongly disagree**,
  **7 = strongly agree**. Book text: "response scale for the SBI ranges from 1 (strongly disagree) to
  7 (strong[ly] agree)." Source C (Bryant, 2021): "a 7-point Likert-type scale (1 = strongly
  disagree, 7 = strongly agree)".
- Points 2-6: no labels were found in the retrieved snippets; they appear to be unlabelled numbers.
  **Not confirmed from a page image.**

### Items (verbatim, published order; Source B Appendix B, cross-checked against Table 2.1 of B)

Subscale codes: **ANT** = Anticipating ("Savoring Through Anticipation"), **MOM** = Savoring the
Moment, **REM** = Reminiscing ("Savoring Through Reminiscence"). **(R)** = negatively worded item,
reverse-scored (or subtracted) in scoring.

1. Before a good thing happens, I look forward to it in ways that give me pleasure in the present. — ANT
2. It's hard for me to hang onto a good feeling for very long. (R) — MOM
3. I enjoy looking back on happy times from my past. — REM
4. I don't like to look forward to good times too much before they happen. (R) — ANT
5. I know how to make the most of a good time. — MOM
6. I don't like to look back at good times too much after they've taken place. (R) — REM
7. I feel a joy of anticipation when I think about upcoming good things. — ANT
8. When it comes to enjoying myself, I'm my own "worst enemy." (R) — MOM
9. I can make myself feel good by remembering pleasant events from my past. — REM
10. For me, anticipating what upcoming good events will be like is basically a waste of time. (R) — ANT
11. When something good happens, I can make my enjoyment of it last longer by thinking or doing certain things. — MOM
12. When I reminisce about pleasant memories, I often start to feel sad or disappointed. (R) — REM
13. I can enjoy pleasant events in my mind before they actually occur. — ANT
14. I can't seem to capture the joy of happy moments. (R) — MOM
15. I like to store memories of fun times that I go through so that I can recall them later. — REM
16. It's hard for me to get very excited about fun times before they actually take place. (R) — ANT
17. I feel fully able to appreciate good things that happen to me. — MOM
18. I find that thinking about good times from the past is basically a waste of time. (R) — REM
19. I can make myself feel good by imagining what a happy time that is about to happen will be like. — ANT
20. I don't enjoy things as much as I should. (R) — MOM
21. It's easy for me to rekindle the joy from pleasant memories. — REM
22. When I think about a pleasant event before it happens, I often start to feel uneasy or uncomfortable. (R) — ANT
23. It's easy for me to enjoy myself when I want to. — MOM
24. For me, once a fun time is over and gone, it's best not to think about it. (R) — REM

Structural rule (Source A, footnote 2): "odd-numbered items were positively-anchored, even-numbered
items were negatively-anchored, and the temporal focus of the items alternated from future to
present to past, successively." The list above satisfies this rule, and every item maps onto the
abbreviated labels in Source A's Table 2 in the same within-subscale order.

### Scoring (Source B, Appendix C, which gives two methods; Source A used the first)

**Method 1, difference score** (used in the 2003 validation; Source A, Table 3 notes b and c):
sum the 4 positive items and subtract the sum of the 4 negative items. Subscale range -24 to +24;
total (12 positive minus 12 negative) range -72 to +72. Appendix C SPSS example for Reminiscing:
`(sbi3+sbi9+sbi15+sbi21)-(sbi6+sbi12+sbi18+sbi24)`.

**Method 2, mean score** (Appendix C): "A. Reverse-score the following items: 4, 10, 16, 22.
B. Sum responses to these four reverse-scored items. C. Sum responses to the following four items
… [D. add the sums from B and C] E. Divide the resulting total by 8, to get a mean score for the SBI
Anticipating subscale." Same pattern for Savoring the Moment (reverse 2, 8, 14, 20) and Reminiscing
(reverse 6, 12, 18, 24); the total reverse-scores "the following 12 (even-numbered) items" and
divides by 24. Mean scores lie on the 1-7 "absolute scale", which Appendix C interprets as
"4 is the midpoint on the absolute scale (50th percentile); 5 lies at the 66.67th percentile …
7 is the highest possible 'absolute' score". SPSS variable for this subscale: `momntavg`
("Mean score for SBI Savoring the Moment subscale").
The reverse-scoring formula itself was not visible in the snippets; on a 1-7 scale it is 8 − x.

### Savoring the Moment administered on its own

- **No published guidance found** from Bryant either permitting or advising against stand-alone
  administration of one subscale. Neither Source A, Source B (searched for "short form",
  "separately", "by itself") nor Source C addresses it.
- What the sources do support: Source A reports the three subscales as separately scored scales with
  their own reliabilities and shows discriminant predictive validity (each temporal subscale predicted
  its own time frame better than the other two). Savouring the Moment alpha across the six 2003
  samples: .78, .83, .78, .69, .68, .89; 3-week test-retest r = .88 (n = 21). Book Table 2.1
  gives alpha = .78 for Savoring the Moment.
- Precedent, not guidance: other researchers have used subsets (Source D administered six items,
  two per subscale; Source F cites Hou et al., 2016 and Eisner et al., 2009, both of which
  report findings for the "ability to savor the moment" specifically).
- Practical implications for stand-alone use: keep the 4 positive / 4 negative balance (all 8 items),
  and note that the items were validated interleaved with the other two subscales, so a block of only
  MOM items changes the context in which they were normed.

### Discrepancies between sources

1. **Anchor direction in the 2003 article.** Source A's Method text says items were rated "on a
   7-point scale ranging from 'strongly agree' (1) to 'strongly disagree' (7)". The same article's
   Table 3 note says "higher scores reflecting stronger endorsement", and both Source B (form) and
   Source C (Bryant, 2021) give 1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree. Treat the 2003 Method
   sentence as an error, or as a description of a reversed printing; use 1 = strongly disagree.
2. **Paraphrased items in secondary literature.** Source F quotes "I find it hard to hang onto a good
   feeling" (item 2 is "It's hard for me to hang onto a good feeling for very long."); Source E
   quotes "I find it easy to enjoy myself when I want to" (item 23 is "It's easy for me to enjoy
   myself when I want to.") and "I don't like to look forward too much" (item 4 continues "to good
   times too much before they happen."). Source A's Table 2 labels are abbreviations and must not
   be used as item text.
3. **Source G (lawyerwellbeing.net)** has item 11 "by thinking/doing certain things" (the book has
   "thinking or doing") and truncates item 22 to "…I often start to feel uneasy." (the book has
   "uneasy or uncomfortable."). Its instructions are paraphrased.
4. **Instruction verb.** Paper form: "circle the one number"; Source D online: "pick the one number".
   For a web form, "pick" or "select" is a defensible adaptation, but it is a change.
5. **Do not confuse with the Children's SBI** (CSBI; Cafasso, 1994), also in the book (e.g. "I know how
   to have a good time"; "It's hard for me to get excited about fun times before they happen."). Its
   items and reverse-keyed set (2, 6, 12, 17 for one subscale) differ from the adult SBI.

### Scoring (Source I scoring key; Source K)

Source I, verbatim:
- "Open-Mindedness: 5R, 10, 15, 20, 25R, 30R, 35, 40, 45R, 50R, 55R, 60"
- "Intellectual Curiosity: 10, 25R, 40, 55R"
- "Aesthetic Sensitivity: 5R, 20, 35, 50R"
- "Creative Imagination: 15, 30R, 45R, 60"

Source K (SPSS): reverse-key with `(1=5) (2=4) (3=3) (4=2) (5=1)` (i.e. 6 − x); each facet =
`MEAN` of its 4 items; domain `BFI2_O = MEAN(BFI2_O_IntellectualCuriosity,
BFI2_O_AestheticSensitivity, BFI2_O_CreativeImagination)`. Because the facets have equal item
counts, this equals the mean of the 12 keyed items. Scores range 1-5.

Source L adds: "researchers interested in examining the BFI-2's scale-level associations with other
variables can safely score each domain and facet scale using simple aggregation of the raw item
responses. However, we strongly recommend that researchers interested in conducting item-level
analyses of the BFI-2 account for the influence of acquiescence by either centering each
individual's set of item responses around their within-person mean or modeling acquiescence as a
method factor." With one domain only, within-person centering would use only those 12 items,
which is not what the authors validated (they centred across all 60).

### Administering a single domain

- **No published guidance found** on administering one BFI-2 domain on its own: not on the Colby page
  (H) or in the article (L), and a web search found nothing.
- Related guidance on the Colby page concerns length only: the BFI-2-S (30 items) and BFI-2-XS
  (15 items) "are appropriate for research contexts in which, due to pressing concerns about
  assessment time or respondent fatigue, administering the full BFI-2 would not be feasible. For
  most studies, however, we recommend administering the full measure due to its greater reliability
  and validity."
- Implications to note in the prereg: the 12 items should keep their facet balance and keying.
  Present them under the same instructions, stem and anchors. They will no longer be interleaved
  with the other 48 items, and the published norms (Source H's "Descriptive statistics for the BFI-2")
  come from full-form administration.

### Discrepancies between sources

1. **BFI-2 vs BFI-2-S keys.** A web-search summary of "the BFI-2 scoring key" returned
   "Open-Mindedness: 5, 10R, 15, 20R, 25, 30R". That is the **BFI-2-S (30-item)** key, whose item
   numbers refer to a different form. Use only the 60-item key above.
2. **Facet order.** The scoring key (I), item list (J) and the article's item-loading table (L) list
   the facets as Intellectual Curiosity, Aesthetic Sensitivity, Creative Imagination. The SPSS
   syntax (K) computes Aesthetic Sensitivity before Intellectual Curiosity. This is presentation
   only; item content and keying agree across Sources I, J, K and L.
3. **Item wording.** The article's item-loading table (L, text extraction) has the same 12 O items,
   printed without final periods and tagged Original/Revised/New. The extraction shows "new Ideas"
   and "abstract Ideas" capitalised, which looks like a text-extraction artefact. The form (I) has
   "ideas"; administer the form's wording.
4. **Licence wording.** See above: "personal and research use", not "freely available for
   non-commercial research".
