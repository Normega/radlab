## 2. Instructions shown to respondents (verbatim, source B and B′)

> For each statement below, please select the number that best describe your general experiences using a scale from 1 to 5, where 1 = *Never* and 5 = *All the Time*.

- "best describe" (not "describes") appears this way in both the 2022 and 2025 versions of the chapter. It is a grammatical slip in the source, reproduced here exactly. The third-party psytests form (F) reads "For each statement below, please select the number that best describes your general experiences." It corrects the grammar and drops the scale clause. That form is not authoritative.
- The instructions name **no time window** ("your general experiences"), so this is a trait measure.

## 3. Response scale

**5 points, scored 1–5. Only the two endpoints are labelled in every authoritative source:**

| Value | Label (source B, verbatim) | Label (source A, verbatim) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Never | never |
| 2 | (no label published) | (no label published) |
| 3 | (no label published) | (no label published) |
| 4 | (no label published) | (no label published) |
| 5 | All the Time | all the time |

- Source A, p. 236: "Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never, 5 = all the time)."
- Source D, p. 939: "Participants indicated the frequency with which each statement reflects their experiences on a 5-point scale (never to all the time)."
- Source E, p. 3: "The items are answered on a scale from 1 ("Never"), to 5 ("All the time")."
- Source F renders the scale as "1 : Never / 2 / 3 / 4 / 5 : All the Time".
- **Labels for points 2–4 (e.g., "Rarely / Sometimes / Often") do not appear in any source I retrieved.** One web-search summary asserted them, but they could not be traced to a document. Do not add them as if they were official. If the online form needs labels on every point, that is an adaptation and should be reported as one.

| 6 | I can slow my thinking at times of stress. |
| 9 | I notice that I don't take difficulties so personally. |

### 4b. As administered on the developers' standalone 11-item form (source B, Appendix 1)

This is the order to use when administering the EQ-D on its own. It is **not** ascending EQ order. It follows the Factor 1 loading order of Table 1 in source A (Sample 1), with EQ20 last.

1. I am better able to accept myself as I am. *(EQ3)*
2. I can observe unpleasant feelings without being drawn into them. *(EQ15)*
3. I notice that I don’t take difficulties so personally. *(EQ9)*
4. I can treat myself kindly. *(EQ14)*
5. I can separate myself from my thoughts and feelings. *(EQ10)*
6. I have the sense that I am fully aware of what is going on around me and inside me. *(EQ16)*
7. I can slow my thinking at times of stress. *(EQ6)*
8. I can actually see that I am not my thoughts. *(EQ17)*
9. I am consciously aware of a sense of my body as a whole. *(EQ18)*
10. I can take time to respond to difficulties. *(EQ12)*
11. I view things from a wider perspective. *(EQ20)*

In the source form, each item is preceded by a blank answer line ("______ 1. …").

### 4c. The other nine EQ items (not scored on the EQ-D; source A, Table 1, verbatim, for reference)

- Rumination factor: EQ1 "I think about what will happen in the future." EQ4 "I notice all sorts of little things and details in the world around me." EQ7 "I wonder what kind of person I really am." EQ11 "I analyze why things turn out the way they do." EQ13 "I think over and over again about what others have said to me." EQ19 "I think about the ways in which I am different from other people."
- Did not load: EQ2 "I remind myself that thoughts aren't facts."
- Dropped from the decentering factor at the CFA stage ("NA" in Table 1, Sample 2 and Study 3): EQ5 "I am kinder to myself when things go wrong." EQ8 "I am not so easily carried away by my thoughts and feelings."

## 5. Scoring and keying

- **Score = sum of the 11 items, range 11–55. Higher = greater decentering.** Source A, p. 239: "Scores on this factor can range from 11 to 55, with higher scores indicating greater decentering."
- **No reverse-keyed items.** No source describes any reversal. All 11 items are worded in the decentering direction, and a straight sum of 11 items scored 1–5 yields exactly the published 11–55 range. (For the TMS, the developers' chapter says explicitly that no item is reversed; the EQ chapter does not make that statement in its accessible text. So "all positively keyed" for the EQ-D rests on the item content plus the scoring statement, not on an explicit sentence.)
- A possible source of confusion: source D's Table 1 lists the EQ as "11/4" under "# items/# negative". That column counts items referring to **negative content** ("the EQ has four (of 11) items focused on negative experiences", p. 937). It does not mean reverse-keyed items.
- Internal consistency: α = .83 (Study 1, both student samples, source A). Source D reports α = .81–.84 citing Fresco et al. Source E reports α = .88.
- Reading level: Flesch-Kincaid grade 4.3 (source A, p. 239).

## 7. Is the decentering subscale administered on its own? Yes.

- The developers' own published form (source B, Appendix 1) **is** the 11-item decentering scale alone. The chapter abstract describes the EQ as "an 11-item self-report measure of trait levels of decentering".
- Source A, Studies 2 and 3: "This study utilized the 11-item decentering factor that was derived in Study 1." The text does not say whether the full 20 items were still administered in those studies.
- Source D (Naragon-Gainey & DeMarree, 2017, p. 939): "The EQ … is an 11-item measure of decentering guided by an MBCT framework."
- The label "EQ-D" for the decentering subscale is used in source E (Rader et al., 2023) and by the third-party site F.
- One design consequence: the six rumination items "were included in the EQ to help rule out the possibility that any increases in wider perspective might be explained by an acquiescent response bias" (source A, p. 236). The standalone 11-item form drops that control. All 11 remaining items are keyed in the same direction, so acquiescence is uncontrolled. This is relevant given the acquiescence notes in `../Reverse keyed items and acquiescence/`.

## 8. Discrepancies between sources

1. **Item order.** The 20-item EQ numbering (3, 6, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 20) differs from the standalone form's 1–11 order (EQ3, 15, 9, 14, 10, 16, 6, 17, 18, 12, 20). Record which order was administered, and keep the EQ number on each variable so the data can be compared across both.
2. **Rader et al. (2023) item list error.** Source E, p. 3, gives the 11 decentering items as "item 3, 6, **8**, 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 and 20". Source A's Tables 1 and 2 show **EQ9** retained and **EQ8** dropped ("NA"). Source E's list is wrong on that one item. Do not copy it.
3. **"EQ-D" means different things.** Source E says "The EQ contains 14 Decentering items (EQ-D) and 6 Rumination items". That is the original *rational* 14-item "wider perspective" subscale described in source A (p. 236: "rumination (6 items) and wider perspective (14 items)"), not the 11-item empirical factor. When citing "EQ-D", specify the 11 items.
4. **Instruction grammar.** "best describe" (sources B and B′, verbatim) versus "best describes" (third-party F).
5. **Anchor capitalisation.** "never … all the time" (A), "*Never* … *All the Time*" (B), "Never … All the time" (E). The wording is the same in all three.
6. **Apostrophes.** The developers' form image uses typographic ’ in "don’t". The article text extraction gives a straight '. This is cosmetic.
7. **Contact address.** The article (2007) gives fresco@kent.edu. The chapter (2022/2025) gives fresco@umich.edu, which is current.
8. **Factor structure.** Source A reports a single factor in students and in remitted-MDD patients. Later work finds two factors: Gecht et al. 2014 (German) and Rader et al. 2023, both "Distanced Perspective" and "Accepting Self-perception" (source E). Source D finds the EQ items load mainly on an "Observer Perspective" factor shared with other measures. The total score is the developers' scoring. Treat subscores as exploratory.

## 9. Alternatives for an online survey

### 9a. Toronto Mindfulness Scale – Decentering subscale (TMS-D): 7 items, verified

Sources: Haigh, Valö, Bosma & Lau (2025), "Toronto Mindfulness Scale (TMS) State Version", https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-031-47219-0_16 . Valö, Haigh, Bosma & Lau (2025), "Toronto Mindfulness Scale (TMS): Trait Version", https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-031-47219-0_17 . Both are in the same Springer Handbook, and Lau, the TMS developer, is a co-author on both. Original article: Lau et al. (2006), *J. Clin. Psychol., 62*, 1445–1467, doi:10.1002/jclp.20326 (not retrieved; paywalled). Trait adaptation: Davis, Lau & Cairns (2009), cited in source D.

- **The state version is the original** (Lau et al., 2006). It is designed to be completed right after a meditation session. Its instructions (verbatim, State appendix) begin: "We are interested in what you just experienced. … how well does the statement describe what you just experienced, just now?"
- **Trait version instructions (verbatim):** "We are interested in your day-to-day experiences. Below is a list of things that people sometimes experience. Please read each statement. Next to each statement are five choices: “not at all,” “a little,” “moderately,” “quite a bit,” and “very much.” Please indicate the extent to which you agree with each statement. In other words, how well does the statement describe your experience?"
- **Anchors (all five labelled):** Not at all / A little / Moderately / Quite a bit / Very much. The state form scores these 0–4.
- **Decentering items = TMS items 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 11, summed.** Verbatim scoring note in both chapters: "All items are written in the positively keyed direction, so no reverse scoring of items is required". This also verifies the [unverified] TMS keying claim in `../Reverse keyed items and acquiescence/mindfulness_scales.md`.
- Trait-version decentering items (verbatim):
  1. I experience myself as separate from my changing thoughts and feelings.
  2. I am more concerned with being open to my experiences than controlling or changing them.
  4. I experience my thoughts more as events in my mind than as a necessarily accurate reflection of the way things ‘really’ are.
  7. I am receptive to observing unpleasant thoughts and feelings without interfering with them.
  8. I am more invested in just watching my experiences as they arise, than in figuring out what they could mean.
  9. I approach each experience by trying to accept it, no matter whether it is pleasant or unpleasant.
  11. I am aware of my thoughts and feelings without overidentifying with them.
  
  (The state version uses past tense: "I experienced myself as separate from my changing thoughts and feelings." and so on.)
- Reliability: α = .85 for the trait TMS-D (Davis et al., 2009, as reported in source D).
- **Licence: not stated** in either retrieved chapter's accessible text. I found no permission statement for the TMS. **[unverified]**
- Fit for Study 2: shorter (7 vs 11 items) and every scale point is labelled. But the trait version is a later adaptation of a state instrument. Its items are long and abstract ("overidentifying", "necessarily accurate reflection"), harder reading than the EQ's grade-4.3 items. And its permission position is undocumented.

### 9b. Drexel Defusion Scale (DDS): 10 vignettes, verified

Source: Forman, Herbert, Juarascio, Yeomans, Zebell, Goetter & Moitra (2012), "The Drexel defusion scale: A new measure of experiential distancing", *Journal of Contextual Behavioral Science, 1*, 55–65. Full text from the authors' lab: https://drexel.edu/~/media/Files/psychology/labs/innovation/Forman%20Herbert%202012%20Drexel%20Defusion%20Scale.ashx . (It is a JCBS paper, not *Behavior Modification*. The byline is confirmed from the PDF.)

- Format: respondents first read a **long definition of defusion** (hands-over-face and yellow-sunglasses metaphors, about 300 words, Appendix 1). They then rate 10 hypothetical scenarios. Rating instruction (verbatim, Table A1): "Based on the definition of defusion above, please rate each scenario according to the extent to which you would normally be in a state of defusion in the specified situation. You may want to read through all the examples before beginning to respond to the questions. (Important: you are not being asked about the degree to which you would think certain thoughts or feel a certain way, but the degree to which you would defuse if you did.)"
- Anchors: 0 Not at all, 1 A little, 2 Somewhat, 3 Moderately, 4 Quite a lot, 5 Very much. This is read from a table header that the PDF splits across columns. Two separate text extractions agree on it, but check it against the rendered PDF before use.
- Scenario headings: Feelings of anger. Cravings for food. Physical pain. Anxious thoughts. Thoughts of self. Thoughts of hopelessness. Thoughts about motivation or ability. Thoughts about your future. Sensations of fear. Feelings of sadness. Example, item 5 verbatim: "Thoughts of self. Imagine you are having a thought such as ``no one likes me.'' To what extent would you normally be able to defuse from negative thoughts about yourself?"
- Reliability: α = .80–.83 (as reported in source D). Correlates |.43| with the EQ (Forman et al. 2012, as cited in source D, p. 937).
- Licence: no permission statement found in the article. **[unverified]**
- Fit for Study 2: **poor for an online survey.** The authors themselves note that "the instructions explaining defusion are nevertheless relatively long" (p. 64). All 10 items reference negative content. It measures defusion (ACT), not MBCT-style decentering. And the definition primes the construct, which risks demand effects (the authors make this point too).

### 9c. How well the three agree

Source D found these measures converge only weakly: "Correlations differed greatly in magnitude depending on the specific measure (rs = .11 to .66)", with a mean r of .37 among decentering measures alone. They are not interchangeable. Choosing one is choosing a definition of decentering.

### Recommendation

For a **trait** decentering measure in an online survey, the **EQ-D (11 items)** is already the best-documented short option. It is free to use with citation, endorsed by the developers as a standalone form, and easy to read (grade 4.3). The TMS-D trait version is the only shorter verified alternative (7 items). It is worth considering only if fully labelled anchors matter more than documented permission and readability. The DDS is not suitable here.
