## 1. Midpoint presence and response styles (MRS, ERS, ARS/NARS, DARS, misresponse): prevalence and contamination of substantive measures

### Takeaway
The cleanest experiment, Weijters, Cabooter & Schillewaert (2010), finds that adding a midpoint **raises net acquiescence (NARS), lowers extreme responding (ERS), and lowers misresponse to reversed items (MR)**. The mechanism is that, when no midpoint is offered, ambivalent respondents are pushed disproportionately toward *disagreement* and toward the extremes. Scale format alone moved "% agreeing" with the same intention items from 22.6% to 60.6%. Response styles contaminate scale means and inter-scale correlations in predictable directions (Baumgartner & Steenkamp 2001), so odd and even formats are not interchangeable and their results are not directly comparable.

### Cited Findings

**Weijters, B., Cabooter, E., & Schillewaert, N. (2010). The effect of rating scale format on response styles: The number of response categories and response category labels. *International Journal of Research in Marketing, 27*(3), 236–247. doi:10.1016/j.ijresmar.2010.02.004** (numbers below are from the Vlerick working paper 2010/07, the pre-publication version; the published version may differ slightly) **[V]**
- Design: online panel, Belgium, Dutch-speaking men only, N = 1,207, response rate 27%, ages 15–65 (median 49). The design was 4 × 2 between-subjects: 4, 5, 6 or 7 points, crossed with all categories labeled vs endpoints only. Cell n = 137–175. The authors decomposed number of points into two orthogonal factors, **midpoint (yes/no)** and **gradations of (dis)agreement (2 vs 3)**: 4 = no midpoint + 2 gradations; 5 = midpoint + 2; 6 = no midpoint + 3; 7 = midpoint + 3. NARS = ln((#agree+1)/(#disagree+1)); ERS = ln((#extreme+1)/(#non-extreme+1)), computed over 21 randomly drawn heterogeneous items. — [Vlerick WP 2010/07](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf); [IDEAS record of published article](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ijrema/v27y2010i3p236-247.html)
- The paper deliberately did **not** model midpoint response style (MRS) as an outcome "because we study the effect of including (or omitting) a midpoint" (footnote 1). — [WP](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)
- Rationale: the midpoint attracts truly neutral/indifferent respondents, who without a midpoint "randomly shift" to the nearest category. It also attracts **ambivalent** respondents, who experience distress when forced to choose and who "will tend to react negatively in the absence of a midpoint" (citing Gilljam & Granberg 1993; Nowlis, Kahn & Dhar 2002; Klopfer & Madden 1980). — [WP](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)
- Results:
  - "The inclusion of a midpoint leads to a significant increase in NARS (H4) and a significant decrease in ERS (H5)".
  - The ERS decrease is smaller when the scale also has 3 rather than 2 gradations (i.e., 7 vs 6 points differ less than 5 vs 4).
  - Adding the midpoint lowers MR, and the reduction is significantly stronger in fully labeled formats (Wald χ²(1) = 13.31, p = .0003).
  - Misresponse to reversed items was mostly negative (39%) rather than positive (2%) (binomial p < .001).
  - Labeling × midpoint interactions on NARS and ERS were not significant (χ²(1) = 0.33, p = .567; χ²(1) = 1.25, p = .263). — [WP](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)
- Interpretation in the paper: "Including a neutral point leads to an increase in NARS due to a disproportional movement of otherwise negative responses to the midpoint. Ambivalent respondents who are forced to take sides tend to react negatively." — [WP](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)
- Other format effects:
  - Full labeling → higher NARS, lower ERS, lower MR.
  - More gradations → lower ERS and no change in NARS, but higher MR when only the endpoints are labeled. — [WP](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)
- Practical magnitude: the percentage of respondents expressing positive trial/purchase intention on the same items "varied widely across formats (from 22.6% through 60.6%)". Distributions were significantly affected by labeling and midpoint inclusion, but not by adding a gradation. — [WP](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)
- Recommendation: "Overall, we suggest avoiding scales without a midpoint, unless particular, relevant reasons present themselves." Even-numbered formats "perform poorly in terms of MR." For general populations use 5-point scales; 7-point scales are less problematic for students and other high-verbal-ability groups. Study 2 (UK online panel, N = 226, 5-point only) found that endpoint-only labels gave higher criterion validity (attitude→intention R²) than full labels. — [WP](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)

**Baumgartner, H., & Steenkamp, J.-B. E. M. (2001). Response styles in marketing research: A cross-national investigation. *Journal of Marketing Research, 38*(2), 143–156. doi:10.1509/jmkr.38.2.143.18840** **[A]**
- The paper examined five styles: acquiescence (ARS), disacquiescence (DARS), extreme response style/response range (ERS/RR), midpoint responding (MRS) and noncontingent responding (NCR). Data were large representative consumer samples from 11 EU countries. Response styles had "systematic effects … on scale scores as a function of two scale characteristics (the proportion of reverse-scored items and the extent of deviation of the scale mean from the midpoint of the response scale)". Correlations between scales "can be biased upward or downward depending on the correlation between the response style components." — [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/response-styles-in-marketing-research-a-cross-national-investigat/); [Tilburg repository](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/3b6fce19-7cf9-4e79-93e8-97c119308eea)

**Van Vaerenbergh, Y., & Thomas, T. D. (2013). Response styles in survey research: A literature review of antecedents, consequences, and remedies. *International Journal of Public Opinion Research, 25*(2), 195–217. doi:10.1093/ijpor/eds021** **[A]**
- The review defines response styles as content-independent tendencies (ARS, DARS, ERS, MRS) that produce systematic error, and organizes the literature into antecedents (respondent, cultural and scale-format factors), consequences and remedies. — [scinapse record](https://scinapse.io/papers/2128355832); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257924458_Response_Styles_in_Survey_Research_A_Literature_Review_of_Antecedents_Consequences_and_Remedies)
- The full text could not be retrieved this session (the Purdue PDF link returned 404), so its specific statements on midpoint inclusion are not verified here.

**Kieruj, N. D., & Moors, G. (2013). Response style behavior: Question format dependent or personal style? *Quality & Quantity, 47*(1), 193–211. doi:10.1007/s11135-011-9511-4** **[A]**
- The study varied scale length from 5 to 11 categories and used a latent-class factor model that captures ERS and ARS simultaneously. ERS "occurs regardless of scale length"; there was only weak evidence of ARS. ERS was reasonably stable across questionnaires and associated with personality profile and age, which supports treating ERS as a **personal style** rather than purely a format artifact. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/qualqt/v47y2013i1p193-211.html)
- [M] Companion paper: Kieruj, N. D., & Moors, G. (2010). Variations in response style behavior by response scale format in attitude research. *IJPOR, 22*(3), 320–342. Not checked this session.

**Moors, G. (2008). Exploring the effect of a middle response category on response style in attitude measurement. *Quality & Quantity, 42*(6), 779–794. doi:10.1007/s11135-006-9067-x** **[M]**
- I could not retrieve the abstract this session. It is a split-ballot comparison with and without a middle category, modelled with latent-class factor models of ERS. Its specific findings should be checked before citing.

**Cabooter et al. (2016)** **[M]**
- Most likely: Cabooter, E., Weijters, B., Geuens, M., & Vermeir, I. (2016). Scale format effects on response option interpretation and use. *Journal of Business Research, 69*(7), 2574–2584. Not retrieved this session; the citation details and findings need checking.

**Wetzelhütter, D. (2020). Scale-sensitive response behavior!? Consequences of offering versus omitting a "don't know" option and/or a middle category. *Survey Practice, 13*(1). doi:10.29115/SP-2020-0012** **[V]**
- Online experiment with Austrian university students (N = 1,282), unipolar satisfaction items, four conditions: 4 categories; 4 + middle category; 4 + DK; 4 + middle + DK.
- Students chose the middle category about 7 times per 20 items when no DK option was offered, falling to about 4 per 20 when DK was also offered (t = 9.408, p < .001).
- DK use stayed at about 6 per 20 whether or not a middle category was present (t = .649, p = .517).
- Omitting the middle category produced significant increases in extreme responses and decreases in mild responses.
- Author: "Respondents choosing a MC do not necessarily have a moderate view: the presence of a DK-O in addition to a MC does reduce the proportion." — [Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484-scale-sensitive-response-behavior-consequences-of-offering-versus-omitting-a-don-t-know-option-and-or-a-middle-category)

### Inferences
- Taken together, Weijters et al. and Wetzelhütter show that removing a midpoint does not make "neutral" respondents disappear. They are redistributed, asymmetrically: toward disagreement (raising measured negativity, i.e., lower NARS) and toward the extremes (raising ERS). Means, top-box percentages and variances therefore all shift with format. Any comparison across studies, waves or groups that used different formats is confounded.
- Because a midpoint lowers misresponse to reversed items, removing it can degrade the factor structure of balanced (mixed-keyed) scales. This is a psychometric cost of forced choice that is separate from bias in the mean.
- Baumgartner & Steenkamp's result implies that how much MRS/ERS contaminates a scale's mean depends on how far the true mean sits from the midpoint. On scales whose true means are near the midpoint, MRS is hard to separate from substantive neutrality.
- Weijters et al. found a full-labeling × midpoint interaction for MR. This suggests that a labeled midpoint ("neutral") gives the clearest benefit; an unlabeled centre box on an endpoint-labeled scale gives less.

### Gaps
- No direct experimental estimate was found of how *midpoint presence* changes the *measured MRS level* or MRS's covariance with substantive scores. MRS is undefined on even scales, so this comparison is structurally hard. Weijters et al. explicitly excluded MRS.
- The findings of Moors (2008) and Cabooter et al. (2016) were not verified. The full text of the Van Vaerenbergh & Thomas review was not retrieved.
- The Weijters et al. numbers come from the working paper. The published IJRM version should be checked for any changes to estimates.
- Both Weijters samples are Western European panels, and Study 1 was men only, so generalisation to women or to non-European samples is untested in that study.

## 2. Cross-cultural differences in midpoint use and implications for group comparisons

### Takeaway
East Asian respondents (Japanese, Chinese, Taiwanese, Hong Kong) use the midpoint more than North American and Western European respondents, and Americans and Hispanic/Latin groups use the extremes more. Within cultures, more individualistic respondents use the midpoint less. Si & Cullen (1998) found that dropping the midpoint (even-numbered scales) reduced the central tendency of China/Japan/Hong Kong managers and increased their variance. This is the main argument that an even scale can partly equalise response-style differences across cultures. Evidence on whether this improves *validity* of comparisons is mixed: Chen et al. found style differences "generally did not alter" mean comparisons, and Lee et al. (2002) found the best number of categories differed by culture.

### Cited Findings

**Chen, C., Lee, S., & Stevenson, H. W. (1995). Response style and cross-cultural comparisons of rating scales among East Asian and North American students. *Psychological Science, 6*(3), 170–175. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.1995.tb00327.x** **[A]**
- High-school students from Sendai (Japan), Taipei (Taiwan), Edmonton/Calgary (Canada) and Minneapolis/Fairfax County (US) answered fifty-seven 7-point Likert items.
- "Japanese and Chinese students were more likely than the two North American groups to use the midpoint", and US students were more likely than the other three groups to use the extremes.
- "Within each cultural group, endorsement of individualism was positively related to the use of extreme values and negatively related to the use of the midpoint."
- These differences "generally did not alter cross-cultural comparisons of item means." — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.1995.tb00327.x/)

**Si, S. X., & Cullen, J. B. (1998). Response categories and potential cultural bias: Effects of an explicit middle point in cross-cultural surveys. *International Journal of Organizational Analysis, 6*(3), 218–230. doi:10.1108/eb028885** **[A]**
- Managers from China, Japan and Hong Kong were compared with managers from the US, Germany and the UK on Western management scales. The two groups differed in central tendency, "particularly the likelihood of choosing the midpoint", and in overall variance.
- Even-numbered response categories (no explicit midpoint) "decreases the central tendencies of the China/Japan/Hong Kong group and increases the variance".
- The authors advise careful consideration of odd vs even formats when surveying these cultures. — [Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/eb028885/full/html)

**Lee, J. W., Jones, P. S., Mineyama, Y., & Zhang, X. E. (2002). Cultural differences in responses to a Likert scale. *Research in Nursing & Health, 25*(4), 295–306. doi:10.1002/nur.10041** **[A]**
- Chinese (n = 136), Japanese (n = 323) and American (n = 160) respondents completed the 13-item Sense of Coherence scale with 4, 5 or 7 response options.
- Japanese respondents more often reported difficulty with the scale, and Chinese respondents more often skipped items.
- Both Asian groups "selected the midpoint more frequently on items that involved admitting to a positive emotion" than Americans.
- Construct validity was better with **4 options for Chinese and Americans, but with 7 for Japanese**.
- The sense-of-coherence–health association was positive in all three groups. — [abstract via science.gov topic page](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/l/likert+scale+questions.html) (secondary aggregator; check against the journal abstract)

**Harzing, A.-W. (2006). Response styles in cross-national survey research: A 26-country study. *International Journal of Cross Cultural Management, 6*(2), 243–266. doi:10.1177/1470595806066332** **[A]**
- The study covered 26 countries and modelled ARS, ERS and middle response style (MRS).
- Country-level power distance, collectivism, uncertainty avoidance and extraversion positively predicted acquiescence and (positive) extreme response styles.
- Respondents from Spanish-speaking countries showed higher ERS and ARS; "Japanese and Chinese respondents tended to be far less extreme."
- Harzing describes it as the most-cited IJCCM article. — [mili.eu summary](https://mili.eu/?p=262); [Harzing blog](https://harzing.com/blog/2017/02/what-if-fully-agree-doesnt-mean-the-same-thing-across-cultures) (secondary; country-level MRS coefficients not verified)

**Johnson, T., Kulesa, P., Cho, Y. I., & Shavitt, S. (2005). The relation between culture and response styles: Evidence from 19 countries. *Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology, 36*(2), 264–277. doi:10.1177/0022022104272905** **[A]**
- ERS data from 19 countries and ARS data from 10 countries were related to Hofstede dimensions. — [search-indexed abstract via Shavitt profile](https://giesbusiness.illinois.edu/profile/sharon-shavitt)
- [M] Commonly reported direction: power distance and masculinity are associated with more ERS, and individualism/uncertainty avoidance/power distance/masculinity with less ARS. Not verified this session; the study did not model MRS.

**Baumgartner & Steenkamp (2001)** found that response styles vary across 11 EU countries and bias cross-national scale scores. — [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/response-styles-in-marketing-research-a-cross-national-investigat/) **[A]**

### Inferences
- Removing the midpoint shrinks one measurable source of cross-cultural non-equivalence (differential MRS), as Si & Cullen show. It does not remove ARS/ERS differences, and it may convert East Asian midpoint use into differential *disagreement* or *extreme* responding. Weijters et al.'s finding that forced ambivalent respondents drift negative would interact with culture. This is an untested inference.
- Lee et al.'s finding that the best number of categories differs by culture means no single format is "culture-fair". Measurement-invariance testing (or explicit response-style modelling) is a more defensible remedy than format choice alone.
- Chen et al.'s finding that mean comparisons were mostly unaltered suggests MRS distorts variances, extremity-based statistics (top-box %, ERS-sensitive correlations) and standardised effects more than raw item means. Researchers comparing groups should be most careful with variance-based and percentage-agree summaries.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly tests whether forced choice *improves measurement invariance or criterion validity* across cultures, as opposed to merely changing distributions. Si & Cullen report distributional changes only, based on the abstract; the full text was not retrieved.
- Harzing's country-level MRS findings (which predictors relate to MRS specifically) were not verified from the full text.
- The cross-cultural evidence found is mostly 1995–2006 and mostly from paper or early web surveys. No recent online-panel or crowdsourced cross-national midpoint study was located.

## 3. Careless/insufficient-effort responding (C/IER) and the midpoint

### Takeaway
Satisficing theory (Krosnick 1991) predicts that low-effort respondents pick the midpoint as an easy way out. Empirical work shows that the midpoint is used for many non-neutral reasons ("it depends", unsure, don't know, item unclear), and that offering a DK option drains a large share of midpoint choices. These findings support the midpoint as a partial "dumping ground". However, **no experiment was found that randomly assigned odd vs even scales and measured attention-check failure, long-string or other C/IER indices.** The careless-responding literature treats midpoint runs mainly as one *detection* pattern. Random responding pulls means toward the midpoint, which can inflate or deflate correlations.

### Cited Findings
- **Satisficing account.** Respondents who "lean" one way but pick the midpoint to save cognitive effort are satisficing (Krosnick 1991). Satisficing rises with lower ability, lower motivation and harder tasks. — [Survey Practice, Wetzelhütter 2020, summarising Krosnick](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484); primary source **[M]**: Krosnick, J. A. (1991). Response strategies for coping with the cognitive demands of attitude measures in surveys. *Applied Cognitive Psychology, 5*(3), 213–236. doi:10.1002/acp.2350050305
- **Midpoint as a hidden "don't know".** When a DK option is added, midpoint use roughly halves (≈7 → ≈4 per 20 items), while DK use is unchanged by adding a midpoint (Wetzelhütter 2020, Austrian students, online). — [Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484-scale-sensitive-response-behavior-consequences-of-offering-versus-omitting-a-don-t-know-option-and-or-a-middle-category) **[V]**
- **[M]** Sturgis, P., Roberts, C., & Smith, P. (2014). Middle alternatives revisited: How the neither/nor response acts as a way of saying "I don't know"? *Sociological Methods & Research, 43*(1), 15–38. doi:10.1177/0049124112452527. Follow-up probes of midpoint choosers indicated that many were "hidden don't knows" rather than genuinely neutral. The full text (Southampton eprint) returned 403, so the exact proportions are unverified. — [eprint link](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/197457/1/SMRrevised4.pdf)
- **Why people choose the middle (personality items).** Middle-category endorsements show relatively long response latency, an "it depends" connotation and "a strong, negative relationship with item clarity". The middle option can function as a "dumping ground" for unsure or not-applicable responses, even when respondents are told to skip items they don't know. — [Montclair research portal, Kulas & Stachowski](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/middle-category-endorsement-in-odd-numbered-likert-response-scale/) **[A]**. Primary citations **[M]**:
  - Kulas, J. T., Stachowski, A. A., & Haynes, B. A. (2008). Middle response functioning in Likert-responses to personality items. *Journal of Business and Psychology, 22*(3), 251–259.
  - Kulas, J. T., & Stachowski, A. A. (2009). Middle category endorsement in odd-numbered Likert response scales: Associated item characteristics, cognitive demands, and preferred meanings. *Journal of Research in Personality, 43*(3), 489–493.
  - Kulas & Stachowski (2013), *JRP, 47*(4), 254–262.
- **Midpoint meanings are heterogeneous.** Nadler, J. T., Weston, R., & Voyles, E. C. (2015). Stuck in the middle: The use and interpretation of mid-points in items on questionnaires. *Journal of General Psychology, 142*(2), 71–89. The study compared 4-point forced choice, 5-point with a "neither" midpoint, and 4-point plus a separate "no opinion" option on 28 attitude items. Respondents' interpretations of the midpoint "varied widely", most commonly "no opinion", "don't care", "unsure", "neutral", "equal/both" and "neither". — [search-indexed abstract](https://www.siue.edu/education/psychology/faculty/nadlerbio.shtml) **[A]**
- **Careless respondents gravitate to midpoint patterns.**
  - Respondents who give partially random data have mean scores closer to the scale midpoint than careful respondents (Stosic, M. D., Murphy, B. A., Duong, F., Fultz, A. A., Harvey, S. E., & Bernieri, F. (2024). Careless responding: Why many findings are spurious or spuriously inflated. *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*, doi:10.1177/25152459241231581). — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/25152459241231581) **[A — from search snippet; full text 403]**
  - In a machine-learning study of careless respondents using a gibberish scale, "uniform midpoint response throughout the survey" was the most common characterised careless pattern (Bloy, L., Resheff, Y., Kluger, A., Malovicki-Yaffe, N. (2025). *AMPPS*, doi:10.1177/25152459251378420). — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/25152459251378420) **[A — search snippet only; unverified]**
- **Midpoint selection as a quality indicator in panels.** Cornesse & Blom (2023) used straightlining, item nonresponse and midpoint selection as satisficing indicators. They found "significantly more straight-lining in nonprobability online panels than in probability-based online panels". Respondents in **neither** panel type were significantly affected by an experimental manipulation of the visual design of the scale midpoint. Citation: Cornesse, C., & Blom, A. G. (2023). Response quality in nonprobability and probability-based online panels. *Sociological Methods & Research, 52*(2), 879–908. doi:10.1177/0049124120914940. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0049124120914940) **[A — search snippet; full text 403]**
- **Core C/IER method papers** **[M — standard references, not re-checked this session; none reports an odd vs even manipulation as far as I know]**:
  - Meade, A. W., & Craig, S. B. (2012). Identifying careless responses in survey data. *Psychological Methods, 17*(3), 437–455. doi:10.1037/a0028085. Recommends bogus/instructed items, long-string, even-odd consistency and Mahalanobis D. It is commonly cited for an estimate of roughly 10–12% careless respondents in an undergraduate sample (verify).
  - Huang, J. L., Curran, P. G., Keeney, J., Poposki, E. M., & DeShon, R. P. (2012). Detecting and deterring insufficient effort responding to surveys. *Journal of Business and Psychology, 27*(1), 99–114. doi:10.1007/s10869-011-9231-8.
  - Curran, P. G. (2016). Methods for the detection of carelessly invalid responses in survey data. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 66*, 4–19. doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2015.07.006. Long-string analysis counts the longest run of an identical option. Midpoint runs are one form, but the index is option-agnostic.
  - DeSimone, J. A., Harms, P. D., & DeSimone, A. J. (2015). Best practice recommendations for data screening. *Journal of Organizational Behavior, 36*(2), 171–181. doi:10.1002/job.1962.
  - Huang, J. L., Liu, M., & Bowling, N. A. (2015). Insufficient effort responding: Examining an insidious confound in survey data. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 100*(3), 828–845. doi:10.1037/a0038510. IER responses cluster near the scale midpoint, so when the attentive sample's mean lies away from the midpoint, IER can *inflate* correlations between substantive measures (verify).

### Inferences
- The midpoint is likely a **heterogeneous** category: true neutrality, ambivalence, uncertainty or don't-know, item ambiguity, and low-effort satisficing. Offering an explicit DK/NA option, and writing clear items, should drain the non-neutral components (Wetzelhütter; Kulas & Stachowski). This is a cheaper fix than deleting the midpoint.
- Removing the midpoint does not remove low effort. A satisficer facing an even scale can straightline on "slightly agree" or respond randomly. Wetzelhütter's increase in extreme and mild-shift responding without a middle category is consistent with this, but the claim that forced choice *increases random responding* is untested.
- Because random or midpoint-heavy careless responding pulls scores toward the centre, its biasing effect depends on where the true distribution sits (Huang et al. 2015, [M]; Baumgartner & Steenkamp 2001). This is a measurable risk on skewed clinical or wellbeing scales.
- A midpoint-run index is not a valid stand-alone carelessness flag. It confounds MRS (a stable style that varies by culture) with low effort. It should be combined with response-time and attention-check indices.

### Gaps
- **No randomized odd vs even experiment measuring attention-check failure, long-string, IRV or Mahalanobis-D outcomes was found.** This is the key evidence gap. The careless-responding literature uses midpoint runs as a symptom, not format as a manipulation.
- The exact proportion of "hidden don't knows" among midpoint choosers (Sturgis et al. 2014) is unverified.
- The details of Stosic et al. (2024), Bloy et al. (2025) and Cornesse & Blom (2023) come from search snippets because the publisher pages returned 403. Effect sizes are unverified.

## 4. Online panels, crowdsourcing (Prolific/MTurk), and smartphone completion

### Takeaway
In probability-based web panels, **device (smartphone vs PC) has little or no systematic effect on midpoint selection, acquiescence, straightlining or other satisficing indicators** (Antoun, Couper & Conrad 2017; Clement et al. 2020). Device differences that do appear tend to be small, inconsistent across waves, or explained by who chooses to use a phone. Nonprobability panels show more straightlining than probability panels (Cornesse & Blom 2023). No study was found that compares midpoint use specifically on Prolific/MTurk against probability panels, or odd vs even formats within crowdsourced samples.

### Cited Findings
- **Antoun, C., Couper, M. P., & Conrad, F. G. (2017). Effects of mobile versus PC web on survey response quality: A crossover experiment in a probability web panel. *Public Opinion Quarterly, 81*(S1), 280–306. doi:10.1093/poq/nfw088** — Randomized crossover design in the Dutch LISS probability panel (1,390 members; 1,004 completed on mobile and 1,127 on PC). There was no device difference in:
  - non-differentiation: 0.35 mobile vs 0.34 PC;
  - rounding;
  - CRT errors;
  - "half-open" responses.

  Smartphone users gave *longer* open-ended answers (short answers 46.7% mobile vs 53.7% PC). Respondents were "at least as likely to provide conscientious and thoughtful answers" on smartphones, despite more multitasking and others being present. — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/81/S1/280/3091905) **[V]**
- **Clement, S. L., Severin-Nielsen, M. K., & Shamshiri-Petersen, D. (2020). Device effects on survey response quality: A comparison of smartphone, tablet and PC responses on a cross sectional probability sample. *Survey Methods: Insights from the Field*.** Two Danish ISSP probability samples, 2018 (n = 1,865; RR 37%) and 2019 (n = 1,139; RR 38%), across seven quality indicators:
  - Midpoint responding: no significant device effect in either year.
  - "Strongly agree" responding: smartphone users chose it more in 2018 (OR = 1.463, p < .05), but this did not replicate in 2019.
  - Straightlining (2019 only): higher on tablets than PCs, but the effect vanished after controlling for demographics.
  - Overall: "no systematic evidence of device effects on survey response quality". — [surveyinsights.org](https://surveyinsights.org/?p=13585) **[V]**
- **Cornesse & Blom (2023)**, *Sociological Methods & Research*: more straightlining in nonprobability than probability online panels; an experimental change to the visual design of the midpoint had no significant effect in either. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0049124120914940) **[A]**
- **Crowdsourced platform quality** **[M]**: Peer, E., Rothschild, D., Gordon, A., Evernden, Z., & Damer, E. (2022). Data quality of platforms and panels for online behavioral research. *Behavior Research Methods, 54*, 1643–1662. doi:10.3758/s13428-021-01694-3. Prolific (and CloudResearch) showed better attention and data quality than MTurk and some commercial panels. Not re-verified this session, and it does not address midpoints.
- **Device self-selection** **[M]**: Lugtig, P., & Toepoel, V. (2016). The use of PCs, smartphones and tablets in a probability-based panel survey: Effects on survey measurement error. *Social Science Computer Review, 34*(1), 78–94. Attributes most device differences in measurement error to who chooses each device rather than to the device itself. Keusch, F., & Yan, T. (2017). Web versus mobile web: An experimental study of device effects and self-selection effects. *SSCR, 35*(6), 751–769. Neither was re-verified this session.

### Inferences
- For mobile completion, the evidence does not support worrying that phones *per se* increase midpoint use or acquiescence on standard radio-button Likert items. Layout matters more (e.g., horizontal 5- or 7-point grids that wrap or require scrolling, and slider handles that start at the centre). Sliders that default to the midpoint risk "non-moves" being recorded as midpoint answers. That is a design hazard, not a response style.
- For crowdsourced samples, the larger risk is low effort (straightlining or speeding) rather than culture-driven MRS. Format choice (odd vs even) is unlikely to fix this. Attention checks, response-time screening and platform choice (Prolific/CloudResearch over raw MTurk, per Peer et al. [M]) carry more weight.
- Prolific and MTurk samples are multinational (UK/US-heavy, with growing numbers from other countries). Pooling them can mix cultural MRS/ERS differences into apparent "low-effort" midpoint patterns, which argues for recording country and modelling it.

### Gaps
- No study was found comparing **midpoint/MRS prevalence on Prolific or MTurk vs probability panels**, or testing odd vs even formats within crowdsourced samples.
- No study was found isolating smartphone effects on **MRS specifically for odd vs even scales**. Device studies measure midpoint selection as a satisficing indicator on odd scales only.
- Keusch & Yan (2017), Lugtig & Toepoel (2016) and Peer et al. (2022) were not re-verified this session.

## 5. Ambivalence vs indifference: is forced choice psychologically meaningful?

### Takeaway
A bipolar midpoint conflates at least three states: **indifference** (low positive and low negative evaluation), **ambivalence** (high positive and high negative), and **uncertainty or don't know**. Attitude research (Thompson, Zanna & Griffin 1995) shows ambivalence and indifference are distinct and predict different behaviour, which a single midpoint cannot separate. Forcing a choice makes ambivalent respondents pick a side, and the evidence suggests they disproportionately pick the *negative* side (Weijters et al. 2010, citing Gilljam & Granberg 1993; Nowlis et al. 2002). So forced choice adds systematic negative-leaning error, not just random error. If ambivalence or indifference matter substantively, they should be measured directly: split positive/negative unipolar items (the Griffin ambivalence index) or an explicit DK/NA option.

### Cited Findings
- **Thompson, M. M., Zanna, M. P., & Griffin, D. W. (1995). Let's not be indifferent about (attitudinal) ambivalence. In R. E. Petty & J. A. Krosnick (Eds.), *Attitude strength: Antecedents and consequences* (pp. 361–386). Erlbaum.** **[A for the concept; page numbers M]**
  - The midpoint aggregates uncertain, ambivalent (moderate-to-high positivity *and* negativity) and indifferent (low positivity and negativity) states, which "reflect different attitudes and drive distinct behaviors".
  - The Griffin index, ambivalence = (P + N)/2 − |P − N| (often written 3W−S variants), separates them by measuring positive and negative reactions separately. — [summary via Wikipedia "Ambivalence"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambivalence); [Priester & Petty 1996 JPSP PDF discussing these indices](https://msbfile03.usc.edu/digitalmeasures/priester/intellcont/1996-JPSP-PriesterPetty-1.pdf) (secondary; formula notation should be checked against the chapter)
- **Klopfer, F. J., & Madden, T. M. (1980). The middlemost choice on attitude items: Ambivalence, neutrality, or uncertainty? *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 6*(1), 97–101. doi:10.1177/014616728061014** **[M]**
  - The search did not surface the article. Weijters et al. (2010) cite it for the argument that offering a midpoint "would increase data quality" — [WP](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf) **[V for the citing statement only]**.
  - The commonly reported finding is that middle responses were associated more with uncertainty than with ambivalence or neutrality. This is unverified and should be checked.
- **Forced choice pushes ambivalent respondents negative.** Weijters et al. hypothesised and supported that, without a midpoint, ambivalent respondents shift disproportionately toward disagreement. They cite Gilljam & Granberg (1993) for "a disproportional movement of negative answers to the midpoint when it was" offered, and Nowlis, Kahn & Dhar (2002) for ambivalent respondents being drawn to extremes when the midpoint is omitted. — [WP](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf) **[V]**
  - Primary citations **[M]**: Nowlis, S. M., Kahn, B. E., & Dhar, R. (2002). Coping with ambivalence: The effect of removing a neutral option on consumer attitude and preference judgments. *Journal of Consumer Research, 29*(3), 319–334. Gilljam, M., & Granberg, D. (1993). Should we take don't know for an answer? *Public Opinion Quarterly, 57*(3), 348–357.
- **Respondent-stated meanings of the midpoint** are a mixture ("no opinion", "don't care", "unsure", "neutral", "equal/both", "neither") (Nadler et al. 2015) — [SIUE profile/abstract](https://www.siue.edu/education/psychology/faculty/nadlerbio.shtml) **[A]**. Personality-item middle responses carry an "it depends" connotation tied to item ambiguity (Kulas & Stachowski) — [Montclair](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/middle-category-endorsement-in-odd-numbered-likert-response-scale/) **[A]**.

### Inferences
- Forced choice is psychologically meaningful only when the construct is genuinely bipolar and most respondents have a directional leaning. For constructs where ambivalence is common (e.g., attitudes toward one's own health behaviours, mixed emotional states, attitudes toward practices with known costs and benefits), forcing a choice converts ambivalence into a directional score that is biased toward negative and extreme answers (Weijters et al.; Nowlis et al. [M]).
- For constructs where "it depends" reflects item ambiguity (Kulas & Stachowski), the better fix is item rewriting or contextualisation, not removing the midpoint.
- When ambivalence or indifference is itself of interest, a defensible design measures positive and negative reactions on separate unipolar scales (Thompson et al. 1995). If "no opinion" is plausible, it should be offered as an explicit, visually separated DK/NA option. Midpoint presence then becomes a less consequential choice.

### Gaps
- The content of Klopfer & Madden (1980) could not be retrieved, so the "uncertainty > ambivalence" finding is unverified.
- No study was found that directly validates forced-choice responses of ambivalent respondents against behaviour or criterion measures (e.g., do forced "slightly disagree" answers from ambivalent people predict behaviour as well as a midpoint plus a separate ambivalence measure?). Weijters et al.'s Study 2 tested criterion validity only for labeling, not for midpoint presence.
