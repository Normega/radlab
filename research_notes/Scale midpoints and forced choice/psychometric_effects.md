# Psychometric consequences of including vs omitting a scale midpoint (and the number of response options)

Scope note for the report writer: most of the classic literature manipulates the *number* of response options (2 to 11+), so "odd vs even" is confounded with "more vs fewer points" (4 vs 5 is also "one more option"). Only a few studies isolate the midpoint (Simms et al. 2019 explicitly asked the odd/even question; Adelson & McCoach 2010 compare 4 vs 5; Chang 1994 compares two even formats; Kulas & Stachowski, Nadler et al., Hernández et al., Tijmstra & Bolsinova speak to what the midpoint *means* when present). Each finding below is tagged **[N-points]** (number of options generally) or **[midpoint]** (specific to presence/meaning of a middle category). Verification level is noted: **[abstract verified]** = read the publisher/indexed abstract; **[secondary]** = taken from another paper's summary or a search-engine summary, not the original.

## Q1. Experiments comparing the same items with and without a midpoint / with different numbers of options: what changes?

### Takeaway
Reliability (alpha, test-retest) rises from 2 to roughly 4-7 options and then plateaus; validity (convergent/criterion) is mostly insensitive to the number of options; model fit can worsen as categories increase. The one large randomized study that directly tested odd vs even (Simms et al. 2019, N = 1,358) found that including or removing the midpoint had no psychometric effect; the "6 is the magic number" press framing about avoiding the midpoint is the authors' interpretation, not a demonstrated midpoint effect.

### Inferences
- Across designs, the internal-consistency / test-retest gains from adding options are concentrated below about 5; between 4, 5, 6 and 7 points differences are small. So adding or removing one middle category on a 4- to 7-point scale is unlikely by itself to move alpha much; the main consequences are elsewhere (meaning of the midpoint, distributions, response styles).
- Validity (convergent/criterion) is consistently the least sensitive property (Simms 2019; Maydeu-Olivares 2009), meaning substantive correlations with outcomes rarely hinge on the midpoint decision.
- Children/younger respondents are a distinct case: fewer, simpler options (3-4) work as well or better and may be preferred (Adelson & McCoach; Alan & Kabasakal).
- The "more options → worse fit" result (Maydeu-Olivares) means a model-fit improvement after collapsing or dropping a midpoint may reflect fewer parameters/categories rather than better measurement.

- Leung (2011) findings not verified. From background knowledge (UNVERIFIED): Leung reported similar means (after rescaling), reliability, and factor structure across 4-, 5-, 6-, and 11-point versions, with the 11-point version closer to normal distribution. Verify before using.
- Not retrieved: Matell & Jacoby (1972) (my understanding, UNVERIFIED: in *Journal of Applied Psychology* 56(6), 506-509, "Is there an optimal number of alternatives for Likert-scale items? Effects of testing time and scale properties" — and their 1971 EPM paper found reliability and validity independent of the number of points; also found more points reduced use of the neutral category); Cox (1980, *Journal of Marketing Research* 17(4), 407-422, "The optimal number of response alternatives for a scale: A review" — UNVERIFIED: concluded 5-9 alternatives typically optimal, 2-3 inadequate, and that an odd number is preferable when neutrality is meaningful); Garland (1991, *Marketing Bulletin* 2, 66-70, "The mid-point on a rating scale: Is it desirable?" — UNVERIFIED: N≈ 63 in New Zealand; omitting the midpoint shifted responses toward the favourable side, interpreted as reducing social-desirability bias); Taherdoost (2019, *International Journal of Academic Research in Management* 8(1), 1-10 — narrative review, not primary evidence). These need direct checks.
- No meta-analysis specifically of midpoint presence/absence on reliability or validity was found. Kieruj & Moors and Simms are the closest large randomized comparisons.

## Q2. IRT / Rasch and response-process evidence: does the midpoint behave as an ordered category?

### Takeaway
Strong, convergent evidence that the middle category is a mixture: for many respondents/items it means "it depends", "don't know", "not applicable" or "no opinion", not a trait level between disagree and agree. This shows up as latent classes that use the midpoint very differently (Hernández et al. 2004), as item-driven ambiguity (Kulas & Stachowski 2009, 2013), and in IRTree mixture models estimating roughly 30% of middle responses as non-informative in one personality dataset (Tijmstra & Bolsinova 2025). Treating such responses as ordinal midpoints biases trait and item parameter estimates.

### Cited Findings
- **Hernández, A., Drasgow, F., & González-Romá, V. (2004). Investigating the functioning of a middle category by means of a mixed-measurement model. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 89*(4), 687-699. https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.89.4.687** [midpoint; abstract verified]. Mixed partial credit (mixed Rasch) model fitted to scales of a personality questionnaire with a "?" middle category. Most scales fit better with 2 latent subpopulations; "the most consistent difference among the latent classes was related to the functioning of the middle response category. For most of the examinees, the probability of choosing the middle category was very close to zero, but a nonnegligible percentage of people selected this category with much higher probability." "The total scores from the 2 subpopulations were incommensurate," and personality factors predicted class membership. (The 16PF was the instrument per my recollection — UNVERIFIED; the class proportions were not in the abstract.) — [Europe PMC abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22functioning%20of%20a%20middle%20category%20by%20means%20of%20a%20mixed-measurement%20model%22&resultType=core&format=json); [Illinois Experts record](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/investigating-the-functioning-of-a-middle-category-by-means-of-a-/)
- **Kulas, J. T., & Stachowski, A. A. (2009). Middle category endorsement in odd-numbered Likert response scales: Associated item characteristics, cognitive demands, and preferred meanings. *Journal of Research in Personality, 43*(3), 489-493. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2008.12.005** [midpoint; abstract verified]. Middle endorsements showed "a relatively high response latency, an 'it depends' connotation, and a strong, negative relationship with item clarity." Respondents endorse the midpoint "for a variety of reasons" although it is "typically scored as indicating moderate or neutral trait standing"; midpoint counts can flag items needing contextualization. — [Montclair State University research portal](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/middle-category-endorsement-in-odd-numbered-likert-response-scale/)
- **Kulas, J. T., & Stachowski, A. A. (2013). Respondent rationale for neither agreeing nor disagreeing: Person and item contributors to middle category endorsement intent on Likert personality indicators. *Journal of Research in Personality, 47*(4), 254-262. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2013.01.014** [midpoint; abstract verified]. - Kulas and colleagues also reported respondents use the middle option as a "not applicable" proxy even when implicitly instructed to skip items they cannot answer (this is the Kulas, Stachowski & Haynes 2008 finding; exact citation not verified — see Gaps). — [search summary of Montclair records](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/middle-category-endorsement-in-odd-numbered-likert-response-scale/)
- **Nadler, J. T., Weston, R., & Voyles, E. C. (2015). Stuck in the middle: The use and interpretation of mid-points in items on questionnaires. *The Journal of General Psychology, 142*(2), 71-89. https://doi.org/10.1080/00221309.2014.994590** [midpoint; secondary]. When asked what the midpoint meant, participants' interpretations varied widely: most commonly "no opinion," "don't care," "unsure," "neutral," "equal/both," "neither"; respondents may pick the midpoint when their true opinion is not neutral; the midpoint may be seen as more socially desirable than "no opinion"; 25% of item means were significantly affected by different response options. (DOI from memory — UNVERIFIED.) — [JMU PAIR survey methods note](https://www.jmu.edu/pair/ads/surveys/continuing-survey/cssmethodschange.pdf); [Kankaraš & Capecchi 2024, METRON](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40300-024-00276-5)
- **Tijmstra, J., & Bolsinova, M. (2025). Modeling within- and between-person differences in the use of the middle category in Likert scales. *Applied Psychological Measurement, 49*(6), 266-281. https://doi.org/10.1177/01466216251322285** [midpoint; IRTree; full text read]. Response-mixture IRTree: each middle response may be non-informative (node 1, non-response tendency θ1) or a genuine neutral position (node 2, target trait θ2). Simulation (N = 1,000/2,000; K = 25/50; 0/10/20% non-informative responses): ignoring the issue with a standard graded response model (GRM) increases bias and variance of θ estimates; at K = 50 with 20% non-informative responses, GRM average absolute bias is more than double that at 0%, and the mixture model cuts absolute bias by 44-45% and MSE by 24-28%. Under the GRM, non-informative midpoints act as noise that attenuates discrimination parameters and biases thresholds; the mixture model recovers them essentially without bias. Empirical example: 20-item, 5-category Machiavellianism scale (label "neutral"), N = 2,000: an estimated 4.91% of all responses and 30.76% of middle-category responses were non-informative; corr(θ1, θ2) = −.19. — [Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11873858/fullTextXML)
- Same paper, literature context: response-style models (latent class: Hernández et al. 2004, Maij-de Meij et al. 2008, Moors 2008, Rost et al. 1997; continuous: Bolt et al. 2014, Falk & Cai 2016, Tutz & Berger 2016) still assume the midpoint is on the same ordinal scale; if some respondents use it as non-response, "the assumption of ordinality of the item scores can no longer be maintained." IRTree models (Böckenholt 2012; De Boeck & Partchev 2012; Jeon & De Boeck 2016) model the midpoint as a separate decision node. — [Tijmstra & Bolsinova 2025](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11873858/fullTextXML)
- IRTree models are commonly split into a "midpoint primary process" (MPP; for odd scales — first decide whether to take the midpoint) and an "agreement primary process" (APP; used for even scales). — [Frontiers in Psychology 2023 article on IRTree response processes, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1003756](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1003756/full)
- Survey-methods literature on the middle alternative is cited as an ongoing debate: Kalton, Roberts & Holt (1980), "The effects of offering a middle response option with opinion questions," *The Statistician, 29*; Presser & Schuman (1980), "The measurement of a middle position in attitude surveys," *Public Opinion Quarterly, 44*; Raaijmakers et al. (2000); Sturgis et al. (2014). — [Tijmstra & Bolsinova 2025 reference list](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11873858/fullTextXML)

### Inferences
- The midpoint fails the ordinal assumption for some fraction of responses; the fraction is item-dependent (ambiguous, context-dependent, complex or sensitive items attract more) more than person-dependent (Kulas & Stachowski 2013), though stable person tendencies also exist (Hernández et al. 2004; θ1 in Tijmstra & Bolsinova).
- In Rasch/PCM analyses this mixture typically appears as a midpoint that is rarely modal anywhere on θ (narrow or absent region of maximal probability, i.e., disordered or reversed thresholds around the middle). The Hernández result (middle-choice probability near zero for most people) is the latent-class version of this.
- The 30.76% figure is one dataset/one model; it is a plausible order of magnitude, not a general constant.

## Q3. Does omitting the midpoint distort the latent trait estimate or improve discrimination?

### Takeaway
Evidence cuts both ways and is thin for a direct test. Removing the midpoint removes a category that is partly non-informative (so it can sharpen discrimination), but it forces genuinely neutral or "it depends" respondents to a side, which shifts means (usually away from the middle in a direction that can follow social desirability or acquiescence) and can add noise. The one large randomized trait study found no net psychometric difference (Simms et al. 2019). Keeping the midpoint and modeling it (IRTree/mixture) is the approach with the clearest demonstrated benefit for trait estimation.

### Cited Findings
- No psychometric effects of including vs removing the midpoint in a randomized BFI study (N = 1,358). [midpoint; secondary via review] — [Jebb et al. 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8129175/fullTextXML); design in [Simms et al. 2019 abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1037/pas0000648%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- Scoring midpoint responses as the scale's middle integer "may result in personality scale attenuation" when items are ambiguous. — [Kulas & Stachowski 2013](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/respondent-rationale-for-neither-agreeing-nor-disagreeing-person-/)
- Treating non-informative midpoints as ordinal (standard GRM) biases θ and attenuates item discriminations; modeling them recovers both (bias −44 to −45%, MSE −24 to −28% in the K = 50, 20% condition). This implies that the *noise* of a midpoint is a modeling problem, not intrinsic to offering it. — [Tijmstra & Bolsinova 2025](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11873858/fullTextXML)
- Midpoint users form a latent class whose total scores are "incommensurate" with non-users' — i.e., the same sum score means different things across classes when a midpoint is offered. — [Hernández et al. 2004](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22functioning%20of%20a%20middle%20category%20by%20means%20of%20a%20mixed-measurement%20model%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- Respondents may select the midpoint when not neutral, and changing response options shifted 25% of item means. — [Nadler et al. 2015 via JMU](https://www.jmu.edu/pair/ads/surveys/continuing-survey/cssmethodschange.pdf)
- Kieruj, N. D., & Moors, G. (2010). Variations in response style behavior by response scale format in attitude research. *International Journal of Public Opinion Research, 22*(3), 320-342 [N-points/odd-even; response styles; secondary]. LISS panel (Netherlands) respondents randomly assigned different scale formats for identical statements (Jan/Feb 2009); latent class factor models diagnose and correct extreme response style (ERS) and acquiescence (ARS) simultaneously. Findings (from memory, UNVERIFIED): ERS and ARS appeared regardless of format; the strength/presence of response styles varied somewhat with scale length and with midpoint presence. — [Tilburg University repository](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/49185c43-272c-4d8a-917d-6a99da18daf6/content); [LISS data archive study](https://dataarchive.lissdata.nl/study-units/view/44)

### Inferences
- Omitting the midpoint does not remove the underlying ambiguity; it relocates it into the adjacent categories (slightly agree / slightly disagree), where it is no longer identifiable. With a midpoint present, the ambiguity is at least visible and modelable (IRTree, mixture, or by flagging high-midpoint items for rewording, as Kulas & Stachowski suggest).
- Whether forced choice "improves discrimination" depends on what midpoint users would otherwise do: if they split near-randomly, discrimination falls; if they lean toward their true side, information rises. The available evidence does not settle which dominates in general; the Simms null suggests they roughly cancel for personality items in students.
- For constructs where true neutrality is common and meaningful (attitudes, momentary states), forcing a side is more likely to distort than for traits where "it depends" reflects item ambiguity.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly estimates trait-score bias for the same people under forced-choice vs midpoint formats using an IRT linking design.
- Garland (1991) and similar marketing studies reporting directional mean shifts (toward favourable/social-desirable side) when the midpoint is removed were not retrieved; direction and size UNVERIFIED.
- Kieruj & Moors (2010) specific numerical findings not verified.

## Q4. Implications for ordinal CFA/EFA (polychoric) and for comparing scores when a scale changes from odd to even (equating/linking)

### Takeaway
Direct evidence here is sparse. What exists says: (a) more categories can worsen SEM goodness of fit (Maydeu-Olivares 2009); (b) scores from people who use the midpoint differently are incommensurate (Hernández 2004), which is a measurement-invariance failure; and (c) changing a validated scale's number of options changes its error structure and requires re-validation rather than simple rescaling (Sun et al. 2025). Linear rescaling (e.g., to a common 0-10 metric) gives approximately comparable means in some market-research data (Dawes 2008) but is not a substitute for linking.

### Cited Findings
- Goodness of fit worsened as the number of response alternatives increased (within-subjects, SEM framework including item factor analysis). — [Maydeu-Olivares et al. 2009](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22effect%20of%20varying%20the%20number%20of%20response%20alternatives%20in%20rating%20scales%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- Lozano et al. (2008) simulations: factorial validity improves with more categories up to about 7 (simulated data; relevant to how many categories polychoric/ordinal factor analysis needs). — [Sun et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02846)
- If midpoint responses are partly non-informative, ordinal treatment is no longer justified ("the assumption of ordinality of the item scores can no longer be maintained"); this undermines the threshold model behind polychoric correlations for those items. — [Tijmstra & Bolsinova 2025](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11873858/fullTextXML)
- Total scores from two latent classes differing in midpoint use were incommensurate. — [Hernández et al. 2004](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22functioning%20of%20a%20middle%20category%20by%20means%20of%20a%20mixed-measurement%20model%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- "If researchers do want to change the response scale of a validated measure, they must re-validate the new measure as the measurement error of the scale is likely to change." — [Sun, Schmidt & Henry 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02846)
- Rescaled means and distribution shape approximately comparable across 5/7/10-point formats (with the unverified caveat that 10-point means may be slightly lower). — [Dawes 2008 via search summary](https://measuringu.com/do-people-use-all-response-options/)
- Changing response options significantly altered 25% of item means. — [Nadler et al. 2015 via JMU](https://www.jmu.edu/pair/ads/surveys/continuing-survey/cssmethodschange.pdf)

### Inferences
- When an instrument moves from odd to even (or back), norms and cutoffs should not be carried over by linear rescaling; a common-person or common-item IRT linking study (or at minimum a randomized split-format calibration) is needed, and measurement invariance across formats should be tested with ordinal (threshold) models, where the midpoint threshold will by construction be non-invariant.
- For polychoric-based CFA, a midpoint that behaves as a separate process will tend to appear as threshold misfit or residual correlations (method variance among ambiguous items), and may contribute to the poorer fit seen with more categories.
- Combining datasets collected with and without a midpoint (common in longitudinal or multi-site work) carries a real risk of format-induced mean differences of the size Nadler et al. report (a quarter of items affected).

### Gaps
- No dedicated equating/linking study of the same scale in odd vs even format was found within the tool budget.
- No measurement-invariance (multi-group ordinal CFA) study across 4- vs 5-point or 6- vs 7-point formats was retrieved; this is a clear gap in what I could verify, though such studies may exist (e.g., in cross-cultural or survey-methods literatures).
- Polychoric-specific simulation work on the number of categories (e.g., Rhemtulla, Brosseau-Liard & Savalei 2012, *Psychological Methods* 17(3), 354-373 — that continuous ML is acceptable at ≥5 categories while categorical estimators are preferred below that; citation and finding from memory, UNVERIFIED) was not retrieved.
