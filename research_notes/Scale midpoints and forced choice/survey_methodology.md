## Q1. Classic split-ballot experiments: how many respondents move to the middle when it is offered, and does the substantive balance of opinion change?

### Takeaway
Offering an explicit middle alternative reliably draws a sizeable minority into it. The best-documented estimate is a mean of 14.6% across 19 items (range 6–23%), comparable to Schuman and Presser's experiments. Most of these people come proportionally from both sides, so the *ratio* of pro to con among substantive answers barely moves. Schuman and Presser and Ayidiya and McClendon found little change in that ratio or in correlates. Kalton et al. and Bishop found real but small and inconsistent shifts in the distribution and in correlations with background variables. Volunteered "don't know" responses fall slightly when a midpoint is offered.

### Cited Findings
- **The canonical summary of the 1940s–1980s experiments.** Schuman and Presser (1981, see also Presser & Schuman 1980) tested substantive midpoints: "middle of the road" politically, keeping marijuana laws "the same as now", and U.S. foreign aid being "the right amount". Offering a midpoint increases the proportion in the middle compared with an omitted condition, where interviewers record volunteered middle answers. — [Sturgis, Roberts & Smith 2014, pp. 18–19 [verified secondary summary]](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- **Little change in the substantive balance.** Schuman and Presser found "little evidence that omitting the midpoint affected either the distribution of responses across the directional categories, or correlations between these outcomes and other variables". Sturgis et al. read this as the new middle responses being drawn "with approximately equal probability from across the remaining substantive answer categories". Ayidiya and McClendon (1990) replicated this in a mail self-completion design. — [Sturgis et al. 2014, p. 19](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- **Conflicting replications.** Kalton, Roberts and Holt (1980) and Bishop (1987) found that offering a middle alternative *did* affect the distribution across the remaining categories and altered correlations with background characteristics, "though not in a substantial way, nor in a consistent direction". — [Sturgis et al. 2014, p. 19](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- **Effect on "don't know".** A midpoint and DK trade off against each other. When a midpoint was offered, DK responses decreased, and vice versa (Kalton et al. 1980; Presser & Schuman 1980; Rugg & Cantril 1944). Schuman and Presser saw "a nonsignificant 2 percent difference, on average, between offered and omitted conditions". They described midpoint choosers as including people "who having no opinion on the issue, find it easier to choose a seemingly non-committal position than to say 'don't know'" (Schuman & Presser 1981, p. 71). — [Sturgis et al. 2014, p. 20](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- **Euro-Barometer 38.1 split-ballot (Nov 1992; ~13,000 face-to-face interviews in 12 EC states, 14 samples).** Half the sample (N = 6,511) got a 5-point agree/disagree scale with "neither agree nor disagree"; the other half (N = 6,513) got 4 points. The items were 19 statements about science and technology, with DK accepted but not offered. Results: [O'Muircheartaigh, Krosnick & Helic 2000 working paper [verified]](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf)
  - The offered midpoint attracted **6.0% (Q18) to 22.7% (Q19), mean 14.6%**, "quite comparable" to Schuman and Presser (1981) and Stember and Hyman (1949–50).
  - Volunteered DK averaged **11.4% when the midpoint was omitted vs 9.4% when offered** (F(1,13022) = 32.81, p < .001). Only 2.0 points moved, i.e. **~15% of would-be midpoint choosers** (2.0/14.6) went to DK. The rest picked substantive options.
  - The extreme:moderate split among substantive answers was **42%:58% in both forms** (n.s.), so displaced midpoint choosers did not pile onto the moderate categories.
  - The agree:disagree ratio among substantive answers was **64:36 (offered) vs 62:38 (omitted)**. That is significant but small, and it runs *against* acquiescence. Overall "agree" rose 48.6% → 54.4% (+5.8) and "disagree" rose 27.4% → 34.2% (+6.8) when the midpoint was removed.
- **Krosnick and Presser's review.** "Many experiments document that people often select middle alternatives when they are explicitly offered, whereas few if any volunteer that response when it is omitted (e.g., Schuman & Presser, 1981)." — [O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000, p. 5](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf); see also [Krosnick & Presser 2010, §9.3.2.4](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)
- **Interviewer influence.** Stember and Hyman (1949/1950) found that interviewer opinion influenced answers to dichotomous questions, but the influence disappeared when a middle alternative was offered. — [Krosnick & Presser 2010, p. 273](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)
- **Midpoint use and scale length.** On 3- and 5-point formats about 20% of respondents chose the midpoint, against ~7% on 7- to 19-point formats (Matell & Jacoby 1972). — [Garland 1991, p. 66 [verified secondary]](http://marketing-bulletin.massey.ac.nz/V2/MB_V2_N3_Garland.pdf)

### Inferences
- The split-ballot literature agrees on the *marginal* effect: offering a midpoint moves roughly 10–20% of respondents into it. It does not agree on the *conditional* effects (distribution among the remaining categories, correlates). The disagreement is about small shifts that point in different directions, not large biases.
- Split-ballot data cannot by themselves say which format is more accurate. As Sturgis et al. (pp. 19–20) argue, the same marginal differences fit "forced directional" responding by truly neutral people, satisficing by people with leanings, or any mixture of the two.
- The classic experiments used *substantive* midpoints ("keep things the same", "right amount"). Generalising them to the modern "neither/nor" label is not straightforward (see Q3).

### Gaps
- I could not open Presser and Schuman (1980), Kalton et al. (1980), Bishop (1987) or Ayidiya and McClendon (1990). Their per-item percentage shifts and sample sizes are therefore not reported here. One search snippet claimed offering a midpoint raises middle responses "by between 11 to 49%" relative to volunteered middles. I could not trace it to a primary source and it is **unverified**.
- Bishop (1987) is often cited for midpoint effects being larger when the midpoint is mentioned last or emphasised in the question stem. I could not verify this from the text.

## Q2. Krosnick's satisficing account and reliability/validity with vs without midpoints

### Takeaway
Satisficing theory (Krosnick 1991) predicts that an explicit midpoint or DK is an "easy out", so omitting it should push respondents to retrieve and report real opinions. For **DK options** the evidence broadly supports that prediction. For **midpoints** it largely does not: midpoint choice is not concentrated among low-ability respondents. The flagship experiment (O'Muircheartaigh et al.) found that removing the midpoint *increased random error* and reduced item loadings, so Krosnick and Presser (2010) recommend offering midpoints and favour about 7-point bipolar scales. The large reliability meta-analyses (Alwin & Krosnick 1991; Alwin 2007) and the MTMM meta-analyses (Saris & Gallhofer 2007) partly disagree: 3-point scales are least reliable, and Saris and Gallhofer report that omitting the neutral category *improves* quality. The literature is genuinely contested.

### Cited Findings
- **The satisficing rationale.** "Offering a midpoint on a scale may constitute a cue encouraging satisficing to people low in ability and/or motivation, especially if its meaning is clearly either 'neutral/no preference' or 'status quo'." The cost of removing it: genuinely neutral people would be forced "to pick a point either on the positive side or on the negative side of the scale, resulting in inaccurate measurement". — [Krosnick & Presser 2010, §9.3.1.4](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)
- **Mixed evidence that midpoint selection is satisficing.** Attraction to middle alternatives was *unrelated to education* in Kalton et al. (1980), Schuman and Presser (1981), O'Muircheartaigh et al. (1999) and Narayan and Krosnick (1996). Krosnick and Schuman (1988) and Bishop (1990) found *more* attraction among people for whom the issue was less important or attitudes less intense, and O'Muircheartaigh et al. found more among those with less interest in the topic. Stember and Hyman found no relation with interest, and O'Muircheartaigh et al. found no relation with knowledge. The authors' verdict: "the evidence on the connection between middle alternatives and satisficing is mixed." — [Krosnick & Presser 2010, §9.3.2.4](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)
- **Opposite to the satisficing prediction.** Narayan and Krosnick (1996) found offering a middle alternative had the most impact on people *highest* in education. Krosnick, Narayan and Smith (1997) found the same with direct measures of cognitive skill. — [O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000, p. 5](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf)
- **O'Muircheartaigh, Krosnick & Helic: reliability and validity results [verified]** — [working paper](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf); [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/har/wpaper/0103.html)
  - Naive analyses showed no difference. Cronbach's alpha was **.64 (offered) vs .65 (omitted)** (n.s.). Predictive validity (the effect of respect for scientists on the attitude index) was **.17 vs .21** (z = 1.25, n.s.).
  - Acquiescence was present equally in both forms: loadings on the acquiescence factor were **b = .33 vs .32** (χ²(1) = 3.03, n.s.). **Offering a midpoint did not reduce acquiescence.**
  - The SEM controlled for acquiescence. Factor loadings differed between forms (χ²(19) = 2,554.81), and **14 of 18** free loadings were stronger when the midpoint was offered. Error variances also differed (χ²(19) = 94.78): **15 of 19** were larger without the midpoint, and all **9** individually significant differences went that way. Conclusion: omitting the midpoint "significantly decreased the validity of responses and increased the amount of random error variance", suggesting "people who genuinely belonged in the middle of the scale made essentially random choices".
  - **Internal inconsistency to flag.** The abstract says offering a midpoint increases reliability "while not affecting the validity". The discussion section says omission "significantly decreased the validity". Krosnick and Presser (2010) summarise it as improving "the reliability and validity of ratings". Cite the reliability result with confidence. The validity result depends on whether stronger loadings count as validity (the authors' discussion) or criterion correlations do (the abstract, where there was no difference).
  - DK and midpoint responses have different predictors. Knowledge, interest, exposure, education, age and gender explained **25%** of variance in DK frequency but only **2%** in midpoint frequency, and some predictors reversed sign. "'Don't know' and middle alternative responses are not manifestations of the same underlying cognitive state."
- **Krosnick and Presser's bottom line.** O'Muircheartaigh et al. showed that "omitting the middle alternative led respondents to randomly select one of the moderate scale points closest to where a midpoint would appear. This suggests that offering midpoints is desirable." Overall, "7-point scales are probably optimal in many instances", mostly on evidence from visual administration. — [Krosnick & Presser 2010, §9.3.2](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)
- **Meta-analytic reliability studies, which partly conflict.** Alwin and Krosnick (1991) found "3-point scales had the lowest reliability, ... no difference in the reliabilities of 2-, 4-, 5-, and 7-point scales, and ... 9-point scales to have maximum reliability". Andrews (1984) found validity and reliability worst for 3-point scales and improving from 5 to 19 points. Scherpenzeel (1995) found the highest reliability at 4–5 points. Krosnick and Presser discount these meta-analyses because number of scale points was "strongly confound[ed]" with other item characteristics. — [Krosnick & Presser 2010, fn. 3](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)
- **Alwin (2002, 2007), Margins of Error.** "Compared to 2-point scales, 3-point scales have significantly poorer reliability", which Alwin attributes to "uncertainty induced by the introduction of a potentially ambiguous midpoint". 5-point scales were about as reliable as 4-point ones, and 7-point scales had "the lowest reliability of all". — [Sturgis et al. 2014, p. 20 [secondary]](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf). Maitland (2009) summarises Alwin (2007) similarly: 2-point scales most reliable, then 4, 5 and 9; "three category scales are less reliable than two or four category scales"; 4- and 5-point unipolar scales were superior. — [Maitland 2009, Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/2950-how-many-scale-points-should-i-include-for-attitudinal-questions)
- **DeCastellarnau's tally of the "neutral alternative" design choice.** Alwin and Krosnick (1991): "Midpoints lower reliability, more valuable in 7 point scales". Andrews (1984): midpoint had only a slight effect. Malhotra et al. (2009): "midpoint reduces validity" (criterion validity). Saris and Gallhofer (2007): "not providing a neutral category improves significantly both reliability and validity" (true-score MTMM). Scherpenzeel and Saris (1997): explicit midpoint has "no effect on reliability but a higher validity". Weijters et al. (2010): midpoint "increases acquiescence and lowers extreme responses". DeCastellarnau classes the neutral alternative as a characteristic with a demonstrated impact on data quality, but the direction differs across studies. — [DeCastellarnau 2018, Table 3 & text [verified]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5993837/)
- **Malhotra, Krosnick and Thomas (2009), branching.** Branching midpoint respondents into leaners gave "no significant gains in criterion validity". Pooling midpoint leaners with weak end-point respondents compromised validity, and the authors concluded "respondents who placed themselves at the midpoint belonged there" (p. 318). Sturgis et al. note this tests only satisficing, not hidden DKs. — [Sturgis et al. 2014, p. 21](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf). **Conflict:** DeCastellarnau summarises Malhotra et al. as "midpoint reduces validity", which reads differently from Sturgis et al.'s quotation. I did not see the original, so the exact claim is unresolved.
- **Revilla, Saris & Krosnick (2014), ESS Round 3, four split-ballot MTMM experiments, 23 countries [verified from RECSM WP 5 version].** For agree/disagree scales, mean quality was **.533 (5-point), .394 (7-point) and .383 (11-point)**. Mean reliability was .717, .716 and .709, and mean validity .753, .555 and .531. Going from 5 to 7 points lowered quality by 0.139 (p < .001), mostly through validity (−0.198), with no significant change in reliability. In 82% of country-by-experiment comparisons, quality fell as categories increased. Recommendation: use 5 categories for agree/disagree scales, not 7 or 11. **Confound:** the 5-point scales were fully labelled (with "Neither agree nor disagree") and vertical, while the 7- and 11-point scales were end-labelled and horizontal, so the result is about the whole format, not the midpoint itself. — [Revilla, Saris & Krosnick, RECSM Working Paper 5 (2009)](https://www.upf.edu/documents/3966940/3986764/RECSM_wp005.pdf); [published SMR 43(1):73–97](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/somere/v43y2014i1p73-97.html)
- **Labelling interacts with this.** Saris and Gallhofer's (2007) MTMM meta-analysis estimates that full labelling raises the reliability coefficient by 0.033 and lowers the validity coefficient by 0.0045. Alwin and Krosnick (1991, Table 4, p. 167) found fully labelled 7-point scales more reliable than end-labelled ones. — [Revilla et al. RECSM WP5](https://www.upf.edu/documents/3966940/3986764/RECSM_wp005.pdf)
- **Krosnick and Fabrigar (1997)** concluded that "evidence on validity is mixed, and the theory of satisficing suggests that including midpoints may decrease measurement quality" (p. 148). They also found a curvilinear pattern with 5–7-point scales most reliable. — [Wetzelhütter 2020 [secondary quotation]](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484); [Maitland 2009](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/2950-how-many-scale-points-should-i-include-for-attitudinal-questions)

### Inferences
- There is a real split between **experimental within-item** evidence (O'Muircheartaigh et al.: offering a midpoint lowers random error) and **meta-analytic cross-item** evidence (Alwin; Saris & Gallhofer: 3-point scales and/or neutral categories are associated with lower quality). Krosnick's camp discounts the meta-analyses for confounding. The SQP/MTMM camp has more items and countries but less clean manipulation of the midpoint itself.
- Krosnick's own position appears to have shifted. Krosnick and Fabrigar (1997) leaned toward caution about midpoints. Krosnick and Presser (2010) recommend offering them on the strength of the Euro-Barometer experiment.
- Satisficing explains DK selection better than midpoint selection. Midpoint choice is not consistently linked to low education or ability (Q2) and has different predictors from DK.

### Gaps
- I could not read Krosnick (1991) itself for its exact claims about the midpoint ("weak satisficing"). Its listing of the midpoint as a satisficing strategy comes via Krosnick and Presser (2010) and O'Muircheartaigh et al. (2000).
- I could not verify the size of the Saris and Gallhofer (2007/2014) SQP coefficient for "neutral category present". Only its direction comes via DeCastellarnau (2018).
- I could not verify reliability numbers by scale length from Alwin (2007) or Alwin and Krosnick (1991). Only the rank orderings are verified, and only via secondary summaries.

## Q3. The midpoint as a hidden "don't know"

### Takeaway
With "neither X nor Y" labels the midpoint is semantically compatible with *having no opinion*, and direct probing shows it is used that way. Sturgis et al. (2014) found that only about 13–25% of neither/nor choosers on low-salience British issues held a genuinely neutral view. After reallocation, three-quarters to nine-tenths were "face-saving don't knows". Recoding them changed regression conclusions. Offering an explicit DK did not reduce this. Raaijmakers et al. (2000) found the same among adolescents. Against this, O'Muircheartaigh et al. found midpoint and DK responses had different predictors in a higher-salience Euro-Barometer battery. The mix of neutral and no-opinion responses at the midpoint appears to depend on topic salience.

### Cited Findings
- **Design.** Ipsos-MORI General Public Omnibus, Great Britain, Apr–May 2008, **n = 3,113**. It used face-to-face show cards, with quota selection within 210 randomly sampled constituencies (not a probability sample). Respondents were randomised to one of three 5-point items: satisfaction with the European Commission (n = 1,029), importance of Britain leading in nanotechnology, and "advantages of GM foods outweigh any dangers" (agree/disagree). DK was not on the card but was recorded if volunteered. Neither/nor choosers were asked whether they had (a) no real opinion, (b) an opinion right in the middle, or (c) neither, with open-text follow-up for (c). — [Sturgis et al. 2014, pp. 22–24 [verified]](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- **Marginals.** Only **41–58%** gave substantive (non-midpoint, non-DK) answers across the three items. EC item: neither/nor **30.3%**, volunteered DK **28.6%**. — [Sturgis et al. 2014, Table 1](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- **Headline result.** "Only **16 percent, 13 percent, and 25 percent** of initial midpoints on the 3 items, respectively, are maintained to be truly 'neutral'". After coding the open-ended "neither of the above" answers, "**between three quarters and nine-tenths** of initial 'neither/nor' responses now turn out to conceal nonattitudes, while only around **one or two in ten** are genuinely neutral opinions." On the GM item, 74% of open-ended explanations were coded as no opinion (e.g. "I don't know", "insufficient information") and 11% as neutral. — [Sturgis et al. 2014, pp. 26–27](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf). The text lists items in the order EC, nanotechnology, GM, but the figure is hard to read in the extracted PDF, so the exact item-to-percentage mapping is unverified.
- **Multivariate consequence.** In an ordered logit of EC satisfaction, the sample fell from n = 735 before recoding to n = 479 after hidden DKs were moved to DK. Male gender and political interest were significant predictors before recoding and non-significant after, and several point estimates changed substantially, not only the standard errors. — [Sturgis et al. 2014, Table 2, p. 28](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- **Against satisficing: hidden DKs come from the interested.** Hidden DK respondents were *more* interested in politics or science than those who openly volunteered DK. EC: χ²(4) = 18, p = .001. GM: χ²(4) = 26, p < .001. Nanotech: χ²(4) = 8.2, p = .086. The authors read this as social desirability (self-presentation): "the decision to select the midpoint rather than admit ignorance is reflective of a social desirability bias". — [Sturgis et al. 2014, Table 3, p. 30](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- **An explicit DK option does not fix it.** In a fresh omnibus split (EC n = 559/490; GM n = 504/531), putting DK on the show card raised DK by ~7 points on the EC item. It had **no effect on the neither/nor rate**, before or after reallocation. The extra DKs came from the substantive categories, which Sturgis et al. read as possible satisficing induced by the DK option. The GM item showed no change in neither/nor either. — [Sturgis et al. 2014, Tables 4–5, p. 32](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- **Hidden DKs as the main threat to validity.** Sturgis et al. argue that, for many neither/nor scales, the hidden DK and not satisficing is "the primary threat to validity". It inflates apparent opinionation and violates the ordinality assumption analysts invoke for bipolar scales. — [Sturgis et al. 2014, pp. 17–18](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf); [LSE repository record](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/101999)
- **Raaijmakers et al. (2000).** A longitudinal panel of Dutch youths aged 12–17 answered 5-point Likert political-attitude items. Younger adolescents used non-substantive (midpoint) responses more, in line with lower political knowledge. Midpoint responses were involved in the vast majority of observed opinion *changes* over time, and often followed earlier DK responses. The authors conclude the midpoint was used in the sense of "undecided" and argue for treating it as possibly missing rather than neutral. — [Utrecht repository record [abstract only, secondary]](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/29581)
- **Contrary evidence.** In the Euro-Barometer split, removing the midpoint sent only ~15% of would-be midpoint choosers to volunteered DK. Predictors explained 25% of DK variance but 2% of midpoint variance, and age and TV-channel effects had opposite signs. The authors conclude DK and midpoint "are not manifestations of the same underlying cognitive state". — [O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf)
- **Mixed motives at the midpoint (2020 web survey, n = 1,282 students).** Wetzelhütter crossed midpoint and DK offering. Respondents who choose DK "may not have a neutral opinion", and those who choose the midpoint "may not necessarily be undecided". — [Wetzelhütter 2020, Survey Practice](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484)
- **Midpoint meanings catalogued.** Kulas and Stachowski (2009) found midpoints are used when respondents are undecided, misunderstand the item, give a conditional ("it depends") response, or hold a neutral opinion. Tourangeau et al. (2004) found the midpoint can be read as "most typical" and used as a reference point. — [DeCastellarnau 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5993837/)
- **Reasons for choosing DK besides lacking knowledge.** Krosnick and Fabrigar (1997) list ambivalence when no midpoint is offered, neutrality when no neutral response is offered, not understanding the question, uncertainty about scale-point meaning, and satisficing. — [Wetzelhütter 2020, quoting Krosnick & Fabrigar 1997](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484). Krosnick and Presser also describe "second-stage" DKs that reflect **ambivalence**: people who know a lot but have contradictory thoughts and feelings. — [Krosnick & Presser 2010, "don't know" filters section](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)
- **The SQP/MTMM line.** Saris and Gallhofer (2007) found that omitting the neutral category significantly improves reliability and validity, while Scherpenzeel and Saris (1997) found no reliability effect and higher validity with an explicit midpoint. — [DeCastellarnau 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5993837/). See Q2 for Revilla et al. (2014).

### Inferences
- The two bodies of evidence can be reconciled by topic salience and label type. Sturgis et al. deliberately chose **low-salience** issues (EC, nanotech, GM in 2008), where 29–30% volunteered DK. The Euro-Barometer science battery had lower DK rates (~9–11%). Hidden DKs should be most common when the topic is obscure *and* the midpoint is labelled "neither/nor".
- For bipolar items analysed as ordinal or interval, the midpoint is a mixture of neutral, ambivalent, uncertain and no-opinion respondents. Treating it as the scale centre is an assumption that should be checked by probing, branching or a separate DK option, not taken as given.
- Offering an explicit DK is not a remedy for hidden DKs in the midpoint (Sturgis et al.). The two need separate design solutions, such as a follow-up probe for midpoint choosers.

### Gaps
- I found no study that pins down the share of neutral vs hidden-DK midpoint responses for **high-salience** or personally relevant topics using the Sturgis-style probe. The generality of the 75–90% figure is unknown.
- I could not obtain Raaijmakers et al. (2000) full text (the repository link returned HTML), so its effect sizes are not reported.
- I could not open Moors (2008, *Quality & Quantity*, "Exploring the effect of a middle response category on response style in attitude measurement"), which is relevant to midpoint and response-style effects.

## Q4. Sensitive questions and social desirability: escape hatch, or does forcing a choice push people to the desirable pole?

### Takeaway
There is evidence both ways. In Garland (1991) and Johns (2005), the midpoint served as a **safe haven for socially undesirable views**: removing it shifted answers *toward the less desirable pole*. That implies forcing a choice can *reduce* social-desirability bias on controversial items. Sturgis et al. (2014) show a related self-presentation motive: people choose neither/nor to avoid *admitting ignorance*. Older work (Worcester & Burns 1975) found that removing the midpoint pushed respondents toward the *positive* pole. The direction appears to be content-specific.

### Cited Findings
- **Garland (1991).** Palmerston North (NZ) household omnibus, 1990, grocery shoppers rating the importance of product labelling. 5-point version **n = 225**, 4-point version **n = 223**, weighted by age and sex. 5-point: very important 28%, important 41%, neither 14%, unimportant 8%, not at all important 8%, DK 1%. 4-point: 33%, 38%, —, **16%**, 10%, 3%. "Unimportant" rose from 8% to 16% (p < .05). Garland's interpretation: "some respondents were reluctant to state that product labelling is unimportant to them and have taken the 'neither ... nor' position". Removing the midpoint helped "alleviate this social desirability bias without changing the direction of opinion" but altered intensity. He notes the result is the *opposite* of Worcester and Burns (1975), who found that a 4-point scale without a midpoint pushes respondents toward the *positive* end, so the effect "is content specific". — [Garland 1991 [verified]](http://marketing-bulletin.massey.ac.nz/V2/MB_V2_N3_Garland.pdf)
- **Johns (2005), British Election Study attitude items.** Omitting the midpoint may impair validity because some respondents choose it "when they have no basis for choosing between agreement and disagreement". It may also improve validity because the midpoint is "used as a safe haven by a 'silent minority', taking refuge in that option rather than confessing to an unpopular viewpoint". Recommendation: offer the midpoint on **obscure** topics and omit it on **controversial** topics where social desirability is uppermost. — [Johns 2005, abstract via Strathclyde repository/T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13689880500178849); [Strathprints record](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/3983/) [abstract only; effect sizes not verified]
- **Avoiding admission of ignorance.** Hidden DKs were concentrated among the more interested. This is "a social desirability bias ... found most often among individuals who believe they should have an opinion on matters of public interest", in line with over-reporting of voting and attitudes on fictitious issues among the politically interested. — [Sturgis et al. 2014, pp. 18, 22, 30](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- **DK options as cover on sensitive items.** "It is better not to provide explicit DK options for sensitive items, as they are more apt to provide a cover for socially undesirable responses." Many studies found that frequent DK givers "would have provided socially undesirable responses" (Cronbach 1950; Fonda 1951; Johanson, Gips & Rich 1993; Kahn & Hadley 1949; Rosenberg, Izard & Hollander 1955). — [Krosnick & Presser 2010](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf). DeCastellarnau lists Krosnick et al. (2005): "DK provision encourages respondents to not provide undesirable or unflattering opinions". — [DeCastellarnau 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5993837/)
- **Weak acquiescence effect.** The Euro-Barometer data gave no evidence that removing the midpoint caused would-be midpoint choosers to *agree* (acquiesce). They leaned slightly more toward *disagree* (+6.8 vs +5.8 points). — [O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf). In contrast, Weijters et al. (2010) found that including a midpoint *increases* acquiescence and lowers extreme responding. — [DeCastellarnau 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5993837/)
- **Survey-practice summary.** Menold and Bogner (2016, GESIS guidelines): "most researchers recommend that a middle alternative should be offered in order to prevent respondents who have a moderate or neutral opinion from having to use an alternative category, thereby systematically distorting the data". — [Wetzelhütter 2020 (quoting Menold & Bogner 2016)](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484)

### Inferences
- The social-desirability evidence is thin: one small NZ omnibus (Garland, N ≈ 450), one BES-based analysis (Johns), and indirect inference (Sturgis). It supports a conditional rule, as Johns proposes: on sensitive or controversial items the midpoint can absorb undesirable answers, so removing it can *reveal* them. On obscure items removal creates forced, possibly random, directional answers.
- The direction of the displacement when a midpoint is removed is item-specific: negative in Garland, positive in Worcester and Burns, slightly toward disagree in the Euro-Barometer. No general "forced choice pushes people toward the desirable pole" law is supported.

### Gaps
- I could not verify Johns (2005) effect sizes or sample details; only the abstract was available.
- I found no randomised study that crosses midpoint presence with a validated sensitive-behaviour criterion (record-check validation). Claims about which format is "more truthful" on sensitive items therefore rest on distributional shifts, not criterion validity.

## Q5. Offering an explicit "don't know"/no-opinion option separately from the midpoint

### Takeaway
Explicit DK filters greatly increase DK rates. Pooled across the studies reviewed, they do **not** improve reliability or validity, and they attract mainly lower-education, lower-effort respondents, consistent with satisficing. Krosnick et al. (2002) found no loss of over-time consistency or price sensitivity when the DK option was omitted. Krosnick and Presser and the SQP/MTMM literature recommend against explicit DK for most attitude items, while a minority of studies (Andrews 1984; De Leeuw et al. 2016) find quality effects. Sturgis et al. show that an explicit DK does not pull hidden DKs out of a neither/nor midpoint.

### Cited Findings
- **Krosnick et al. (2002), nine experiments in three household surveys [verified].** — [Krosnick et al. 2002, POQ 66:371–403](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2002/2002%20The%20impact%20of%20no%20opinion%20response%20options%20on%20data%20quality-%20N.pdf); [JPSM record](https://jpsm.umd.edu/publication/impact-no-opinion-response-options-data-quality-non-attitude-reduction-or-invitation)
  - **Study 1** (1989 NES Pilot; panel drawn from 1,640 respondents to the 1988 NES; 7-point items). No-opinion rates were offered vs omitted: party ID **12.5% vs 1.0%**, defense spending **12.7% vs 1.7%**, Central America **11.3% vs 2.4%**, gun control **6.1% vs 1.7%**. Over-time consistency was no higher when the DK option was offered.
  - **Study 2** (NORC face-to-face Exxon Valdez contingent-valuation referendum, **N = 1,182**, 1993, RR 73%). Abstention was **4.7% (unfiltered) vs 17.2% (filtered)**, +12.5 points. The effect was +20.0 points among the least educated and +4.4 among the most educated. The not-vote option attracted 11.0% of oral voters and 14.0% of secret-ballot voters. Sensitivity of votes to price was **not** reduced when DK was omitted. Offering the not-vote option and secret voting both "compromised response quality individually and interactively".
  - **Study 3** (Ohio State CATI, N = 300 national + 217 Franklin County; national RR 21.2%). DK attraction was greater for questions later in the interview and among respondents who reported less effort.
  - Conclusion: "inclusion of no-opinion options ... may not enhance data quality and instead may preclude measurement of some meaningful opinions."
- **Other DK evidence summarised by Krosnick and Presser (2010).** — [Krosnick & Presser 2010](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)
  - Gilljam and Granberg (1993), nuclear power: **15%** chose an offered DK, but only **3% and 4%** gave no substantive answer to two later unfiltered questions. Those later answers correlated with each other and predicted the referendum vote months later.
  - Bishop, Oldendick, Tuchfarber and Bennett (1979) found slightly stronger criterion associations with DK filters. Schuman and Presser (1981) "rarely found such differences". Alwin and Krosnick (1991), McClendon and Alwin (1993), Krosnick and Berent (1993), Krosnick et al. (2002) and Poe et al. (1988) found answers no more reliable with DK filters.
  - Visser, Krosnick, Marquette and Curtin (2000): pre-election polls predicted outcomes better when initial DKs were pressed for a leaning.
  - Hippler and Schwarz (1989): strongly worded filters ("or haven't you thought enough about this issue to have an opinion?") signal that much knowledge is required and intimidate respondents.
  - DK filters do encourage people without information to admit it (Schuman & Presser 1981), but "there is considerable evidence that DK filters do not improve measurement".
- **DeCastellarnau's tally on the DK option.** Alwin (2007): explicit DK has reliability comparable to none. Andrews (1984): explicit DK gives higher quality. De Leeuw et al. (2016): explicit DK increases missing data and lowers reliability, while an implicit DK lowers missing data and raises reliability. McClendon (1991): explicit DK does not reduce acquiescence or recency effects. McClendon and Alwin (1993): no reliability benefit. Rodgers et al. (1992): lower validities with explicit DK. Saris and Gallhofer (2007): no significant effect on quality. Scherpenzeel and Saris (1997): no effect. Saris and Gallhofer (2014) recommend an implicit (recorded, not offered) DK. Classification: whether an explicit vs implicit DK affects quality "is not clear". — [DeCastellarnau 2018 [verified]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5993837/)
- **DK and midpoint are not substitutes.** An explicit DK raised DK by ~7 points on the EC item but did not lower neither/nor responding (Sturgis et al. 2014). Midpoint and DK had largely different predictors in the Euro-Barometer study (R² 2% vs 25%; O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000). — [Sturgis et al. 2014](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf); [O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf)
- **Conditional recommendation (Menold & Bogner 2016, GESIS).** Omit DK when respondents are familiar with the topic; provide it when they are unfamiliar. Provide a midpoint when it is conceptually required; omit it when it is not. — [Wetzelhütter 2020, Fig. 1](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484)

### Inferences
- The DK evidence and the midpoint evidence point in different directions. For DK, the satisficing account is fairly well supported: attraction is concentrated among low-education, low-effort, late-in-interview and self-administered or secret answering. For the midpoint it is not, and the midpoint's main contamination appears to be hidden no-opinion (Sturgis) rather than effort-saving.
- A defensible design reading of this literature, inferred rather than any single source's recommendation: offer a labelled midpoint on bipolar items; do not offer an explicit DK on attitude items for which most respondents should have a view; record a volunteered or implicit DK; and, where the topic may be unfamiliar, probe or branch midpoint choosers to separate neutral from no-opinion responses.

### Gaps
- Almost all of this evidence comes from interviewer-administered general-population surveys of political and policy attitudes. I found little on self-administered web or app surveys about **personal experience** ratings, such as feelings and states. There the "no opinion" construct differs: people always have *some* state, so a midpoint more plausibly means "moderate", "mixed" or "unsure about my own state" than "uninformed". The generalisation should be flagged as untested.
- I could not retrieve per-item over-time consistency coefficients from Krosnick et al. (2002), Table 2. Only the qualitative conclusion and the abstention rates are verified.
