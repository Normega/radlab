# Odd (midpoint) vs even (forced-choice) rating scales, and comparability when the number of points changes

Scope note: these notes cover (1) textbook and methods-guide recommendations, (2) what widely used instruments do, (3) whether a consensus or synthesis exists, and (4) comparability evidence for the same items given with different numbers of points or with and without a midpoint. The lab's case, the same 32 items on a 6-point scale in one study and a 7-point scale with a midpoint in another, is the applied target throughout. Empirical findings and textbook opinion are labelled as such. Anything I could not verify against a primary or near-primary source during this session is listed under Gaps rather than Cited Findings.

## 1. What textbooks and methods guides recommend, and why

### Takeaway
The survey-methods literature (Krosnick & Presser; Boateng et al. 2018, which relies on them) recommends 5 points for unipolar dimensions and 7 for bipolar ones, labelling every point with words, and on balance offering a midpoint on bipolar scales. That last recommendation rests mainly on one unpublished conference paper (O'Muircheartaigh, Krosnick & Helic). The scale-development textbook tradition (DeVellis) treats odd versus even as a design choice with trade-offs, not a rule. No source found prescribes even scales as best practice, but personality-psychometric evidence (Simms et al. 2019) finds 6 points as good as anything longer.

### Cited Findings
**Survey-methods handbooks (Krosnick tradition)**
- Krosnick & Presser, "Question and Questionnaire Design," in J. D. Wright & P. V. Marsden (Eds.), *Handbook of Survey Research* (2nd ed.), Emerald/Elsevier, 2010. A pre-print dated 15 Feb 2009 was read in full for these notes. The chapter sets out the theoretical case on both sides:
  - *Against a midpoint*: "Offering a midpoint on a scale may constitute a cue encouraging satisficing to people low in ability and/or motivation, especially if its meaning is clearly either 'neutral/no preference' or 'status quo'."
  - *For a midpoint*: "Some people may truly belong at the scale midpoint… If many people have neutral attitudes to report, eliminating the midpoint will force them to pick a point either on the positive side or on the negative side of the scale, resulting in inaccurate measurement."
  - [Krosnick & Presser 2009 preprint PDF](https://benjaminhirt.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2024/01/2009_handbook_krosnick-1.pdf)
- Same chapter, empirical review. Whether respondents are drawn to middle alternatives under conditions that encourage satisficing is "mixed": attraction to the midpoint was unrelated to education, but greater among people with weaker attitudes or less interest in the topic. "More importantly, O'Muircheartaigh and colleagues (1999) found that adding midpoints to rating scales improved the reliability and validity of ratings. Structural equation modeling of error structures revealed that omitting the middle alternative led respondents to randomly select one of the moderate scale points closest to where a midpoint would appear. This suggests that offering midpoints is desirable." — [Krosnick & Presser 2009 preprint](https://benjaminhirt.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2024/01/2009_handbook_krosnick-1.pdf)
- Same chapter: "Overall, our review suggests that 7-point scales are probably optimal in many instances." Reliability is higher when every point carries a verbal label, and labels should divide the continuum into roughly equal units (Krosnick & Berent 1993; Klockars & Yamagishi 1988). — [Krosnick & Presser 2009 preprint](https://benjaminhirt.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2024/01/2009_handbook_krosnick-1.pdf)
- Same chapter, footnote 3: meta-analyses of scale length drawn from pre-existing surveys (Andrews 1984; Alwin & Krosnick 1991; Alwin 1992, 1997; Scherpenzeel 1995) "yielded inconsistent findings" because they mixed subjective and objective items and confounded number of points with other item features. Andrews (1984) found 3-point scales worst. Alwin & Krosnick (1991) found 3-point scales had the lowest reliability. — [Krosnick & Presser 2009 preprint](https://benjaminhirt.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2024/01/2009_handbook_krosnick-1.pdf)
- Source quality: the O'Muircheartaigh, Krosnick & Helic (1999) study behind the "offer midpoints" advice is cited in the chapter as a conference paper: "Middle alternatives, acquiescence, and the quality of questionnaire data," AAPOR Annual Meeting, St. Petersburg FL, May 1999. It later appeared as a Harris School (U. Chicago) working paper, no. 01.03. A summary of that working paper's abstract says offering a middle alternative "reduces the amount of random measurement error… thereby increasing reliability, while not affecting the validity." That is slightly weaker than the handbook's "improved the reliability and validity." — [IDEAS/RePEc working-paper record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/har/wpaper/0103.html); [Krosnick & Presser 2009](https://benjaminhirt.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2024/01/2009_handbook_krosnick-1.pdf). **Conflict: the two accounts disagree on validity.**
- Pasek & Krosnick, "Optimizing survey questionnaire design in political science," in J. E. Leighley (Ed.), *Oxford Handbook of American Elections and Political Behavior* (2010), ch. 3. Same rule in bullet form: "Ratings tend to be more reliable and valid when five points are offered for unipolar dimensions (e.g., 'not at all important' to 'extremely important'; Lissitz and Green 1975) and seven points for bipolar dimensions (e.g., 'Dislike a great deal' to 'like a great deal'; Green and Rao 1970)." It also says to put verbal labels on all points, and that numeric labels added to verbal ones "do not increase data quality and… can mislead respondents" (Schwarz et al. 1991). — [Pasek & Krosnick chapter PDF (Stanford)](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2009/2009%2003-Leighly-Chapter03.pdf)

**Scale-development primers and textbooks**
- Boateng, G. O., Neilands, T. B., Frongillo, E. A., Melgar-Quiñonez, H. R., & Young, S. L. (2018). Best practices for developing and validating scales for health, social, and behavioral research: A primer. *Frontiers in Public Health, 6*, 149. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2018.00149
  - On number of points they defer to Krosnick & Presser: 2–3-point responses have lower reliability than 5–7-point Likert-type scales, "the gain levels off after seven points," and they recommend "five points… for unipolar items" and seven for bipolar items.
  - They also note that items with 5–7 categories and no strong floor or ceiling effects can be treated as continuous in CFA, while fewer than 5 call for categorical estimators.
  - I found no midpoint-specific recommendation beyond inheriting the 5/7 rule. The first ~100k characters of the full text were reviewed.
  - [Boateng et al. 2018, Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2018.00149/full)
- DeVellis, R. F. (and DeVellis & Thorpe, 2022, *Scale Development: Theory and Applications*, 5th ed., SAGE) frames odd versus even as a trade-off. An odd number allows equivocation ("neither agree nor disagree") or uncertainty and implies a central neutral point; an even number makes respondents commit, if only weakly. Neither is presented as inherently better. **Secondary source**: this comes from a search-engine summary of the book and a McGill blog summary of the 1991 edition, not a page-cited quote from the 2022 edition. — [McGill CRCF summary of DeVellis 1991](https://blogs.mcgill.ca/crcfbrownbag/2014/05/10/devellis-r-1991-scale-development-theory-and-applications/); [DeVellis & Thorpe 2022 review listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/357803002_Robert_F_DeVellis_Carolyn_T_Thorpe_2022_Scale_development_Theory_and_applications_California_SAGE_Publications_Inc_320_pages_6000_softcover)

**Empirical studies used as guidance (not consensus statements)**
- Lozano, L. M., García-Cueto, E., & Muñiz, J. (2008). Effect of the number of response categories on the reliability and validity of rating scales. *Methodology, 4*(2), 73–79. https://doi.org/10.1027/1614-2241.4.2.73. A simulation study. It concludes items need at least 4 categories, and "from seven categories onwards the gains are scarce," so it recommends 4–7. It does not address midpoints as such. — [Univ. Oviedo repository record](https://portalinvestigacion.uniovi.es/documentos/5e78cab32999521b3d11f205?lang=en)
- Simms, L. J., Zelazny, K., Williams, T. F., & Bernstein, L. (2019). Does the number of response options matter? Psychometric perspectives using personality questionnaire data. *Psychological Assessment, 31*(4), 557–566. https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000648
  - Design: N = 1,358 undergraduates randomly assigned to complete the same personality measure (the BFI, per the press release) on 2 to 11 options or a visual analogue scale. The study explicitly asked whether an even or odd number is better.
  - Results: precision was attenuated for 2–5 options; criterion validity "did not follow this pattern"; there were "no psychometric advantages… for any response scales beyond 6 options, including visual analogs."
  - [Univ. at Buffalo research record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/does-the-number-of-response-options-matter-psychometric-perspecti/)
- The Simms press release puts the even-number case in opinion form: an even number "eliminat[es] a middle choice that often sits like an island of apathy," and "six appears to be the magic number." **This is author commentary in a press release, not a stated result of the paper.** — [UB news release, Apr 2019](https://www.buffalo.edu/news/releases/2019/04/035.html)
- Sturgis, P., Roberts, C., & Smith, P. (2014). Middle alternatives revisited: How the neither/nor response acts as a way of saying "I don't know"? *Sociological Methods & Research, 43*(1), 15–38. Follow-up probes of people who chose the midpoint found "the vast majority" were face-saving "don't knows" rather than truly neutral, and reallocating them changed substantive inferences. — [LSE Research Online record](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/101999); [SAGE study PDF](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)
- Revilla, M. A., Saris, W. E., & Krosnick, J. A. (2014). Choosing the number of categories in agree–disagree scales. *Sociological Methods & Research, 43*(1), 73–97. Four multitrait–multimethod (MTMM) experiments in European Social Survey Round 3 found that for agree–disagree scales, 5 categories gave higher-quality data than 7 or 11. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/somere/v43y2014i1p73-97.html); [RECSM working paper PDF](https://www.upf.edu/documents/3966940/3986764/RECSM_wp005.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Odd versus even" is less a single question than three:
  - Is the dimension **bipolar** or **unipolar**?
  - Is the midpoint a **true neutral** or a **hidden don't-know**?
  - Does omitting it **push respondents to an adjacent point at random** (O'Muircheartaigh et al.) or to a **systematic side** (social desirability)?

  The literature's answer depends on which of these dominates for the items in question.
- Revilla et al. (2014) and the Krosnick & Presser rule partly pull against each other. "Seven points for bipolar" comes from construct-specific bipolar scales; for agree–disagree items specifically, the MTMM evidence favours 5. So "7 with a midpoint" is not automatically the methods-literature default for an agree–disagree battery.
- For a 32-item multi-item scale like the lab's, the psychometric evidence (Lozano 2008; Simms 2019) suggests 6 versus 7 points makes little difference to reliability. The comparability question (section 4) matters more than which format is "better."

## 2. What widely used validated instruments do, and whether developers justified it

### Takeaway
Practice follows the unipolar/bipolar distinction loosely. Agree–disagree (bipolar) instruments such as the SWLS and BFI generally include a labelled neutral midpoint. Frequency and intensity (unipolar) instruments use 4–6 points, where a middle category is "sometimes" or "moderately," not neutral, so the odd/even question largely dissolves. MAIA-2, the closest analogue to a 6-point interoceptive scale, uses a 0–5 frequency format ("never" to "always"). In none of the cases checked did the instrument papers report an empirical test of odd versus even. The format is generally inherited or by convention.

### Cited Findings
- **MAIA-2** (Mehling, W. E., Acree, M., Stewart, A., Silas, J., & Jones, A., 2018, "The Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness, Version 2 (MAIA-2)," *PLoS ONE, 13*(12), e0208034). 37 items rated on a six-point frequency scale, 0 = Never to 5 = Always. It is a unipolar frequency format, so there is no neutral midpoint by design. — [UCSF Osher MAIA-2 form](https://osher.ucsf.edu/sites/osher.ucsf.edu/files/inline-files/MAIA-2.pdf); [NovoPsych MAIA-2 summary](https://novopsych.com/?p=7868); [PMC record of Mehling et al. 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6279042/)
- **BFI** (Big Five Inventory): Simms et al. (2019) gave the BFI items on 2 to 11 options in an experiment and found no gains beyond 6. The standard BFI format is 5-point agree–disagree with a midpoint. — [UB news release](https://www.buffalo.edu/news/releases/2019/04/035.html); [Simms et al. 2019 record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/does-the-number-of-response-options-matter-psychometric-perspecti/)

### Inferences
- The unipolar/bipolar distinction (Pasek & Krosnick; Krosnick & Presser) is the right lens for the lab. If the 32 items are frequency or intensity items (MAIA-style "how often…"), the "midpoint" of a 7-point version is a moderate frequency, not neutrality. Adding it changes the category structure rather than adding a "no opinion" escape. If they are agree–disagree statements, a 7-point midpoint is a true "neither" and the Sturgis et al. (2014) hidden-don't-know concern applies.

### Gaps
- **Instrument formats below were not verified against the original validation papers this session.** They reflect widely documented standard formats and should be checked against the cited originals before being quoted:
  - **PHQ-9**: 4-point frequency 0–3 ("not at all" to "nearly every day"); Kroenke, Spitzer & Williams 2001, *J Gen Intern Med, 16*, 606–613.
  - **GAD-7**: same 0–3 format; Spitzer, Kroenke, Williams & Löwe 2006, *Arch Intern Med, 166*, 1092–1097.
  - **DASS-21**: 0–3 "did not apply to me at all" to "applied to me very much or most of the time"; Lovibond & Lovibond 1995.
  - **FFMQ**: 1–5 "never or very rarely true" to "very often or always true"; Baer et al. 2006, *Assessment, 13*, 27–45.
  - **PANAS**: 1–5 unipolar intensity "very slightly or not at all" to "extremely"; Watson, Clark & Tellegen 1988, *JPSP, 54*, 1063–1070.
  - **SWLS**: 1–7 agree–disagree with 4 = "neither agree nor disagree"; Diener, Emmons, Larsen & Griffin 1985, *J Pers Assess, 49*, 71–75.
  - **Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale**: 4-point strongly agree to strongly disagree, no midpoint; Rosenberg 1965.
  - **WEMWBS**: 1–5 "none of the time" to "all of the time"; Tennant et al. 2007, *Health Qual Life Outcomes, 5*, 63.
  - **BFI**: 1–5 "disagree strongly" to "agree strongly" with "neither agree nor disagree"; John & Srivastava 1999.
- I found no evidence (searched only briefly) that any of these developers ran a head-to-head test of odd versus even formats before fixing the format. That absence is itself unconfirmed.
- I did not verify whether the original MAIA (Mehling et al. 2012) also used 0–5 or whether the format changed between versions.

## 3. Is there a consensus statement or meta-analytic synthesis?

### Takeaway
I found no formal consensus statement (APA, AAPOR, ISPOR or similar) prescribing odd or even scales. The closest things to syntheses are narrative reviews (Krosnick & Presser), MTMM meta-analyses of scale length, and multi-condition experiments. Those meta-analyses are acknowledged to yield "inconsistent findings" because of confounding, so there is no meta-analytic consensus on midpoints specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Krosnick & Presser (2009/2010, footnote 3): meta-analyses of pre-existing surveys (Andrews 1984, 1990; Alwin & Krosnick 1991; Alwin 1992, 1997; Scherpenzeel 1995) "yielded inconsistent findings," because they "mixed together measures of subjective judgments with measurements of objective constructs" and involved "strong confounds between number of scale points and other item characteristics." — [Krosnick & Presser 2009 preprint](https://benjaminhirt.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2024/01/2009_handbook_krosnick-1.pdf)
- Simms et al. (2019) state in their abstract that "relatively little consensus exists" on the optimal number of options and on even versus odd. — [UB research record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/does-the-number-of-response-options-matter-psychometric-perspecti/)
- Weijters, Cabooter & Schillewaert (2010), "The effect of rating scale format on response styles: The number of response categories and response category labels," *International Journal of Research in Marketing, 27*(3), 236–247. The authors note researchers use varied formats "with few guidelines." Their 8-condition online experiment (N = 1,207, plus a follow-up with N = 226) found "strong effects of scale format on response distributions and misresponse to reversed items." — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ijrema/v27y2010i3p236-247.html)
- Kieruj, N. D., & Moors, G. (2010), "Variations in response style behavior by response scale format in attitude research," *International Journal of Public Opinion Research, 22*(3), 320–342. Scale length was varied from 5 to 11 categories and a latent-class factor model used. Extreme response style occurred regardless of length, with only weak evidence of acquiescence. — [Tilburg/LISS data archive study page](https://dataarchive.lissdata.nl/study-units/view/44); [Kieruj & Moors 2013 follow-up, Quality & Quantity 47, 193–211](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/qualqt/v47y2013i1p193-211.html)

### Inferences
- The defensible "consensus" is narrow:
  - Too few points (2–3) is bad.
  - Gains plateau around 5–7.
  - Every point should be labelled with words.
  - Agree–disagree formats carry acquiescence costs.
- On the midpoint itself the field is genuinely split. Its effect depends on item type and content (e.g., sensitivity or social desirability).

### Gaps
- I searched for AAPOR, APA or ISPOR guidance on midpoints and found none. ISPOR's PRO good-practice reports (e.g., on content validity and mode equivalence) may touch response-option choice but were not retrieved.
- I found no meta-analysis focused specifically on midpoint inclusion (as opposed to scale length).

## 4. Comparability when the same items are given with different numbers of points or with/without a midpoint

### Takeaway
- **Linear rescaling.** Rescaling to a common range (linear stretch, or POMP-style percent of maximum) yields roughly comparable means in randomized split-ballot experiments (Dawes 2008). But the evidence that format shifts response *distributions* and response styles (Weijters et al. 2010; Sturgis et al. 2014; Revilla et al. 2014) means linear rescaling cannot be assumed to give equivalent scores at the individual or distributional level, especially when one version has a midpoint and the other does not.
- **Equating and linking.** The more rigorous options are observed-score (equipercentile) equating with a random-groups or common-person design (Singh 2022), or IRT linking (Kolen & Brennan). Both require data in which the two formats can be tied together.
- **Invariance testing.** Conventional categorical multigroup CFA invariance testing cannot directly compare thresholds across different numbers of categories. Only configural- and loading-level comparisons are available unless categories are collapsed.

### Cited Findings
**Rescaling evidence**
- Dawes, J. (2008). Do data characteristics change according to the number of scale points used? An experiment using 5-point, 7-point and 10-point scales. *International Journal of Market Research, 50*(1). Randomized split-sample experiment. Reported result: data from the three formats are "approximately comparable in terms of mean score (once re-scaled) and various measures of variation and data shape." — [Yurova & Bendixen IJMR record citing the comparison](https://scholars.nova.edu/en/publications/how-respondents-use-verbal-and-numeric-rating-scales-a-case-for-r/). **Secondary**: summary via search results, not the Dawes paper itself.
- de Jonge, T., Veenhoven, R., & Arends, L. (2014). Homogenizing responses to different survey questions on the same topic: Proposal of a scale homogenization method using a reference distribution. *Social Indicators Research*. They describe "Linear Stretch," in use "almost a century ago," which stretches the ranks of response options to a common range (e.g., 0–10). They argue it ignores differences in how response options are used and propose a "Reference Distribution Method" that "produces comparable time series on different questions and allows discontinuities in data to be corrected." — [Veenhoven abstract page](https://personal.eur.nl/veenhoven/Pub2010s/2014g-ab.html); [full text PDF](https://personal.eur.nl/veenhoven/Pub2010s/2014g-full.pdf)

**Equating and linking**
- Singh, R. K. (2022). Harmonizing single-question instruments for latent constructs with equating using political interest as an example. *Survey Research Methods, 16*(3), 353–369. https://doi.org/10.18148/srm/2022.v16i3.7916
  - Proposes "observed score equating with a random groups design" from psychometrics for ex-post harmonization of instruments that differ in "wording and response format."
  - Shows "equating works well in transforming the numerical scores," and is feasible with an online nonprobability sample as well as probability samples.
  - Random-groups equating "needs data for both instruments from the same population."
  - [SRM article page](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/7916)
- IRT linking: Kolen, M. J., & Brennan, R. L. (2014), *Test Equating, Scaling, and Linking: Methods and Practices* (3rd ed., Springer) is the standard reference for anchor-item designs that place forms on a common scale. — [summary context via sirt package documentation](https://rdrr.io/pkg/sirt/man/linking.haberman.html). **Secondary**: book not consulted.

**Invariance testing**
- Categorical multigroup CFA invariance procedures generally require the same number of categories per indicator across groups. Collapsing categories to force equality has meaningful effects on model fit and reduces reliability (Fisher information). — [Elosua, Psicológica (uv.es), on collapsing categories in MG-CFA](https://www.uv.es/psicologica/articulos2.11/13ELOSUA.pdf). **Secondary**: from a search summary; not read in full.

**Experiments varying scale length on the same items**
- Wakita, Ueshima & Noguchi (2012), *Educational and Psychological Measurement*: 722 undergraduates answered the same items in 4-, 5- and 7-point formats, analysed with IRT. The number of points affected the psychological distance between options, particularly on the 7-point scale. — [search summary; ERIC EJ1359497 also on category sufficiency](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1359497.pdf). **Secondary/unverified**: the citation for Wakita et al. comes from a search summary; the ERIC link is a different paper on category sufficiency.
- Medical-education rating study comparing 5- and 7-point scales on the same instrument: on the 7-point version residents used the extra categories, especially among positive ratings, giving a more even distribution and a smaller ceiling effect. — [PMC7510269](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7510269/)
- Weijters et al. (2010): format (number of categories and labelling) has "strong effects" on response distributions and misresponse to reversed items. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ijrema/v27y2010i3p236-247.html)

**Midpoint-specific shifts**
- O'Muircheartaigh et al. (via Krosnick & Presser): without a midpoint, would-be midpoint respondents scatter *randomly* to the adjacent moderate points. This implies little mean shift but added noise. — [Krosnick & Presser 2009](https://benjaminhirt.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2024/01/2009_handbook_krosnick-1.pdf)
- Sturgis et al. (2014): midpoint choosers are mostly hidden "don't knows," so the midpoint's presence or absence changes *who* lands where and alters inferences. — [LSE record](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/101999)

**Context: agree–disagree versus item-specific formats**
- Saris, W. E., Revilla, M., Krosnick, J. A., & Shaeffer, E. M. (2010). Comparing questions with agree/disagree response options to questions with item-specific response options. *Survey Research Methods, 4*(1), 61–79. A design combining random-assignment experiments with multitrait–multimethod (MTMM) analysis in representative samples found agree–disagree items "had much lower quality" than comparable item-specific questions. The authors attribute the problem to five decades of documented acquiescence. — [UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/handle/10230/43541); [SSOAR full text](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/handle/document/44197)
- Dykema, J., Schaeffer, N. C., Garbarski, D., Assad, N., & Blixt, S. (2022). Towards a reconsideration of the use of agree-disagree questions in measuring subjective evaluations. *Research in Social and Administrative Pharmacy, 18*(2), 2335–2344. A review recommending item-specific over agree–disagree questions "for most purposes," because more studies link item-specific questions to better validity and reliability and agree–disagree questions to acquiescence and response effects. — [Loyola eCommons](https://ecommons.luc.edu/soc_facpubs/45); [PMC8692311](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8692311)
- Krosnick & Presser (via Pasek & Krosnick 2010): 10–20% of respondents agree with both a statement and its reversal. Balancing keyed items only "moves acquiescers… to the midpoint of the final score's continuum (where they also don't necessarily belong)" (Billiet & McClendon 2000). "It is preferable simply to ask questions with construct-specific response alternatives." — [Pasek & Krosnick chapter PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2009/2009%2003-Leighly-Chapter03.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Lab's case (6-point without midpoint versus 7-point with midpoint, same 32 items):**
  - *Mean level*: POMP or linear-stretch rescaling, (x − min)/(max − min) × 100, is the conventional first step and, per Dawes (2008)-type split-ballot evidence, probably gives roughly comparable means. But the change bundles two manipulations: one more category, and a midpoint. The literature shows the midpoint can move respondents non-randomly (Sturgis et al.) or only add noise (O'Muircheartaigh et al.), so mean comparability cannot be assumed. It is an empirical question for these items.
  - *Item and distribution level*: different category counts change response-style expression (extreme responding, acquiescence) and category use. Item-level distributions and item-level correlations are therefore not directly comparable after linear stretching. Scale-level correlations with external criteria are likely more robust (cf. Simms 2019: criterion validity did not vary with format the way precision did).
  - *Invariance testing across studies*: with different numbers of categories, scalar (threshold) invariance cannot be tested in standard categorical MG-CFA. Options are (a) treating items as continuous (defensible at 6–7 categories per Boateng et al. 2018) and testing configural, metric and scalar invariance on rescaled scores; (b) collapsing the 7-point midpoint into an adjacent category or splitting it, which is ad hoc and costs information; or (c) IRT with a graded response model fitted separately, then linking on assumed-invariant item discriminations. Option (c) requires anchor assumptions. With no common persons and no randomization across formats, any linking leans on the assumption that the two samples share a latent distribution, which is untestable.
  - *Gold standard*: a small calibration study that randomly assigns respondents to the 6-point or 7-point version (random-groups design), or gives both to the same people in counterbalanced order (single-group design). This permits observed-score equating (Singh 2022; Kolen & Brennan 2014) or direct invariance testing of a format effect, and yields a crosswalk for the existing data.
- **Midpoint versus agree–disagree format**: if the 32 items are agree–disagree statements, the larger threat to validity is probably the agree–disagree format itself (acquiescence; Saris et al. 2010; Dykema et al. 2022) rather than the midpoint. The midpoint question is second-order compared with that.

### Gaps
