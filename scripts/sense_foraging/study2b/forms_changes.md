## Debrief form

### About This Study

**Approved (Study 1):**

> This study is testing a new questionnaire designed to measure people's ability to notice and shift into a receptive, sensory way of engaging with the world (sometimes called "sense foraging"), as distinct from a more goal-directed, evaluative way of engaging with it. In this first study we are examining how the questionnaire's 48 statements group together (its factor structure) and which statements best capture each part, so that we can produce a shorter version. Some statements were worded in the opposite direction from others (for example, describing difficulty rather than ease): mixing directions helps us separate what people agree with from a general tendency to agree. A later study will examine how the questionnaire relates to established measures of mindfulness and bodily awareness.

**Study 2b:**

> This study is testing a new questionnaire designed to measure people's ability to notice and shift into a receptive, sensory way of engaging with the world (sometimes called "sense foraging"), as distinct from a more goal-directed, evaluative way of engaging with it. Earlier studies found that its statements measure one broad capacity and chose a short version. This study checks that short version in a new group of people and examines how it relates to established measures of trait mindfulness (FFMQ-15), bodily awareness (Brief MAIA-2), decentering (Experiences Questionnaire), savouring (Savoring Beliefs Inventory), openness (BFI-2 Open-Mindedness), sensation seeking (BSSS-8), need for cognition (NCS-6), sensitivity to light and sound (SPSQ-SF), worry about body sensations (ASI-3), finding meaning in events (Synchronicity Awareness and Meaning-Detecting Scale), and a tendency to describe oneself favourably (BIDR-16). Your ratings of the brief descriptions of people tell us whether the difference between the two modes was clear. Some statements were worded in the opposite direction from others (for example, describing difficulty rather than ease): mixing directions helps us separate what people agree with from a general tendency to agree.
