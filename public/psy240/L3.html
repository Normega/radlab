Where we are

From "is there a line" to "which side"

Weeks 1 and 2 built the tools. From today, every lecture is: here is a family of disorders, here is what separates them, and here is how confident we should be.

The Field Guide carries the criteria. These three hours are for the things a page cannot do: telling them apart, arguing about them, and seeing them in a person.

Set the expectation once, here, and you never have to justify not reading criteria off a slide again.

Part one

One emotion, seven disorders

The distinction underneath everything

Fear is not anxiety

Both are in every disorder in this chapter. What separates the diagnoses is not the emotion — it is what triggers it.

The time axis is the whole distinction: present versus future. Ask for an example of each from the room before revealing the bottom line.

Why anyone, and why this one

Three vulnerabilities, not one cause

Barlow's triple-vulnerability model: nobody inherits "an anxiety disorder." You inherit, learn, and meet things that stack.

The first two make someone anxious in general. The third decides what the anxiety is about — which is exactly the question the next slide sorts the whole chapter by.

Lecture 1's diathesis–stress model, made specific: two diatheses you carry everywhere, one you learned about a particular thing, and a stressor to set it off. Say that link out loud.

The Guide has the ingredients but not this model. The anxiety-disorders page's Etiology section lists them separately — gene–environment interaction as its central claim, the amygdala, maladaptive appraisals, conditioning, and avoidance as what keeps it going. What it lacks is Barlow's account of how they combine, so the model is not examinable as stated. That integration is open on the gap board: say so, it is a good amber target.

The organising question

What is the anxiety about?

Specific phobiaa particular object or situation
Social anxietybeing evaluated negatively by others
Panic disorderthe next attack — and the bodily sensations that herald it
Agoraphobiasituations that would be hard to escape or get help in
GADmany things, attached to nothing in particular
Separation anxietybeing separated from an attachment figure
Selective mutismspeaking where speech is expected

One question sorts almost the whole chapter.

One thing the table hides: these diagnoses co-occur so often that comorbidity is the rule here, not the exception — Lecture 2's bridge symptoms, live.

This table is the single most useful thing on the midterm and they should photograph it. Say so.

Do not read it aloud line by line — put it up, give them twenty seconds, then reveal the bottom line and move.

Case · the one they miss

He avoids the subway and large stores

"If something went wrong I couldn't get out, or get help fast enough."

Not specific phobia. The fear isn't of the subway. It's of what escape would be like.

Not social anxiety. Nothing here is about being judged.

Agoraphobia — two or more situations, feared because escape would be difficult or help unavailable.

This lands straight after Exercise A's case 5, which was also agoraphobia. Pull up its distribution on the Run tab first: expect panic and specific phobia to split the room, and separation anxiety to catch anyone who fixed on the daughter. Then this second person — different places, same reason — is the point: the diagnosis lives in why the place is feared, not where it is.

Agoraphobia is the most misremembered diagnosis in the chapter ("fear of open spaces" is a mistranslation that has outlived its usefulness). Correct it explicitly.

The one that gets only a row

Social anxiety: the fear is being seen

Not shyness. The worry is being judged — as stupid, anxious, boring or unlikeable — and often that the anxiety itself will show: blushing, trembling, a voice that shakes.

So the symptoms feed the fear: being seen to be anxious is the thing feared. The dread can start days or weeks before the event, and ends in either avoidance or enduring it under strain.

About 7% in the United States. Between 0.5 and 2% in most of the rest of the world.

Is the U.S. that much more socially anxious, or is the instrument measuring something cultural? That is Lecture 2's question, back again.

Exercise A's case 2 was this. It is among the commonest disorders in the chapter; this is where it gets its due.

Both prevalence figures are the Guide's (APA, 2022). Let the room argue about the gap before you move on — the honest answer is that nobody is sure how much is real difference and how much is the criteria travelling badly across cultures.

A number worth carrying

Nearly 75% fear more than one thing

A patient reports a dog phobia. The epidemiology says: keep asking.

The average person with a specific phobia fears three or more objects or situations.

So an assessment that stops at the presenting fear systematically under-detects. This is a measurement point, not a trivia point — it is Lecture 2 showing up in a clinic.

Whenever you can connect a disorder fact back to the methods lecture, do it. It is what stops week 2 feeling like a detour.

Specific phobia, sorted

Four kinds — and one runs backwards

Animaldogs, spiders, snakes
Natural environmentheights, storms, water
Blood–injection–injuryneedles, invasive medical procedures
Situationalairplanes, elevators, enclosed spaces

Blood–injection–injury is the odd one. Fear raises heart rate and blood pressure; here they rise, then fall sharply — and the person faints. That was Exercise A's case 1.

So its treatment inverts the usual advice: not relaxation, but applied tension — tensing the muscles to hold blood pressure up.

The four specifiers are the Guide's (APA, 2022) and are on the midterm. The fainting mechanism (the vasovagal response) and applied tension are not yet in the Guide — standard, well-replicated, but say they are not examinable as stated.

Point forward: the treatments slide after Break 2 shows desensitization pairing exposure with relaxation. For this one phobia, relaxation is exactly wrong.

Part two

Panic, and the loop that keeps it going

First, the boundary

A panic attack is not panic disorder

An abrupt surge of fear with four or more physical or cognitive symptoms, peaking within minutes.

Attacks are common. The disorder is not.

The diagnosis needs the attacks to be unexpected, and then a month or more of persistent worry about further attacks or significant behaviour change to avoid them.

Which is why the clinical question after "did you have an attack?" is always "and what has it done to your life since?"

This is the stepped-vignette on the midterm, so teach the sequence explicitly: attack → unexpectedness → the month of apprehension or avoidance.

The mechanism

The loop that feeds itself

The misinterpretation is not just a symptom — it is the engine.

Trace the loop with a finger on the projection. Then ask: if this is the mechanism, what would a treatment have to do? They will get to "prove the prediction wrong" on their own, which sets up the next slide.

Therefore

Interoceptive exposure

Spin in a chair. Breathe fast. Deliberately produce the sensations — and let the catastrophic prediction fail to come true.

And therefore: anything that blunts the sensations undermines the treatment. A safety behaviour, a distraction, sometimes a medication — each prevents the disconfirmation the therapy depends on.

The mechanism tells you what will help and what will quietly get in the way.

This is the best example in the course of a mechanism generating a treatment. Say it in those terms.

The medication point is contested; the Field Guide's debate page has it. Do not resolve it here — flag that it is arguable and let anyone interested claim the gap.

The loop's honest problem

Panic that wakes you up

Many people with panic disorder have attacks that arrive out of sleep — and not from dreams. They come from deep, non-dreaming sleep, usually early in the night.

If panic is the catastrophic misreading of a sensation, who is doing the misreading while you are asleep?

One answer: the sleeping brain still monitors the body, and a shift in breathing or heart rate at the edge of deep sleep can trip the alarm. That rescues the model — but only by making "interpretation" something that does not need you to be awake.

Leave this unresolved on purpose; it is the best available argument that the misinterpretation account is incomplete, and students who just learned the loop should feel its edge.

Not yet in the Field Guide — nocturnal panic appears nowhere in it. Good green gap for the panic-disorder page.

The other loop

GAD: worry that works, briefly

Panic's loop runs through the body. GAD's runs through thought — quieter, which is why it got one row in the table and is the easiest disorder in the chapter to miss.

  • Worrying blunts the body's arousal — thinking about everything is a way of never feeling any one thing fully
  • That relief negatively reinforces the worrying — Lecture 1's operant gear, turning
  • Until it runs on its own: the criterion is precisely that the worry is uncontrollable

Two loops so far — panic feeds on sensations, worry feeds on relief-in-thought. After the break: a third, and it feeds on rituals.

The figure is deliberately the panic loop's geometry with different labels — put them side by side in your head and say so; the parallel form is the pedagogy. The Guide's generalized-anxiety-disorder page carries the clinical picture; intolerance of uncertainty is the cognitive account to name if the room wants more.

Part three

Obsessions, compulsions, and a chapter that is a hypothesis

The relief loop

Everyone has intrusive thoughts

"Did I lock the door?" Most people check once, or reassure themselves, and it dissolves.

The difference isn't having the thought.

It is being unable to neutralise it without a ritual — and the ritual's temporary relief reinforces it, converting a behaviour into a compulsion.

Which is exactly why the treatment has to block the compulsion, not just present the trigger. Leave the escape route open and you preserve the contingency you are trying to break.

Scale and clock: about 1.2% of the population, mean onset 19.5 — this room's age — and left untreated it runs chronic.

This is negative reinforcement from Lecture 1's behavioural model, arriving with a clinical job. Point back explicitly — students who saw operant conditioning as abstract get it here.

Both numbers are the Guide's (APA 2022): 1.2% prevalence, onset 19.5 with a quarter of cases by 14. "This room's age" is what makes them land.

Why some people cannot let it go

Try not to think of a white bear

Thirty seconds, starting now: do not think of a white bear. Tally every time you do.

Deliberately suppressing a thought makes it come back more (Wegner et al., 1987). Suppression is a compulsion that manufactures its own trigger.

Add thought–action fusion — treating a thought as if it were the deed: thinking it makes it likelier, or is as bad as doing it — and an ordinary intrusion becomes a moral emergency that must be pushed away, which brings it back.

Which is why exposure asks people to hold the thought, not fight it.

Run the demo for real and ask for a show of hands on the tallies — nearly everyone scores above zero, which is the point.

The white-bear rebound is the Guide's (obsessive-compulsive-disorder page). The Guide describes intrusions "fused with moral alarm" but uses the name thought–action fusion only on the illness-anxiety page; the term itself is standard (Shafran, Thordarson & Rachman, 1996).

The evidence, honestly stated

ERP: up to 86% — of whom?

Exposure and response prevention is first-line for OCD, and the headline number is genuinely impressive.

Among treatment completers.

The repeated exposures with rituals blocked are extremely distressing, and getting people to stay in treatment is the single largest barrier.

A completer rate overstates what the treatment achieves in the world. That is not a criticism of ERP — it is a specification of what the number means.

Adding SSRIs to ERP was not superior to ERP alone in that trial.

Lecture 2's separation of effect from evidence, applied. Ask what number they would want instead (intention-to-treat), and let them arrive at it.

The chapter itself

OCD's neighbours were grouped by a hypothesis

Before DSM-5, this chapter did not exist. OCD sat with the anxiety disorders.

It was moved out and grouped with four conditions thought to share its machinery: a repetitive behaviour the person cannot stop, performed to relieve a distressing internal state.

The four, by name: hoarding disorder · body dysmorphic disorder — a perceived defect in appearance, with the chapter's grimmest stakes: 4× the suicidal ideation and 2.6× the attempts of controls · trichotillomania (hair-pulling) · excoriation (skin-picking)

That grouping is a hypothesis, not an observation — and the chapter's own evidence tests it unevenly.

Hoarding disorder is where it fits worst: many patients report neither intrusive thoughts nor ritual urges. Which is exactly why treatment moved away from ERP toward CBT with motivational interviewing.

This is the lecture's best example of taxonomy as a live scientific claim rather than a filing system. It also rewards anyone who paid attention in week 2.

When the fix makes it worse

Body dysmorphic disorder and the surgeon

A defect others cannot see, or barely see — and three to eight hours a day spent on it: mirrors, grooming, comparing, camouflaging.

Some pursue repeated cosmetic surgery for a "perfect" appearance.

But if the problem is in the perceiving, not the nose, a better nose leaves the problem exactly where it was.

The 3–8 hours and the repeated-surgery pattern are the Guide's (APA, 2022). Keep the surgery point as reasoning, not a statistic — the Guide does not carry a figure for how many surgical patients have BDD.

Tie back to the suicidality figures on the previous slide: this is the disorder in the chapter where the stakes are highest.

The rest of the neighbourhood

Tics, hair-pulling, skin-picking

Tic disorders — sudden, repeated movements or sounds.

Tourette's needs both motor and vocal tics, for over a year. Persistent: one kind only, over a year. Provisional: under a year.

Trichotillomania · excoriation — pulling hair, picking skin.

Usually not painful. Pulling is usually done alone and can relieve an itch-like tension; picking often begins with acne.

Tic or compulsion? A compulsion is done to relieve anxiety, usually after an obsession. A tic is done in a particular way — evenly, a set number of times — until it feels "just right."

All of this is the Guide's (tic-disorders, trichotillomania, excoriation-disorder pages), and Quiz 3 and the midterm both ask about it. Tic disorders live in DSM's neurodevelopmental chapter, but the Guide assigns them to this lecture because the differential is with OCD.

The treatment for the body-focused behaviours is habit reversal training — name it: a close cousin of ERP's response prevention, where a competing action blocks the behaviour while the urge passes.

The neurobiology, and its ceiling

The best-developed account is still correlational

Overactive orbitofrontal cortex, insufficient caudate filtering, too many impulses reaching the thalamus — experienced as intrusive obsessions and the urge to act.

  • Supported by neuroimaging and by lesion cases where OCD symptoms remitted after damage to those structures
  • But: imaging is correlational and does not establish direction
  • And: no equivalent findings are reported for the other disorders in the chapter — so the circuit does not explain the grouping

Build the reveals slowly. The last bullet is the one that matters: the best biological story in the chapter cannot justify the chapter.

The through-line of treatment

All of it is the same idea

Meet the thing. Don't escape. Let the prediction fail.

What differs is only what you expose the person to, and how fast.

Four treatments, one idea — worth stating because it makes the whole chapter's treatment section memorable instead of a list.
