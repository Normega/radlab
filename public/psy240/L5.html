Part one

Two questions generate the whole chapter

The organising idea

Polarity and duration

Hold those two axes and you can reconstruct most of the boundaries without memorising them.

Episodes also carry specifiers, and specifiers change treatment: seasonal pattern — the one you know as SAD, and light therapy follows from it — peripartum onset, and an MDD episode landing on top of persistent depressive disorder: double depression, the grid's worst corner.

Build the grid on the board as you talk rather than just showing it. Students who draw it themselves keep it.

Anchor the scale once, out loud: mood disorders are among the commonest reasons anyone seeks mental-health care — the Guide's mood-disorders and epidemiology pages carry the figures, so point rather than quote.

The specifier line: double depression is on persistent-depressive-disorder. Seasonal pattern, light therapy and peripartum onset are on no Guide page (major-depressive-disorder mentions only melatonin in winter symptoms) — not in the Guide, so not examinable as stated. Say so if asked.

Building block one

A depressive episode, in four domains

At least two weeks, at least five symptoms — and one of them must be depressed mood or anhedonia.

  • Mood — depressed most of the day; anhedonia, the loss of pleasure in what used to give it
  • Behavioural — activity and productivity fall; home and work are disrupted
  • Cognitive — worthlessness, self-blame, poor concentration, thoughts of suicide
  • Physical — sleep too little or too much, fatigue, appetite and weight change, slowed or agitated movement

Which domain leads depends on where you are. In China and other Asian countries, presentations emphasise the physical over the cognitive.

And a normal response to a major loss can look the same. The criteria leave room for clinical judgement, informed by the person's history and their culture's norms for expressing distress.

Before the culture box, ask: if a patient doesn't say "I feel worthless", what would you look for instead? Then reveal it.

All from major-depressive-disorder: Presentation (the four domains), Diagnosis (five symptoms, two weeks, one of the two cardinal symptoms; the bereavement differential), Epidemiology (culture). The US 12-month rate there is ~7%; the Canadian lifetime rate 11.2% against the US 16.6% is worth one spoken line.

Building block two

Mania and hypomania: one picture, two intensities

Mania: abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood plus increased energy, most of the day, nearly every day — with three associated symptoms, or four if the mood is only irritable.

Grandiosity · decreased need for sleep · pressured speech · racing thoughts, flight of ideas · distractibility · a burst of new, often reckless projects.

The four-day line is a committee's decision. Two days was proposed, and family-history data supported it. It was rejected over the risk of more false positives.

Ask before the hypomania row appears: what would you change about mania to make it "less"? Duration, and consequence — then reveal.

Sources: mood-disorders (What this chapter covers: the two building blocks), bipolar-i-disorder (Presentation; Diagnosis: three symptoms, four if only irritable), bipolar-ii-disorder (Presentation; Contested: the two-day proposal). The four-day story is Lecture 2's error trade-off, live: a threshold is where a committee put the line between two kinds of mistake.

The single fact that reorganises everything

One manic episode is enough

Bipolar I does not require a depressive episode. Ever. A manic episode is the whole requirement.

And it works retroactively. Someone carrying a bipolar II diagnosis for six years who has a first full manic episode is reclassified — bipolar II requires that mania has never occurred.

Diagnosis here is a claim about a lifetime, not about today.

This trips up almost everyone the first time. Ask what that means for someone diagnosed at 19 — the answer, that the diagnosis is provisional on the rest of their life, is genuinely unsettling and worth saying.

The most consequential misreading

Bipolar II is not "mild bipolar I"

Bipolar II is defined by an absence: full mania has never happened. It is easy to read an absence as less severity.

The depressive pole is where it does its damage. High depressive burden, poor functioning, poor outcomes — and suicide rates equal to or higher than bipolar I.

Ask: who is more impaired today — someone with bipolar I in remission, or someone with bipolar II in a depressive episode? The mood-disorders page answers it (The logic of the boundaries): quite possibly the second. Severity now and the lifetime label are different questions.

bipolar-ii-disorder, Contested (Berk et al., 2025) for the burden and the reliability table. Do not quote any bipolar prevalence comparison — the Canadian-lifetime vs US-12-month contrast and the bipolar I 12-month figure are both flagged.

Which is worse — one severe three-month episode, or two years of low-grade misery? Chronic depression carries greater impairment and more reduced quality of life.

An MDD episode on top of PDD is double depression. CBASP is the only psychotherapy designed for chronic depression.

Take a show of hands on "which is worse" before the reveal. Most pick the dramatic episode; the grinding one is the answer.

Chronic-course risk profile, if asked: comorbid mental and physical disorders, poor social integration, early onset, early trauma (mood-disorders, Etiology).

A diagnosis built to fix a mistake

DMDD

In the 1990s, researchers proposed that childhood bipolar disorder presents not as manic episodes but as severe chronic irritability.

  • Chronically irritable children were diagnosed bipolar — and treated with mood stabilisers and antipsychotics
  • DSM-5 added DMDD as the alternative home for that presentation
  • And the follow-up data settle it: these children grow into unipolar depressive and anxiety disorders, not bipolar adults

Irritability has two parts: the outbursts, and the sullen, angry mood between them. Assess only the outbursts and you see a behaviour problem; the mood part carries the prognosis.

A category invented to correct a diagnostic practice — and the prognosis is the evidence that the practice was wrong.

Connect to Lecture 2: this is what "clinical utility" looks like in the wild. The category may or may not carve nature; it demonstrably stopped children being medicated for the wrong thing.

Two more from disruptive-mood-dysregulation-disorder if there is time: ICD-11 solved the same problem differently, with a chronic-irritability specifier on oppositional defiant disorder — two manuals, two placements. And the payoff: stimulants, withheld under the bipolar framing, became an option where ADHD is present.

Defined by a calendar

PMDD

Across most cycles, at least five symptoms — and at least one must be core affective: mood swings, irritability or anger, depressed mood, or anxiety and tension.

The content could be any depression. The timing is the diagnosis.

Promoted from DSM-IV's "criteria sets for further study" into the main manual in DSM-5 — the move drew medicalization critiques the Guide names but does not yet cover.

Ask: the same symptoms, present all month and just worse before menses — PMDD? No: the postmenstrual week has to be quiet, and the reveal makes that visible.

premenstrual-dysphoric-disorder (Presentation, Diagnosis); the medicalization point is in mood-disorders, Contested. The page also notes PMDD/PMS is common in women with bipolar II. No prevalence, etiology or treatment is in the Guide — every one of those sections is an open gap, which is worth saying to a room with a green contribution due tonight.

Part two

Explaining depression

Three accounts, all partial

What is actually best evidenced?

Cognitive. Beck's triad: negative views of self, world and future. Best-evidenced psychological account, and the basis of CBT.

Learned helplessness. Uncontrollable aversive events produce passivity; people who explain bad events as internal, stable and global are more prone.

Monoamine hypothesis. Low serotonin and norepinephrine. Underlies SSRIs and MAOIs — and the source itself concedes it is incomplete.

Genes split the chapter. About 30% of a depressed person's relatives are affected, against 10% generally. Bipolar twin concordance reaches ~72% in identical twins, against 5–15% in fraternal twins and other relatives.

And depression's 2:1 female-to-male ratio does not appear in the bipolar disorders — whatever explains the one has to explain the other.

Do not present these as competing to be right. Present them as different levels, each with a treatment attached, which is Lecture 1's integrative move applied to one disorder.

The behavioural account (Lewinsohn: less response-contingent reinforcement) is the fourth, and it is the rationale for behavioural activation on the treatment slide. Heritability from mood-disorders, Etiology and major-depressive-disorder, Etiology; the sex-ratio point from mood-disorders, Epidemiology and gender-differences-in-depression — of its five theories, artifact and hormone are unsupported and rumination is the best-supported partial account.

Where the chemical story came from

Two drugs, and a reverse inference

  1. 1950s: tuberculosis drugs that raised monoamines lifted mood
  2. Blood-pressure drugs that lowered norepinephrine induced depression
  3. Therefore, the reasoning went, depression is caused by low monoamines

Headaches are not an aspirin deficiency.

That a drug alters a system and changes symptoms does not establish that the system's state caused them.

The hypothesis is useful, incomplete, and everywhere in public discourse as though it were settled.

Callback to Lecture 1 — say "aspirin deficiency" and they should finish the sentence. If they do, that is the course working.

Treatment, briefly

Several things work, and about equally

  • CBT — targets the cognitive triad directly
  • Behavioural activation — increase contact with natural reinforcement; comparable to CBT and to medication
  • Antidepressants — and see the previous slide about what that does and does not prove
  • MBCT — best known for preventing relapse
  • Bipolar is the exception — lithium and mood stabilisers are the treatment; antidepressants alone are not
  • Severe, treatment-resistant depression: ECT — effective, stigma-laden, with real memory side effects; the Guide's electroconvulsive-therapy page tells it straight

The cleanest contrast in the therapy literature: CBT confronts and changes a maladaptive thought. Mindfulness acknowledges and accepts it. Pushing out, versus not getting caught up.

The MBCT/CBT contrast is one line and students remember it for years. Do not over-elaborate it.

If asked which to choose: that is the dodo bird verdict from Lecture 2, and the honest answer is that head-to-head differences are small and often underpowered.

Two on the list, opened up

Interpersonal therapy, and two kinds of mindfulness

IPT (Klerman, Weissman, 1970s). Depression is a common, treatable illness — not the person's fault — tied to a current life event. Four problem areas:

complicated bereavement · role disputes · role transitions · interpersonal deficits

MBSR (Kabat-Zinn, 1982) — meditation, yoga, attention to physical experience, to lower stress generally.

MBCT (Segal, Williams & Teasdale, 2002) — attention to thoughts and their emotions. Best known for preventing relapse in depression.

Mindfulness-based therapy for anxiety and depression: moderate symptom improvement (Hofmann et al., 2010) — one of the few magnitudes anywhere in the Guide.

Ask: a student's depression began the term they moved cities and left every friend behind. Which IPT problem area? Role transition. Then ask what MBCT would do with the thought "I'll never belong here" — notice it and not get caught up in it, rather than dispute it.

interpersonal-therapy; mindfulness-based-therapy (The two main forms; Evidence). IPT sits with CBT, BA and antidepressants as first-line with no modality reliably ahead (major-depressive-disorder, Treatment).

Where the two sides meet

Get the diagnosis right before the prescription

Half of bipolar II cases were previously undiagnosed, with a median delay of almost eight years. In one French study, careful re-examination of the same depressed patients raised the bipolar II rate from 22% to 40%.

Eight years treated as unipolar is eight years of the wrong first-line drug.

Ask before the right branch appears: why would a psychiatrist ask a depressed person about their best week ever? Then reveal.

mood-disorders, Treatment ("the diagnosis therefore has to be right before treatment starts"); mood-stabilizers; bipolar-ii-disorder, Contested (undiagnosed half, eight-year median delay, EPIDEP 22% vs 40%). Lithium's central clinical problem is adherence — people stop it to regain the highs (bipolar-i-disorder, Treatment). Psychotherapy in bipolar disorder is adjunctive, not a substitute.

If asked about antidepressant classes: SSRIs, tricyclics and MAOIs, no class proven superior; MAOIs held for treatment-resistant cases (antidepressant-medications).

Where it sits in the manual

Suicide is not a disorder

It is an outcome — one that cuts across the DSM rather than sitting inside it.

Which is why it appears on eleven different disorder pages in your Field Guide rather than having a chapter of its own.

Around 90% of people who die by suicide have a diagnosis of at least one mental disorder, most often a mood disorder.

Read that figure in both directions.

It establishes that mental disorder is the dominant risk context. It does not establish that disorder is sufficient — the overwhelming majority of people with these diagnoses do not die by suicide.

The both-directions move is the intellectual content of this hour and it is also the humane one: it refuses both "it's just the illness" and "anyone might".

The number the field looks past

Who is actually at risk

US figures, 2017. Jobes & Joiner (2019)

A prevention programme evaluated only on deaths is measuring the rarest outcome — and is nearly blind to the population it is meant to reach.

And read the layers by sex: women attempt more; men die more. Attempt data and death data describe different populations, and must be read separately.

This is Lecture 2's measurement thinking applied where it matters most. It is also the answer to "why do we ask people about thoughts rather than just tracking outcomes".

The sex crossover is on the Guide's suicide-and-self-harm page; means-lethality is the standard account, and it is also why means restriction works — connect it to the means leg on the next slide and the "reduce access to means" line after it.

Why the 90% is not sufficient

Three conditions, at the same time

  1. Vulnerability, biological or psychological → treatment
  2. Means to act → means safety
  3. No protective factors — support, faith, coping and problem-solving skills → connection and coping

Each leg is separately modifiable, and prevention works on all three.

Hopelessness, not sadness, is the state that recurs across the risk literature. Optimism is protective.

Depressive disorder: 17-fold risk. Bipolar disorder: 25–50% attempt at least once. In older adults, feeling like a burden to others matters.

Ask the room for the second leg before revealing the targets: which of the three could a friend change tonight? Means and connection.

suicide-and-self-harm: "Why it happens: a three-part model", "How suicide relates to mental disorder", "Risk factors". Do not attribute the model to a named author — the page's attribution is flagged and excluded from items; teach the structure only. Keep it at this level: no method or case detail.

What the evidence supports

Asking does not plant the idea

The single most persistent myth in this area.

Asking directly about suicidal thoughts does not create them. It is the beginning of every risk assessment there is, and it is something anyone in this room can do for a friend.

What helps: ask plainly · stay with the person · reduce access to means · connect them to a service rather than a promise · follow up the next day.

Keep this practical and brief. Do not turn it into gatekeeper training, but do not skip it — for some students this is the most useful ten minutes of the course.

Guide status: the "asking does not plant the idea" finding is not on suicide-and-self-harm, so it is not examinable as stated. Means safety and follow-up contact are on the page (Risk factors; Prevention).

Prevention that works

Not a promise — a plan

No-harm contracts asked people to promise not to act. They were coercive, and evidence displaced them.

  • Safety Plan Intervention (Stanley & Brown) — triggers → own coping → distraction with others → supportive help → professional help → securing means. It beat no-harm contracting on attempts in a large cohort of US veterans.
  • Crisis Response Plan — written on an index card, in the person's own words. 76% fewer attempts at six months.

Caring contacts. A letter of concern every four months for five years after discharge reduced suicides (Motto & Bostrom). Among the cheapest interventions in the course with a mortality effect.

The barrier: about 60% of people with suicidal thoughts don't seek help — mostly because of low perceived need, or wanting to handle it alone.

Ask what "follow up the next day" on the previous slide has in common with the letters. That is the same move at two scales.

suicide-and-self-harm, Prevention. The help-seeking figure also opens student-support-resources ("Why this page exists") — point at the support slide again here. The page also covers post-discharge risk and involuntary hospitalisation; leave those for the law-and-ethics lecture unless asked.

Treatment

Treat the suicidality, not only the disorder

The pattern: treatments that target suicidality directly — rather than treating the disorder and expecting the risk to follow — produce the largest effects.

Two of the strongest trials used soldiers and veterans. Whether the effects hold elsewhere is open.
