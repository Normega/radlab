Part one

What counts as a substance problem

The framework

One definition, ten substance classes

Substance use disorder: continued use despite substance-related problems — with as few as two indicators from a standard list sufficing. DSM-5-TR names ten classes — alcohol, cannabis, opioids, stimulants, sedatives, hallucinogens, inhalants, tobacco, caffeine, other — and one criterion set serves nine of them. Caffeine is the exception.

  • Tolerance — escalating dose for the same effect
  • Withdrawal — a substance-specific syndrome on stopping
  • Loss of control, failed cut-downs, time consumed, roles abandoned, use despite harm…

Note what the definition does not mention: quantity. The syndrome is defined by the relationship to the substance, not the dose.

Two indicators is a low bar and students should notice that — it returns in the caffeine slide as a live argument.

Class count and the caffeine exception: substance-use-disorder (Diagnosis) and alcohol-use-disorder (Diagnosis). DSM-5 also dropped "addiction" as a diagnostic term for its vague definition and stigmatising overtones — clinicians still use it for severe presentations.

Three diagnoses per class — nearly

Which boxes are empty?

Each class can carry a use disorder (the pattern) plus intoxication and withdrawal (the states). Intoxication and withdrawal are states; use disorder is the diagnosis — you can have either state without the disorder.

The last gap is the instructive one. Inhalant withdrawal is clinically documented — nausea, tremor, sweating, seizures, much like alcohol withdrawal — and the manual does not name it. The absence of a criterion is a claim about the world, and it can be wrong.

Show the grid with the four gaps hidden and ask: "four of these boxes are empty — which?" Reveal row by row. Caffeine and tobacco are the ones students get wrong in opposite directions: everyone assumes caffeine has a use disorder and that you can be "intoxicated" on nicotine.

Guide: substance-related-and-addictive-disorders (states vs diagnosis); caffeine-related-disorders; tobacco-use-disorder (Presentation); hallucinogen-related-disorders (Diagnosis); inhalant-use-disorder (Diagnosis). Last year's deck said DSM-5 includes caffeine use disorder — it sits in Section III, which is the point of the next caffeine slide.

The mechanism, and its honest limits

The reward account

The mesocorticolimbic dopamine pathway, with opioid peptides, GABA, serotonin and endocannabinoids in supporting roles: substances hijack the machinery that reinforces survival behaviour.

Your Guide flags its own source here: no imaging, lesion or human neurochemical evidence is cited, and the source contradicts itself on which way the neurotransmitters move.

The behavioural account needs no such caution: positive reinforcement while intoxicated, negative reinforcement twice over — relief of distress, and relief of withdrawal. Withdrawal makes stopping punishing and use reinforcing.

Two strands complete the account. The conditioning reaches the cues — places, people, expectancy itself moves behaviour, which is why craving outlives detox. And who gets caught is not random: vulnerability is roughly half heritable, and early first use multiplies the risk. Diathesis–stress, again.

Lecture 1's operant machinery carrying a whole chapter. The withdrawal-as-negative-reinforcement loop is the third self-sustaining cycle of the term — invite the room to name the other two (panic, insomnia).

The cue-conditioning line pays off in relapse prevention: the environment holds the triggers, which is why recovery so often requires moving the life, not just stopping the drug.

Why the high shrinks and the crash grows

Opponent-process theory

Solomon & Corbit (1974): any strong affective state recruits an opposing process that outlasts it. The drug's high (the a-process) is followed by its mirror image (the b-process).

With repetition the b-process grows stronger and faster while the a-process stays fixed. One change, two symptoms: tolerance (the crash eats more of the high) and withdrawal (stop the drug and the crash stands alone).

The endpoint it predicts: using not to feel good, but to stop feeling bad — the negative-reinforcement loop from the last slide, given a mechanism and a timeline.

Before revealing the right panel, ask: "if the drug's effect on the brain hasn't changed, why does the regular user feel less of it?" Take two answers, then reveal: because the opposing process arrives sooner and bigger and cancels more of it.

Guide: substance-use-disorder, "Opponent-process theory" section (added 2026-09-03, after the August audit — unaudited but uncontroversial). Last year's deck had the "spring" metaphor for withdrawal in its notes; this is the citable version of that idea.

Withdrawal runs opposite to the drug

The alcohol withdrawal clock

Chronic alcohol boosts GABA's inhibition; the brain compensates with fewer GABA receptors and more NMDA (glutamate) receptors. Stop abruptly and the compensation is unmasked — excitation with nothing damping it.

The most dangerous complication comes last. A patient who looks stable at twelve hours is not past the risk — often someone admitted for something else, who has stopped drinking because they are in hospital.

Untreated, delirium tremens carries a mortality compared to severe malignant disease; with early detection and treatment, about 1% or less. Benzodiazepines are the only class with demonstrated superiority for seizure prevention.

Ask before the reveal: "you've just admitted a heavy drinker for a broken leg. When is the danger?" Most will say "right away". Reveal DT at day two to three.

Guide: alcohol-use-disorder ("Alcohol withdrawal syndrome and delirium tremens" — timeline table, mortality, benzodiazepines); substance-withdrawal (Etiology — the GABA/NMDA compensation, and the scope note on stimulants). Last year's DTs line gets its clock here.

Do not quote the incidence pair (about 8% withdrawal, up to 15% DT or seizures): it is audit-flagged on both pages, since a subset cannot exceed its superset. The CIWA-Ar "ten items" description is also flagged.

Intoxication

The dose does not define the syndrome

Blood alcohol and what it typically looks like:

Serum concentrationPicture
under 50 mg/dLimpaired skills, more talk, relaxation
over 100 mg/dLataxia, impaired judgement, slurred speech
over 200 mg/dLamnesia, double vision, vomiting
over 400 mg/dLrespiratory depression, coma, death

Now the lethal dose in a non-tolerant drinker: roughly 300 mg/dL — below the band listed for death. A tolerant drinker can be coherent at a level that would kill someone without tolerance. "How much did they take?" is a weaker question than it sounds.

And the antidote list is one line long: naloxone, for opioids. Alcohol, stimulants, cannabis, hallucinogens, solvents — nothing to give. Opioid intoxication is a respiratory event, and breathing slows progressively even at therapeutic doses.

Ask: "what blood alcohol level kills?" Let the room read the table and say 400. Then reveal 300. The resolution is tolerance: the table describes drinkers in general; the lethal figure is for someone without tolerance.

Guide: substance-intoxication (Etiology — the Baldacara et al. 2024 table and the lethal-dose contrast; Treatment — the antidote table). Management of intoxication is emergency medicine, not psychiatry — a live classification question on that page. Stay away from the page's Istanbul emergency-department composition figures (the "largest block" sentence is audit-flagged).

Last year's blood-alcohol-by-drinks chart (Wilkinson et al., 1977) is not in the Guide; this table replaces it.

The class that arrives by prescription

Benzodiazepines: the harm rose, the misuse didn't

United States, over roughly two decades:

MeasureChange
Benzodiazepine prescriptions+67%

Prevalence of misusestable

If misuse didn't rise, what did? More medication in circulation, and more co-prescribing with opioids. Alone, benzodiazepines rarely kill; with an opioid prescription added, the risk of drug-related death is nearly 15-fold.

Where misused pills come from: friends or family with prescriptions, 64.4%. Why people misuse them: over 75% to manage sleep, tension or distress — the conditions the drugs are prescribed for. And fewer than 2% of people prescribed a benzodiazepine escalate to high doses.

Hide the third row and ask: "prescriptions up two-thirds, deaths up fivefold — what happened to misuse?" Almost everyone predicts it rose. The stable line is a clue about mechanism, not a contradiction.

Guide: sedative-hypnotic-anxiolytic-related-disorders (Epidemiology — the trends list and sources of supply; Etiology — the 15-fold combination risk; Presentation — under 2% escalate). Avoid the page's lipophilicity/half-life rule: it is audit-flagged because its own example, diazepam, contradicts it. Last year's CIHI/CTADS Canadian sedative figures are not in the Guide.

The substance in your cup

Caffeine: two diagnoses and a question

Intoxication — a diagnosis
Withdrawal — a diagnosis
Use disorder — Section III, a condition for further study

Withdrawal starts after about 24 hours without it. In 618 adults: headache 35.9%, fatigue 35.4%, poor concentration 30.3%.

One mechanism explains both. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors — it removes a brake rather than pressing an accelerator. Regular use grows more receptors: less alertness per cup (tolerance), and on stopping, more sensitivity to adenosine than before you ever started (withdrawal).

In one adult sample, 95% used caffeine daily and 19.5% met the proposed use-disorder criteria. Either a vast untreated problem — or criteria catching ordinary consumption. That doubt is why it stays in Section III.

And the withdrawal is partly belief: decaf that people thought was caffeinated reduced symptoms, and being told the dose was cut produced them.

Show of hands first: who has had caffeine today? Then: "does anyone here have a disorder?" The two-indicator threshold from the opening slide is the reason 19.5% is plausible and the reason it is suspect.

Guide: caffeine-related-disorders (Presentation, Etiology, Treatment, Contested). The samples are Bodur et al. (n = 618) and Abdoli et al. (Iran, n = 1,228), both from tea-dominant caffeine cultures — say so; Canadian intake is coffee-weighted. Published estimates of the use disorder range 8–20%, a spread that points at the instrument.

Stimulants, close to home

Study drugs

17% of US college students report misusing stimulant medication. Why? Rank the motives before the reveal.

  1. Academic performance — the most common
  2. Self-medicating suspected, undiagnosed ADHD
  3. Weight loss and staying awake
  4. Euphoria, or boosting alcohol — and last, a minority motive: getting high

Three different things, often blurred: misuse (a therapeutic drug used wrongly — much the commonest), abuse (non-therapeutic use for the effect), diversion (giving or selling it).

Does treating ADHD with stimulants cause addiction later? The most recent meta-analysis: neither increased nor decreased risk. Swedish registry data: substance abuse lower with treatment, and lower still with longer treatment.

Ask the room to rank the four motives in pairs for thirty seconds. Most put "getting high" first; it comes last. The moral-panic framing predicts the wrong order.

Guide: stimulant-use-disorder (Epidemiology — the 17%, which the module gives without its own citation; Contested — Newcorn 2025 on motives, misuse/abuse/diversion and the ADHD-treatment evidence). Speed is part of the hook: immediate-release formulations face the most misuse pressure, and crack peaks in 3–5 minutes against 20–30 for cocaine (substance-intoxication). Adults with ADHD carry substance use disorder rates two to three times the general population's — the impulse-control link for part three.

The gateway argument

First is not cause

65% of people who use illicit drugs say cannabis was their first. Does that make it a gateway?

It is what you would expect of the most available illicit drug whatever its causal role. Of roughly 20 million regular US users, about 10% develop dependence — most never progress even to a cannabis use disorder, let alone to other drugs.

To show a gateway you would need what the cited evidence lacks: longitudinal or genetically informed designs that separate cause from common liability — shared genes, peers, availability.

The better-supported adolescent concern is different: heavy use during a critical period of brain development, and its cognitive effects — an argument for prevention that does not need the gateway claim.

Legal in Canada since 2018 — which changes the population, the prevalence and the policy context this material describes. The Guide's US source cannot supply the Canadian picture.

Ask: "what study would convince you cannabis causes later drug use?" Collect designs; the answer the page wants is a design that rules out common liability. Callback to correlation and third variables from L2.

Guide: cannabis-as-gateway-drug; cannabis-related-disorders (Epidemiology, Etiology, Contested). Avoid the page's "highest prevalence of any substance abused" line — audit-flagged, since alcohol is a substance too; the qualifier is probably "illicit". Last year's 15% Canadian past-year figure is not in the Guide. The ~10% dependence figure is used by both camps — low harm, or two million people affected.

The most hopeful evidence in the course, and the least known. Most people with substance use disorders get better, most without formal treatment. Say it twice.

Canada, now

The toxic drug crisis in four numbers

  • 56,631 opioid toxicity deaths since 2016 (national surveillance)
  • Deaths falling 23% — while EMS responses rise 9%
  • 70% of deaths also involved a stimulant — most opioid deaths are now polysubstance deaths

Read the middle pair together: fewer deaths, more emergencies. Something is keeping more overdoses from becoming deaths — which is what naloxone distribution and supervised consumption are for.

And keep the quieter number beside the crisis: alcohol use disorder — 76.3 million people worldwide, 1.8 million deaths a year. The emergency is opioids; the baseline harm is legal, and in this room.

The falling-deaths/rising-EMS divergence is the analytically interesting fact — a measurement story, not just a tragedy story. It sets up Insite after the break.

Do not quote the page's 2025 death total or its fentanyl-versus-analogue split: the page gives two different totals (5,608 and 5,630) and the fentanyl pair is flagged. Part two

Treatment — and the argument about what treatment is

What works best is barely used

Contingency management

Vouchers or prizes for verified abstinence. Operant conditioning, applied with a straight face.

It is the best-supported psychosocial treatment in this literature — and it is rarely disseminated.

Why? Paying people not to use offends intuitions about virtue. The evidence and the intuition point in opposite directions, and the intuition is winning.

Ask the room why it feels wrong before giving the dissemination fact. This is the Rush/tranquilizing-chair question from L1 pointed at ourselves.

The debate with a body count

Methadone: treatment or substitution?

The objection: an agonist maintains dependence. One drug has been swapped for another.

The reply: maintenance stabilises life, cuts overdose death and disease transmission, permits eventual taper — and the dependence that remains is to a measured, legal, survivable dose.

The Canadian guideline goes further: buprenorphine–naloxone first-line, strong recommendation, high-quality evidence — and withdrawal management alone should be avoided, because detox without maintenance elevates death risk.

"Detox alone is contraindicated" is the guideline fact students least expect. GRADE columns from L2 doing real work.

The rest of the toolkit

Three pharmacological logics, one social one

Agonistsubstitute a safer, measured version — methadone, the nicotine patch
Antagonistblock the receptor so the drug does nothing — naltrexone
Aversivemake use punishing — disulfiram, which turns alcohol into instant illness

And the one everyone has heard of: AA and the twelve steps — reach no clinical service matches, and an evidence base complicated by anonymity and self-selection.

Sit that beside contingency management: the best-evidenced treatment is barely used, and the most-used is the hardest to evaluate.

The three logics are the exam vocabulary (Guide: agonist-and-antagonist-medications, aversion-therapy, twelve-step-programs). The closing contrast is the slide's real content — it reprises the CM point and gives the room a sentence they will repeat.

Reading a treatment effect

"Fifty percent better" — for whom?

Nicotine replacement: 133 trials, 64,640 people, RR 1.55, high-certainty evidence. Every licensed form beats control. How many extra quitters per 1,000?

Same relative risk, nearly four times the absolute benefit. What a "50% increase" is worth depends on the quit rate you start from.

And read the intervals, not the point estimates: sprays look best but rest on a handful of trials; patch and gum, with 48,000 participants between them, are the estimates to lean on.

Ask for a number before the reveal — most will say "fifty". The answer is "it depends": 22 or 82. This is the L2 effect-size lesson on the treatment with the best evidence base in the chapter.

Guide: tobacco-use-disorder (Treatment — Hartmann-Boyce et al., 2018 Cochrane review). Do not quote the page's "NNT roughly 11 to 56": it is audit-flagged because the page's own table implies about 12 to 46. Last year's 15% Canadian smoking rate is not in the Guide — the page says Canadian prevalence data are insufficient.

A category on the move

Gambling sits with the substances now

DSM-5 moved gambling disorder into the addictions chapter — the first behaviour to be classified with the substances.

  • Same reward-pathway story, same tolerance-like escalation, same relief loop
  • Internet gaming disorder waits in Section III — "conditions for further study" — while ICD-11 already lists it

Forty years ago gambling was an impulse-control problem; today it is an addiction. Same behaviour, re-theorised — a category being argued about in real time.

Callback to PMDD's promotion (L5) and the OCD chapter's assembly (L3): the manual's edges move, and students have now watched it happen three times.

Gambling disorder, clinically

One in ten

Only about 10% of people with gambling disorder ever seek treatment.

The ones who do are a severe group: in one treatment-seeking sample, 32% had experienced suicidal ideation and 17% had attempted suicide at least once.

No drug is approved for it anywhere. Opioid antagonists — naltrexone, nalmefene — are the best-supported, for a small but significant benefit. Psychological treatment is the most effective option: CBT with motivational interviewing, built around normative feedback — your own gambling, usually underestimated, set against what people actually do.

Why so few come? Not knowing treatment exists, and fear of the stigma of a psychiatric diagnosis. The reclassification that made it a recognised disorder may also have raised the barrier to asking for help.

Ask: "why might nine in ten people with a recognised disorder never seek help?" Then reveal the two named barriers. Treatment-seekers are selected for severity, so every clinical figure describes a tenth of the affected population.

Guide: gambling-disorder (Epidemiology, Treatment, Contested). Do not quote the 30.2% twelve-month attempt figure — audit-flagged as implausible beside the 17% lifetime figure. The "social problems" protective factor is also flagged. Last year's "2% of Americans" lifetime figure: the Guide's only prevalence is a DSM-III-R-era 2–3% of adults. Impulse control

The chapter of outward expression

DSM chapter 15, reunited

Problems expressed outward — behaviour that violates others' rights or collides with social norms.

Intermittent explosive disorderrecurrent outbursts wildly out of proportion to provocation
Kleptomaniatension → theft of unneeded objects → relief
Pyromaniathe same loop, with fire
Oppositional defiant disorderangry, argumentative, vindictive — ≥4 of 8 symptoms, 6 months

Note the tension-relief loop appearing again in kleptomania and pyromania — the chapter's kinship with the addictions is visible in its structure.

Impulsive, not instrumental

Intermittent explosive disorder

Recurrent outbursts out of proportion to the provocation — and aimed at nothing: not money, not power, not intimidation. Not diagnosed under age 6.

Compared withWhat separates it
Conduct disorderCD's aggression is instrumental, and CD has theft, deceit and truancy; IED has none of those
ADHDboth impulsive, but serious aggression belongs to IED — and IED lacks the sustained-attention problems

US: 2.6% in a year, 4.0% lifetime. Where it co-occurs with PTSD, lifetime suicide attempts reach 41% — and trauma in the first 20 years of life is a named risk factor for IED itself.

No medication exists specifically for it; CBT with relaxation training is the psychological treatment. And in court, the "irresistible impulse" test looks made for this diagnosis — yet it is rarely used, because the law treats "could not stop" with the scepticism the diagnosis invites.

Case to put to the room: a student throws his phone against a wall after losing an online match, three times this year, and feels ashamed afterwards. IED? Draw out "not instrumental" and "out of proportion" — and what you would still need to know.

Guide: intermittent-explosive-disorder (Presentation, Diagnosis, Epidemiology, Etiology, Treatment, Contested). Two audit flags to avoid: the frequency criterion as written ("twice a week … for up to three months" reads a minimum as a ceiling), and "treatment can be highly effective if started early", which rests on a Psychology Today source. Last year's "lifetime prevalence 7%" is not the Guide's figure — use 4.0%.

Where the evidence runs out

Pyromania barely exists in the literature

Fire-setting the behaviour: about 1% lifetime, strongly associated with antisocial personality.

Pyromania the diagnosis: the literature search returned essentially nothing.

And kleptomania's pharmacology is four neurotransmitter systems with no integrated model — SSRIs paradoxical, naltrexone effective, nobody knows quite why.

These are the chapters where your Guide's declared gaps run thickest — and where a well-sourced student contribution genuinely moves the state of the course's knowledge.

Honest poverty of evidence, presented as opportunity. Amber 2 is due Nov 27; these pages have claimable gaps.

A contrast worth a sentence: kleptomania's clinic sample is 71.4% female, and 63.4% had been arrested for shoplifting; fire-setters are 82.1% male, half meet criteria for antisocial personality disorder and 71.7% for alcohol use disorder (kleptomania, pyromania pages). Two diagnoses defined by a missing motive, with opposite sex ratios.
