Part one

Measurement

The two words

Reliable and valid are not the same thing

Reliability is consistency. Two clinicians agree; the same person scores the same on Tuesday and Friday.

Validity is whether the thing measures what it claims to.

A reliable instrument is not automatically a valid one.

You can engineer agreement by tightening the criteria — without ever establishing that the category carves nature at a joint.

This is the single most important distinction in the lecture and it comes back in Part two, when we look at what the DSM did to raise reliability. Make them say it back.

A worked example

My bathroom scale is extremely reliable

It reads 8 kg heavy. Every single time.

Perfect test–retest reliability. Zero validity.

Now the uncomfortable version: a diagnostic category two clinicians always agree on, that does not correspond to anything real, behaves exactly like this scale.

Cheap example, lands every time. Then push into the uncomfortable version and leave it there — you are setting up the DSM field-trial material after the first break.

The one they misuse

Face validity is not evidence

A questionnaire whose items are obviously about worry looks like it measures anxiety.

The textbook is explicit: face validity is an impression — not a statistical or evidence-based form of validity.

And yet it is what makes a test look credible to a client, an insurer, or a court. The least evidential form of validity is the most persuasive one in the room.

Worth a beat. Students often assume "looks right" is a weak version of "is right" rather than a different thing entirely.

The method paradox

The most-used assessment is the least reliable

This is a trade-off, not a solved problem. Structure buys agreement and spends the clinician's freedom to follow what seems clinically important.

Ask which they would want if they were the patient. Rooms split, and both answers are defensible — that is the point.

Part two

The manual

What a classification does

Three jobs, and the third one is awkward

  1. A shared language, so one clinician's "major depressive disorder" is recognisably another's
  2. Counting — every prevalence figure depends on a category existing
  3. Billing. Insurers require diagnostic codes to pay claims

Part of the reason the categories look the way they do is administrative, not scientific. Once a category is a billing code, there are incentives to diagnose within the system's vocabulary regardless of fit.

Do not present this cynically — present it as a fact the manual itself lists among its functions. The cynical reading is available to them without your help.

The manual on itself

A strikingly modest self-description

DSM-5-TR describes itself as a historically determined cognitive schema imposed on clinical and scientific information to make it more usable.

APA (2022), p. 11

Not a map of natural kinds. An organising device, admitted to be one, in a book routinely treated as a taxonomy of real things.

Read the quote slowly and let it sit before the reveal. Students who have been told the DSM is "the bible of psychiatry" find this genuinely destabilising, which is useful.

The reliability question, answered

How much do two clinicians actually agree?

The DSM-5 field trials tested exactly this. The number that gets quoted:

Roughly 40% of diagnoses examined did not reach the cutoff for acceptable inter-rater agreement.

Conway et al. (2019), characterising the field trials

Read that citation carefully. It is a secondary characterisation offered while arguing for an alternative classification — not the primary trial report. Treat the exact proportion as indicative, not exact.

The direction is what matters, and it bears on every disorder page you will read this term.

Model the move you want from them: quote the number, then interrogate where it came from, then say what survives the interrogation. This is precisely what a Field Guide contribution has to do.

If not categories, then what

Four challengers — and they disagree with each other

The fault line in the reveal is the thing to teach. "Dimensional approaches" is a label that hides a real disagreement about what a disorder is.

Anyone writing a Field Guide contribution on classification needs this slide.

The one students get wrong

RDoC is not a replacement manual

It is a framework the US National Institute of Mental Health built to organise and fund research.

  • There are no disorder thresholds anywhere in it — it studies full-range variation instead of defining a cut-point
  • Its author states plainly that DSM diagnoses will be needed for records and insurance for the foreseeable future

Why it was built is institutional, not scientific: the DSM had become the gatekeeper for research funding, so the system whose validity was in question was also deciding which research might settle the question.

Flag this as an exam-grade misconception. If a student writes "RDoC replaces the DSM" they have misread the entire project.

The most radical one

Network theory: nothing behind the symptoms

Insomnia produces fatigue. Fatigue impairs concentration. Impaired concentration produces failure and self-reproach. Self-reproach disturbs sleep.

No latent depression required. The pathology is the set of interactions.

And a prediction falls out of it: comorbidity is not measurement error to be fixed with better categories. Bridge symptoms — insomnia sits in the depression, anxiety and PTSD networks alike — let activation spread from one diagnostic neighbourhood to the next.

Draw the loop on the board as you say it; four arrows, thirty seconds. It is the clearest possible contrast with the medical model and students remember the drawing.

Part three

The survival kit

Scope

Enough to read the rest of the course

We do research design properly in Lecture 6, when your own trial starts, and appraise it in Lecture 11 at the debrief.

Today: four ideas you need before the next nine weeks of "this treatment works."

Say the forward plan out loud. Students who know a proper methods block is coming stop panicking about the compressed version.

Idea 1

Two out of seventeen

For two decades, a treatment counted as "well established" if supported by two independently conducted studies.

So: two studies find it works. Five find it no better than placebo. Ten find it worse than placebo.

It still qualifies.

Tolin and colleagues, who point this out, add: "This is not a hypothetical scenario."

Tolin, McKay, Forman, Klonsky & Thombs (2015), Clin Psychol Sci Pract 22(4)

Give the numbers one at a time and let the room do the arithmetic out loud. Someone always says "wait, that's two out of seventeen" — that is the moment.

The fix they propose is systematic review plus GRADE, which is why guideline tables report recommendation strength separately from evidence quality.

Idea 2

Separate the effect from the evidence

Strength of the effect. How much does it help?

Strength of the evidence. How sure are we?

These are independent.

A large effect can rest on weak evidence. A well-evidenced treatment can have a small effect.

Any summary that collapses them — "evidence-based", "well established" — has thrown away what the reader needs.

This is the most portable idea in the lecture. It reappears in every guideline table in the Field Guide, and it is why the benzodiazepine deprescribing recommendation can be Strong on Low-quality evidence without contradiction.

Idea 3

A null result is not a finding of equivalence

Thirty patients per arm. No significant difference. The authors conclude the treatments are "equally effective."

They have not shown that. A superiority trial that finds nothing has failed to detect a difference — which is not the same as having shown there is none.

Confident superiority claims need roughly 50+ per arm for a medium effect. Equivalence trials need several times more.

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

Tie back to Lecture 1's diathesis-stress slide if it helps: underpowered nulls are why "no difference between therapies" claims are so common and so often unwarranted.

Idea 4

Who ran the study, and what did not get published

Allegiance. Different reviews of the same literature reach dramatically different conclusions, and study outcomes correlate with the first author's commitments.

Note the careful formulation the source uses: a risk factor empirically associated with bias — not a verdict on any individual study.

The file drawer. Small trials with negative results tend not to get published, so a literature of many small studies overstates the true effect.

In the depression literature, recovering the unpublished studies abolished psychodynamic therapy's apparent inferiority. Bias can manufacture differences in either direction.

The second small-print point is the one that surprises people: publication bias is usually taught as "makes treatments look better than they are", and here it made one look worse.
