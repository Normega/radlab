Part one

When the event is part of the diagnosis

What makes this chapter unusual

Elsewhere the manual describes states. Here it describes responses.

Every disorder in the trauma- and stressor-related chapter requires an identifiable stressor. That requirement is what defines the grouping.

The disorders are then separated by three questions: how severe was the stressor, how long has the response lasted, and what kind of response is it?

The chapter's other answers to those questions: adjustment disorders (a milder stressor), prolonged grief disorder (Lecture 1's grieving husband, now with the diagnosis DSM gates at twelve months), and in neglected children reactive attachment disorder and disinhibited social engagement disorder. Each gets a slide before the break.

A caution for accuracy: the Field Guide currently overstates this as the ONLY chapter where an external event is part of the diagnosis — substance-related disorders also require exposure. That's a flagged finding awaiting correction, so teach the framing, not the exclusivity claim.

The family-tree line closes Lecture 1's promise to "do prolonged grief properly in Lecture 4" — say the callback out loud.

The gate before the symptoms

What counts as the event

Actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence — reached by one of four routes:

  • it happened to you
  • you witnessed it, in person
  • you learned it happened to a close family member or friend
  • repeated exposure to its details through your work — first responders, emergency physicians

Watching distressing news coverage is not a route. The fourth route is why the diagnosis reaches the paramedic who was never in danger herself.

Ask before revealing: "which of these would you have guessed?" Most people guess the first two and not the fourth.

All four routes are on the posttraumatic-stress-disorder page, Presentation. The media exclusion is worth saying out loud because students assume it counts.

The response itself

What PTSD looks like

Four clusters, and the diagnosis needs all four:

  • Re-experiencing — intrusive memories, nightmares, flashbacks in which the event is happening now
  • Avoidance — of reminders: places, conversations, thoughts
  • Negative shifts in mood and thought — numbing, detachment, distorted blame of self or others
  • Hyperarousal — startle, vigilance, disturbed sleep, irritability

Every vignette in this chapter runs on these four. The clocks on the next slide only tell you what to call them.

Keep the phenomenology respectful and brief — the flashback's present-tense quality ("is happening now", not "is remembered") is the one detail worth slowing down for, and it sets up the exposure logic later: treatment turns an event that is happening into one that happened.

Same content, different clocks

Acute stress disorder or PTSD?

They can never be diagnosed together — the windows don't overlap. After 30 days ASD either becomes PTSD or remits.

And note the floor: distress in the first three days is not a disorder. Acute distress after trauma is normative and usually self-limiting.

ASD also counts differently: nine of fourteen symptoms, in any mix — no cluster is required, as it is for PTSD. About half go on to PTSD, yet many with PTSD never met ASD criteria.

The floor is the part students skip and the part that matters ethically — the criteria are built so that ordinary recovery is not pathologised.

The gap that needs explaining

Most trauma-exposed people do not develop PTSD

A majority of Canadians experience a traumatic event. Only a small minority develop the disorder.

So the question is not only "why does trauma cause PTSD?"

It is "why do most exposed people not develop it?" — which is diathesis-stress with protective factors, exactly as in Lecture 1.

Deliberately no numbers on this slide: the Guide's PTSD prevalence figures currently don't reconcile with each other and are flagged for correction. The conceptual point stands without them, so make it qualitatively and don't quote a rate.

Scar, or something that was there first?

The twin who never went to war

PTSD comes with a smaller hippocampus. The obvious reading: trauma shrank it.

And exposure is not one thing either: more severe, longer and more interpersonal trauma predicts more PTSD — a dose–response.

Before the reveal, ask: "if trauma shrank it, what should the unexposed twin's hippocampus look like?" Normal. Then reveal: it wasn't. Gilbertson et al. (2002), on the PTSD page's dose–response section.

This is the course's cleanest twin design settling a direction-of-causation question that imaging alone cannot. Two answers to "why don't most exposed people develop PTSD?": what they brought to it, and how bad it was.

If you want the contrast: last year's slide said "damaged hippocampus". The twin study is why that wording is now too strong.

The rest of the chapter · 1

Adjustment disorder: an ordinary stressor, out of proportion

A job loss, a divorce, a diagnosis — within the past three months — and distress out of proportion to it, or real impairment.

No symptom list. No count, no clusters — unlike PTSD and ASD. The presentation is named by specifier instead: with depressed mood, with anxiety, with disturbance of conduct, or mixed.

And it yields: if the full criteria for another disorder are met — a major depressive episode, say — that diagnosis takes precedence.

Defined mostly by what it is not — yet it is the seventh most-used diagnosis among psychiatrists worldwide, and it carries a suicide risk around twelve times the general population's.

The route question for the midterm: it is the nature of the stressor, not the symptom count, that sends a case here rather than to ASD or PTSD. Laid off, no life threat, tearful and sleepless six weeks later: adjustment.

All of this is on the adjustment-disorders page (Diagnosis, Contested). Avoid the page's Etiology dichotomy about proximity versus prior experience — it is flagged.

The rest of the chapter · 2

Prolonged grief disorder

Intense yearning for, or preoccupation with, someone who died — plus at least three more: part of oneself has died, disbelief, numbness, meaninglessness, intense loneliness.

12 months

since the death, in DSM-5-TR

6 months

in ICD-11 — same phenomenon, half the wait

Does it medicalise love? Only 7–10% of bereaved people meet criteria. Nine in ten are outside it.

The best evidence it is not just depression: grief-focused CBT relieves the grief; interpersonal therapy and nortriptyline relieve bereavement-related depression and leave the grief untouched.

Say the callback: Lecture 1's grieving husband, and the promise to do prolonged grief properly here.

The treatment dissociation is the argument to land — a treatment that separates two conditions is better evidence of their distinctness than a criteria set that asserts it. All on the prolonged-grief-disorder page. Stay away from the page's addiction-model paragraph; it is flagged.

The rest of the chapter · 3

Same neglect, opposite children

The history can't tell them apart — only the behaviour can. And the history is necessary, nowhere near sufficient: fewer than 10% of severely neglected children develop either.

Disinhibited behaviour often outlasts a stable placement and the other signs of neglect. Reactive attachment vs autism: was there neglect, and are there restricted, repetitive behaviours?

Ask what the sibling raised in the same home should look like. Most likely: fine. That is diathesis–stress again, the same shape as trauma and PTSD.

From the reactive-attachment-disorder and disinhibited-social-engagement-disorder pages. Williams syndrome is the other named differential for the disinhibited child — disinhibited with no neglect history points away from the attachment diagnosis. Treatment targets the caregiver, not the child alone.

A treatment that doesn't work

Psychological debriefing

Get to survivors within 72 hours. Walk them through the facts, the thoughts, the feelings. Normalise the reaction. Arrange support. It is intuitive, humane, and it was everywhere.

Research across natural disasters, burns and war routinely finds it does not reduce posttraumatic symptoms or shorten recovery.

One account of why: it may encourage rumination on the event and the symptoms, maintaining them.

Neither WHO nor NICE lists it among recommended treatments. And yet the movement has been to add structure and training rather than to stop.

This is the course's cleanest case of a plausible intervention that fails, and it connects straight to Lecture 2's "treatments that harm". Ask why it survives — the answer, that it feels wrong to do nothing, is worth sitting with.

What does work

Face it, on purpose, with help

  • Trauma-focused CBT and exposure-based therapy — the best-evidenced route: revisiting the memory and its meaning until it becomes an event that happened rather than one that is happening
  • EMDR — exposure with eye movements added — only three of its eight steps involve them; the rest is exposure and CBT. It works; whether the eye movements add anything beyond the exposure is a live debate — and a claimable Guide gap

Note the shape: the treatment that fails soothes immediately. The ones that work ask the person to approach.

The contrast with the previous slide is the pedagogy — intuitive-and-fails against counterintuitive-and-works, the same inversion as L3's exposure logic. The Guide carries all three pages: trauma-focused-cognitive-behavioral-therapy, eye-movement-desensitization-and-reprocessing, and the emdr-versus-trauma-focused-cbt debate page.

Part two

Dissociation

The construct

Something that should be joined up is not

Dissociative amnesiamemory — access to your own history
Dissociative identity disorderidentity — two or more distinct states
Depersonalization/derealizationperception of self and world

Three disorders, one idea: a disruption in the normally integrated functions of consciousness, memory, identity and perception. What differs is what comes apart.

Amnesia itself comes in grains — localised to the event, selective within it, rarely generalised to a whole life — and its strangest form is fugue: unplanned travel, identity left behind.

Keep this fast — the table does the work. The interesting material is the next two slides.

Fugue deserves thirty spoken seconds — a concrete case travels well, and the Guide's dissociative-amnesia page carries the subtypes.

Dissociative amnesia

Stored, not lost

The memory was encoded and stored. What fails is getting it back — a retrieval failure, and potentially reversible. That separates it from amnesias with a neurological cause, which are usually permanent.

An elegant mirror — resting on one study. The whole imaging literature is 22 studies and 49 patients, with one prospective controlled design.

Localized amnesia is the commonest subtype; generalized is extremely rare. The retrieval-failure framing is on the dissociative-amnesia page, Presentation; the over-suppression mirror and the size of the evidence base are in its Contested section (Taib et al., 2023).

The fugue anecdote from last year's deck — the CBC Doc Project story of the Toronto firefighter — fits here if you have two minutes.

The boundary problem

Half of adults have had one

~50%

of adults report at least one lifetime episode of depersonalization or derealization

~1–2%

one-month prevalence of the full disorder (UK)

Dissociative experience is common. It is not, by itself, disorder.

Sleep deprivation and substances produce it. What makes it clinical is persistence and impairment — and if there's an identifiable stressor with the wider stress-disorder picture, that diagnosis takes precedence instead.

Reliable laugh: ask who has ever driven somewhere and not remembered the drive. Then make the serious point — the experience is ordinary, the disorder is not, and the difference is not the experience's presence.

What makes it not psychosis

Reality testing stays intact

She watches herself from outside her body. Familiar rooms look artificial, like a set. She is frightened she is going mad.

She knows it is not real. That is the distinguishing feature.

One slide, one distinction, move on. Students conflate these constantly and this is the fix.

Before the argument · what it actually looks like

DID is usually quiet

Two or more distinct personality states — or an experience of possession — plus recurrent gaps in memory for everyday events.

Possession-form presentations are obvious. Most others are covert: a changed name, handwriting, accent, wardrobe. Where identities can't be seen, they are inferred from breaks in the sense of self and agency, plus the amnesias.

Not the switching of films. And not culture: possession states that are a normal part of an accepted cultural or religious practice are excluded by the criteria.

Students arrive with the film version. Take it away before Exercise A, so they argue about the real presentation.

From the dissociative-identity-disorder page, Presentation and Diagnosis. Community prevalence is about 1.5% of US adults. Sudden switches are usually set off by a significant stressor.

Exercise A · 18 minutes · groups of three

The DID argument

Two accounts of the same disorder. Neither side has a knockout.

Trauma model. Repeated childhood abuse in a child without coping resources produces separate identities as an escape. DID patients show the highest rate of childhood trauma of any psychiatric disorder.

Sociocultural model. Media portrayals and suggestive clinical technique teach the role. Diagnosed cases rose sharply after Sybil was published.

In threes: take a side you don't hold. Five minutes to build the strongest version. Then say what evidence would change your mind.

radlab.zone/class/psy240

Insist they argue the side they don't hold — it is the entire pedagogical value.

Two epidemiological facts worth dropping into the debrief (both on the DID Guide page): the diagnosis shows strong female predominance in clinical samples, and the identities typically first form in childhood even when diagnosis comes decades later.

Bring it back with the honest position: the case-count rise after Sybil fits social learning AND improved recognition equally well, trauma histories here are retrospective and self-reported, and no arbitrating study is cited. Then the sharp bit: DSM's own Criterion D excludes culturally sanctioned possession states, which concedes that identity-alteration can be socially constituted. It just doesn't say where the line falls.

Part three

Symptoms in the body

Clear this up first

This chapter is not about imaginary illness

The suffering is assumed authentic.

Somatic symptom disorder no longer requires the symptoms to be medically unexplained. It can be diagnosed alongside a genuine medical condition.

The organising question is not "are the symptoms real?" It is "what is this person's relationship to their bodily symptoms?"

Say this before anything else in the chapter. The folk reading — that this is the malingering chapter — is wrong and it poisons everything that follows if left standing.

Three relationships

Where the disorder actually sits

DisorderSomatic symptoms?The disorder is in the…
Somatic symptom disorderpresent, often severaldisproportionate thoughts, feelings and behaviours about them
Illness anxiety disordernear-absentpreoccupation with having or acquiring serious illness
Functional neurological symptom disorderpresent, neurological in formincompatibility with recognised neurological disease

Same body. Three completely different clinical problems.

The middle column is what students should copy down — presence or absence of somatic symptoms is the first fork in the differential.

The reasoning error this chapter punishes

A negative workup is not a diagnosis

Seizure-like episodes. Neurology finds nothing. Is this functional neurological symptom disorder?

Not yet — and the failure to find disease never establishes it.

The criteria require positive evidence of incompatibility between the symptom and recognised neurological conditions. A positive test, not an absence.

Diagnosis by exclusion is how people get told "it's all in your head" and how real disease gets missed. The criteria were rewritten specifically to stop it.

This is the most clinically consequential slide in the lecture. Anyone going into medicine will meet this and get it wrong unless someone tells them.

What the positive test looks like

Conversion disorder, renamed — and ruled in

The old name survives in brackets: conversion disorder, from the psychoanalytic idea that unconscious conflict is "converted" into a bodily symptom.

DSM-5 dropped the requirement that a psychological stressor be found. What is left is a name asserting a mechanism the criteria no longer require.

Hoover's sign: a weak leg can't push down on request — yet pushes down normally when the person is asked to lift the other leg. A sign found, not a disease ruled out.

5–15% of neurology clinic patients, with disability comparable to epilepsy and Parkinson's.

Act Hoover's sign out if you can — lie back on the desk, or mime it standing. The weakness of hip extension resolves when attention moves to the opposite leg.

From the functional-neurological-symptom-disorder page, Presentation and Contested (Stone et al., 2024). Avoid the page's Treatment section figures for now; that section's main source isn't declared and is flagged.

An old idea you will still hear

Primary and secondary gain

Primary — internal. The symptom protects the person from anxiety or conflict they would otherwise feel directly.

Secondary — external. What follows the symptom and keeps it in place: attention, sympathy, time off, disability support.

Secondary gain looks just like reinforcement. The two models disagree about causal priority: for behaviourists the payoffs are the cause; for psychodynamic theorists they arrive only after the conflict made the symptom.

The caution: neither gain can be observed, and the language implies the person is doing it on purpose — which is the line that separates this chapter from the next slide.

From the primary-and-secondary-gain page. The causal-priority disagreement is the examinable part.

The caution is the bridge into factitious disorder: once intent to deceive is real, you are no longer in somatic symptom territory.

The one that is deliberate

Factitious disorder is the exception

Deliberate falsification of symptoms, in oneself or another, with intent to deceive — and no obvious external reward.

vs the rest of the chapter: everywhere else the suffering is authentic and unintended.

vs malingering: malingering has an external incentive — a disability claim, avoided duty. Factitious disorder has none anyone can find.

Two contrasts, both directions. The absence of external incentive is the diagnostic hinge and it is what makes the condition so strange.

The arrow runs the other way

Psychological factors affecting other medical conditions

Once called "psychosomatic". Here the medical condition is primary and real; psychological or behavioural factors worsen its course, its treatment, or the disease process itself.

  • Depression predicts early-onset coronary heart disease; it is about five times as common in heart patients
  • Irritable bowel: up to a quarter of the population, nearly half of gastroenterology referrals

vs somatic symptom disorder: there, the mental disorder is primary and the symptoms carry excessive thoughts. Here, the illness is primary and the mind is aggravating it.

From the psychological-factors-affecting-other-medical-conditions page. The structural point is what gets examined: this is the one category in the chapter whose focus is not a mental disorder.

Treatments are the familiar ones turned outward — relaxation, biofeedback, hypnosis, CBT groups for chronic illness.
