Part one

Growing up with it

What defines the chapter

Onset in the developmental period

Neurodevelopmental disorders are present as the nervous system develops — not acquired later as a decline from a previous level.

That sentence is the differential for both of today's chapters. The question is never just "what are the symptoms?" — it is "was there ever a before?"

Beyond today's three headliners the chapter also holds specific learning disorder — reading, writing or math far below age level, 5–15% of school-age children, some of them statistically in this room — plus the communication disorders and tic disorders. Guide pages, all.

Plant "was there ever a before?" now; it returns in the NCD half and again in the closing exercise.

The SLD line matters for who is sitting in the room: say it plainly and without pity, and point at accessibility services in the same breath.

Autism spectrum disorder

Two features, both required

  1. Deficits in social communication and social interaction — across contexts
  2. Restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities

Both. A child with social-communication difficulty and no restricted/repetitive pattern has, by definition, something else — which is why DSM-5 created social (pragmatic) communication disorder.

And remember Lecture 4's differential: attachment disorders require a neglect history and lack the restricted/repetitive features. History and second criterion — that's the fork.

Scale: 1–2% of the population, 3:1 male worldwide — 4:1 among Canadian diagnoses. Whether the historical rise reflects wider criteria and better recognition or a real increase is an open question the Guide declares, not one it answers.

"Is autism actually increasing?" is the question the room wants to ask. The honest answer is that the Guide gives current figures only (1–2%; 3:1 globally; 4:1 among Canadian diagnoses, Ofner et al., 2018) and lists the ascertainment-versus-incidence debate as a Needs-research gap on both the autism and chapter pages — a live amber contribution, so say that rather than settling it from the podium.

"Spectrum" deserves one careful minute: DSM-5 absorbed Asperger's disorder and PDD-NOS into one diagnosis, with severity rated separately for each criterion by the support required (Levels 1–3), and support needs range enormously. Avoid functioning-label shorthand; describe support needs instead. And the history trap: restricted/repetitive behaviours decline with age, so a history of them still satisfies criterion B.

Autism · where it comes from

Highly heritable, no single gene — and not vaccines

Twin studies put heritability at 56–95%, with identical twins more concordant than fraternal. Yet no specific genetic marker has been found, and only about 15% of cases are tied to a known mutation.

  • Brain: the most consistent finding is overgrowth of brain structures; amygdala differences; temporal-lobe underactivity during face perception
  • Around birth: older parental age, complicated childbirth, extreme prematurity, valproic acid in utero — and no single parental factor predicts autism

The vaccine claim began with Andrew Wakefield's late-1990s MMR study — which asserted only a relation, never causation. Cherry-picked data and funding bias followed: the paper was retracted and Wakefield lost his licence. Study after study since finds no relation.

The public still believes otherwise. The belief outlived the paper — evidence failing to travel.

Ask first: "Hands up if you have heard someone say vaccines cause autism." Nearly every hand. Then: "What do you think the original study claimed?" Reveal that it never claimed causation — the overgeneralisation happened in the retelling.

Guide: autism-spectrum-disorder, Etiology; neurodevelopmental-disorders, Etiology (Rao & Andrade, 2011; Dudley et al., 2018; Sheikh et al., 2018).

From the 2025 deck and not in the Guide — not examinable as stated: the "cold parenting" history, the 3–5% sibling recurrence figure, "31% have intellectual disability", and the US "1 in 68". The Guide's Canadian figure (1 in 66 children aged 5–17) is on the next slide's notes.

Autism · treatment

The best-evidenced treatment is intensive — and rationed

Early intensive behavioural intervention: 30–40 hours a week for some children, starting around age 3 or 4, for about two years. The rationale is plasticity — younger brains rewire more readily.

Its core is applied behaviour analysis: find what a behaviour is for — antecedent, behaviour, consequence — then change the environment or the reward. Everything is tracked.

Medication does not treat autism. It targets what travels with it — anxiety, hyperactivity, irritability. 70% of autistic children carry another diagnosis.

Ontario, 2019: funding tied to age and family income; some families left the province for services. At 30–40 hours a week, who can access the treatment is part of whether it works.

Ask: "If the evidence says 30–40 hours a week, who pays for it?" Then the Ontario box.

Canada: 1 in 66 children and youth aged 5–17 are diagnosed; males four times as likely to be diagnosed; more than half diagnosed by age six and more than 90% by 12 (Ofner et al., 2018).

Say plainly that a substantial neurodiversity critique of ABA exists, partly from autistic adults who received it, and that the Guide page declares it as a Needs-research gap rather than covering it — a contribution opportunity, not settled content.

Guide: autism-spectrum-disorder, Treatment and "ASD in Canada".

ADHD

Two symptom clusters, one insistent question

Inattention, and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity — six of nine symptoms in a cluster, for six months, in two or more settings. About 7.2% of children worldwide.

  • More common in males; inattentive presentations more common in females — and likelier to be missed
  • Stimulants: 70–80% of children show fewer symptoms. They can worsen tics — and alpha-2 agonists treat both

The two-settings rule is doing ethical work: a child who only "has ADHD" in one classroom may be telling you about the classroom.

The two-settings observation lands well delivered dry. It is also Lecture 2's measurement thinking: the informant is part of the instrument.

ADHD · the parenting myth

Which way does the arrow run?

Mothers of children with ADHD were less attentive and imposed more structure on play (Cunningham & Barkley, 1979). The folk reading: poor parenting causes ADHD.

And a brief interaction with an impulsive, noncompliant child was enough to raise parents' drinking. The child's behaviour drives the parenting.

Evidence that psychosocial factors cause ADHD is very weak. A lack of structure at home can worsen symptoms — worsening is not causing. Sugar: largely disproven.

Before the first reveal ask: "If parenting caused it, what should happen to the mother's behaviour when only the child is given medication?" Nothing. Instead her parenting normalised.

This is the Guide's cleanest example of a reciprocal effect mistaken for a one-way cause — and it is why parent-focused treatment working is not evidence of a parenting cause. One line worth giving families: what caused the disorder in an individual child generally does not change how it is treated.

Guide: adhd, Etiology — "The parenting myth, and why the arrow runs the other way" (Barkley & Cunningham, 1979; Pelham et al., 1997; Milich et al., 1986).

ADHD · what actually works

Medication wins the headline. Read the rest of the trial.

The MTA study (1999) had four arms: community care, intensive behavioural treatment, stimulant management, and the combination.

On core symptoms, medication was the most effective — and the combination was no better than medication alone.

  • Combined treatment: children less disruptive at school
  • With behavioural treatment: lower stimulant doses for the same outcome — and lower doses are better tolerated
  • Parents more satisfied when behavioural management was included

Complementary strengths: the best outcomes likely come from both. One headline result is not a treatment recommendation.

Closer to campus: most students who misuse prescription stimulants get them from peers with valid prescriptions. The remedies — monitoring, screening, malingering tests — also restrict access for patients who need the drug, and cost most for the least affluent.

Before the reveal: "Four arms. Which wins on core symptoms?" Most will say the combination. It ties with medication — then the bullets explain why behavioural treatment still belongs in the package.

How children are actually treated: about 30% medication only, 15% behavioural only, 32% both, 23% no treatment (CDC, 2018a).

The diversion line is a policy point about access, not about anyone in the room — keep it that way.

Guide: adhd, Treatment — "The Multimodal Treatment Study" (MTA Cooperative Group, 1999; Hinshaw et al., 2000; Pelham et al., 2005; Jensen et al., 2001); Contested for diversion (McCabe & Boyd, 2005).

Intellectual developmental disorder

Two deficits plus onset — never IQ alone

  1. Intellectual functioning — reasoning, problem-solving, abstract thinking
  2. Adaptive functioning — the practical business of daily life, against age and cultural expectations
  3. Onset in the developmental period

Severity is graded on adaptive functioning, not IQ score. A number does not tell you whether someone can live independently; adaptive assessment does.

Where it comes from: chromosomal conditions dominate — Fragile X and Down syndrome, whose extra copy of the amyloid gene brings early-onset Alzheimer's, a bridge to today's second half — plus prenatal alcohol, drugs and toxins, infection, and severe deprivation. About 1% of the population.

Recall L2's intelligence-testing critiques — cultural loading, single-dimension assumption. The adaptive-functioning pivot is the field's answer to its own psychometric history.

One sentence of history is owed here, from last year's deck: Alberta's Sexual Sterilization Act ran until 1972. Not in the Guide — not examinable as stated; the IDD page lists the eugenics era and institutionalisation as a Needs-research gap, so it is also an amber contribution waiting for a Canadian source.

Guide: intellectual-developmental-disorder (Etiology; Diagnosis — severity by adaptive functioning); neurocognitive-disorder-due-to-alzheimers-disease, Contested, for the Down syndrome / APP line.

Specific learning disorder

IQ is what you can do. Achievement is what you are doing.

IQReading scoreLearning disorder?
Child A7070No — the score is what ability predicts
Child B10070Possibly — the gap is the signal

Intervention must be tried before diagnosis — the failure to respond is part of the evidence. DSM-5 dropped the IQ–achievement discrepancy requirement, yet many still use it: two identification procedures in use at once. And an IQ at either extreme hides the weakness.

Ask before the table reveals: "Both children read at 70. Which one has a learning disorder?" You cannot tell from the reading score alone — that is the point.

Dyslexia and dyscalculia are alternative terms, not DSM diagnoses: the clinician writes "specific learning disorder with impairment in reading". Heritability about 50%; up to 10 times the risk with an affected first-degree relative. Lifelong, with a shifting array of difficulties.

The Guide's IEP/IDEA machinery is American; Canadian identification runs through provincial frameworks, and the page says so. Point students with a learning disorder to accessibility services, without ceremony.

Guide: specific-learning-disorder, Diagnosis and Treatment; neurodevelopmental-disorders, differential map.

Genetic is not unchangeable: PKU. One recessive gene; on an ordinary diet, severe intellectual disability; on a phenylalanine-restricted diet begun early, cognition develops largely normally. A wholly genetic condition with a wholly environmental treatment — and much of the reason for the newborn heel-prick test.

The same few exposures recur across nearly every row — low birth weight, prenatal substances, prematurity — which points to disrupted early development, not anything disorder-specific.

Ask the room to predict the third column before revealing it row by row: "Which of these has a medication that treats the disorder itself?" Most will guess more than one.

The behavioural core is shared: ABA, parent management training and habit reversal are all operant conditioning aimed at different targets. And every disorder in the chapter is reported as more common in males — with detection bias offered as the mechanism only for ADHD.

Guide: neurodevelopmental-disorders, Etiology and Treatment; intellectual-developmental-disorder, "PKU"; adhd, Treatment.

Part two

Declining into it

The chapter's architecture

One syndrome, graded twice, explained nine ways

  • Delirium — acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, secondary to illness or substances
  • Mild NCD — modest decline, independence preserved
  • Major NCD — substantial decline, independent functioning lost

Decline is judged across six cognitive domains — attention, executive function, learning/memory, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition — and then attributed to one of nine etiological subtypes.

The mild/major line is independence, not test score — the same adaptive-functioning move as the first half of the lecture. Point at the symmetry.

The first differential, always

Delirium is not dementia

Delirium: hours to days · fluctuates · attention collapses first · always secondary to illness, a substance or a toxin · most recover fully

Major NCD: months to years · steady · memory and domains erode · usually degenerative · rarely reversible

Delirium runs 1–2% in the community and up to 88% in end-of-life care. Miss it and you treat a reversible medical emergency as an irreversible decline.

Textbooks add a third shape: vascular NCD — the second most common cause — drawn as steps, each drop a stroke. Fluctuating against steady is solid; the staircase is a signature to test, not trust — we come back to it.

Recall the prevalence page: the same 200 patients were 16% or 28% delirious depending on the manual. Both lessons live on this diagnosis.

Three shapes on the board — sawtooth, slope, staircase. Sawtooth-versus-slope is the exercise's answer key and is well supported (delirium page, Differential). The staircase is the textbook vascular signature that the Guide's vascular page (Contested) says is not unequivocally supported by data — the "cleaner than the brains" slide pays this off. Guide: delirium; neurocognitive-disorders; vascular-neurocognitive-disorder.

Delirium · what the evidence says

Prevention works. Treatment doesn't.

Hospital prevention programs bundle unglamorous measures: orientation, early mobilisation, hydration, sleep, medication review, family involvement — and making sure people have their glasses and hearing aids.

SettingDelirium incidence
Hip-fracture surgery (RCTs)29% lower
Medical wards (controlled trials)35% lower
Low-risk patientsno benefit

Once delirium has started, the same measures do not resolve it. Its pathophysiology is not well understood.

Baseline risk decides whether prevention works at all. The clinical skill is targeting: find the high-risk patient before the delirium starts.

Ask: "What is the cheapest intervention on that list?" Glasses and hearing aids. Then reveal the table row by row, stopping on the low-risk row.

What prevention did not change: falls, functional decline, length of stay or mortality. Abraha et al. (2015): 26 systematic reviews covering 31 primary studies; blinding impossible; hospital evidence only.

Guide: delirium, Treatment.

The heavyweight

Alzheimer's: the pathology arrives before the symptoms

Beta-amyloid plaques accumulate between neurons, believed to appear before symptoms; neurofibrillary tangles form inside them after — both damaging hippocampus, amygdala and cortex.

  • 60–90% of dementias; dementia prevalence climbs from 1–2% at 65 to ~30% at 85
  • Standard treatment is symptomatic: cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine, early-to-middle stages. ApoE e4 is a vulnerability, not a cause
  • ~90% of patients are cared for by relatives — which is why caregiver support is listed as a treatment

And the foil: frontotemporal NCD arrives personality-first — disinhibition and blunted judgement with memory initially intact, at younger ages — and is easily mistaken for a psychiatric or personality change before anyone suspects the brain.

Caregiver support as a treatment modality is the humane fact of the chapter — the identified patient is not the only patient.

The plaques-before-symptoms ordering is stated in the source without longitudinal data (beta-amyloid-and-neurofibrillary-tangles, Limits) — hence "believed". Hospital charts still speak the older A-words: aphasia, apraxia, agnosia, anomia (neurocognitive-disorders, "Two vocabularies").

Guide: neurocognitive-disorder-due-to-alzheimers-disease; neurocognitive-disorders, Epidemiology; frontotemporal-neurocognitive-disorder.

Alzheimer's · the new drugs

"27% slower decline" — how big is that?

Lecanemab and donanemab are the first drugs aimed at the pathology itself. Lecanemab's trial: 18 months of infusions, scored on an 18-point scale.

0.45 ÷ 1.66 = 27%. A small absolute difference in a slow measure makes a large relative percentage — and the percentage reaches the headlines.

The harms: brain swelling or bleeding in about a quarter of treated patients; number needed to harm ≈ 3; deaths with severe bleeding.

A pattern across the chapter: an antibody that cleared the prion protein changed nothing clinically, and Lewy body density tracks no symptom. Clearing the visible protein has not delivered in three diseases.

Ask first, before the diagram: "A drug slows decline by 27%. Good drug?" Hands up. Then show the scale and the MCID bar.

Donanemab: 3.4 points on a 144-point scale (2.4%), against a perceptible change of 5 points. Both effects are around half the size reported for donepezil (Espay, Kepp & Herrup, 2024 — critics).

The other side, fairly: Haass and Selkoe (2022) argue the amyloid hypothesis is sound and the trials started too late and ran too short; the Icelandic APP variant that lowers amyloid by about 40% protects against the disease. Both sides concede the field has no validated definition of a clinically meaningful change. Neither author is a neutral referee.

Guide: neurocognitive-disorder-due-to-alzheimers-disease, Treatment ("Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies") and Contested; neurocognitive-disorder-other-aetiologies, Prion disease and "What is contested across these four".

The one that can kill via the wrong prescription

Lewy bodies, and a trap

Fluctuating cognition, well-formed visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder — and routinely misdiagnosed as Alzheimer's.

The trap: the standard antipsychotic response to agitation can produce severe, sometimes fatal, sensitivity reactions in Lewy body disease. The misdiagnosis is not cosmetic — it changes what might kill the patient.

Same lesson as apnea-mimicking-depression in Lecture 7: the differential is the safety mechanism.

Strongest clinical-stakes moment of the lecture. One vignette-style beat, no gore.

Last year's "third most common dementia" is not in the Guide — the Lewy page declares population prevalence a gap. What it does say: hallucinations in about 80%, insight typically retained; REM sleep behaviour disorder can precede everything else by years. Guide: neurocognitive-disorder-with-lewy-bodies, Presentation and Treatment.

The categories are cleaner than the brains

Mixed pathology is the rule, not the edge case

  • One third of people who develop dementia after a stroke meet criteria for Alzheimer's
  • Up to half of carefully research-diagnosed Alzheimer's cases show unsuspected Lewy pathology at autopsy
  • The Hachinski score, built to separate vascular from Alzheimer's at the bedside, failed to predict what autopsy found

The Nun Study: among people whose brains met criteria for Alzheimer's, those who also had infarcts were more likely to have been demented in life. Same plaques, different outcome.

Cognitive reserve is the other half: education, complex work and cognitively rich lives delay the clinical expression of pathology — matched brains, later symptoms. It buys time; it does not prevent the disease.

So the stepwise "staircase" of vascular NCD is a textbook signature, not a reliable sign. And cholinergic drugs work as well in vascular dementia as in Alzheimer's — treatment does not justify the split either.

Ask: "If Alzheimer's and vascular dementia are separate diseases, what should autopsy show?" Clean separation. It doesn't.

The caution runs both ways: co-occurrence is not synergy — one pathology may be an innocent bystander, and silent infarcts need not produce symptoms. The proposed reframe is a continuum with pure forms at the rare extremes. The source is from 2003 and predates amyloid PET and blood biomarkers; say so.

Reserve's sting: high-reserve patients can present late, with more pathology already accumulated, and then decline fast.

Guide: vascular-neurocognitive-disorder, Contested; neurocognitive-disorder-due-to-alzheimers-disease, Contested; neurocognitive-disorder-with-lewy-bodies, Epidemiology; neurocognitive-disorders, "Two vocabularies worth holding side by side".

Why subtype at all?

Same drug, four answers

Donepezil, a cholinesterase inhibitor, in —

Alzheimer's diseaseeases symptoms and slows decline, early-to-middle stages
Traumatic brain injurymay improve attention and memory; guideline-recommended for moderate-to-severe TBI
Mild NCDcontraindicated by three guidance documents
Frontotemporal NCDno benefit; worsened disinhibition in 4 of 12 patients in one series

Frontotemporal NCD spares the cholinergic system, so the drug targets a deficit the disease does not have. SSRIs, aimed at its serotonergic deficit, are the one class with randomised support.

And antipsychotics: the severe sensitivity reaction in Lewy body disease, parkinsonism and tardive antecollis in frontotemporal NCD. Two dementias where the standard response to agitation is the dangerous one.

Ask for a prediction row by row: "Helps, useless, or harmful?" The point is that prescribing a dementia drug because the diagnosis is dementia is exactly the error subtyping exists to prevent.

Guide: neurocognitive-disorder-due-to-alzheimers-disease, Treatment; neurocognitive-disorder-due-to-traumatic-brain-injury, Treatment (Pharmacological); mild-neurocognitive-disorder, Treatment (Chen et al., 2021); frontotemporal-neurocognitive-disorder, Treatment (Gambogi et al., 2021); neurocognitive-disorder-with-lewy-bodies, Treatment.

Two that move backward

Not every decline declines

  • TBI-related NCD: recovery can move a major back to a mild — the categories are stations, not destinations
  • Substance/medication-induced NCD: the one subtype that may partially reverse with abstinence
  • TBI rehabilitation's lesson: memory drills do little for long-term memory, while a notebook or phone reminder has the strongest evidence — a different route to the same outcome beats rebuilding the damaged one.

Which is the live debate: is mild-to-major one continuum, staged and one-way? The recovery evidence says the traffic runs both directions.

The debate page (Blaze vs Petersen) has open gaps — last contribution plug of the term for anyone still short.

Mild NCD · what to tell someone

Most people with mild NCD do not convert this year

6–15% a year

of people with mild cognitive impairment develop Alzheimer's. In any given year, most do not.

So prognosis counselling is itself a recommendation. Guidelines back exercise, cognitive training and diet — and advise against the dementia drugs.

What nobody has studied: the people who stay stable or improve — the group most affected by being told they have a pre-dementia condition.

Ask first, before the number: "Your grandmother is told she has mild NCD. What is the chance she has Alzheimer's a year from now?" Most guess far higher than 6–15%.

Prevalence of mild NCD: 2–10% at 65, 5–25% at 85. Exercise recommended by 4 of the 13 guidance documents, cognitive training by 4, diet by 3 (Chen et al., 2021). Reversion rates are a declared Guide gap — a good amber contribution.

Guide: mild-neurocognitive-disorder, Treatment and Epidemiology.

Substance-induced NCD · the emergency inside it

Wernicke's: missed by its own textbook triad

Heavy drinking plus thiamine deficiency. The classic triad — abnormal eye movements, unsteady gait, confusion — is rarely present in full.

Caine's criteria need two of four features, and sensitivity rises from 23% to 85%.

The error to know: delirium in someone who drinks gets labelled withdrawal, and the Wernicke delirium underneath is missed. And oral thiamine is not a weaker treatment — in suspected Wernicke's it is not a treatment.

Missed, it becomes Korsakoff syndrome: severe anterograde amnesia, patients unaware of their own deficits, and confabulation — which fills the gaps and is not lying.

The hopeful half: up to a year of abstinence improves attention, working memory, problem-solving and visuospatial function, with brain volume increasing; learning and short-term memory recover least. Alcohol accounts for 10–22% of dementia before 65.

Ask: "A patient smells of alcohol, is confused and unsteady. What is the first diagnosis in your head?" Withdrawal — which is exactly how Wernicke's gets missed. Treatment is parenteral thiamine; the Guide gives the regimen.

Assessment has to wait at least six weeks of abstinence, or it measures the acute state and underestimates recovery.

From the 2025 deck and not in the Guide — not examinable as stated: "50–70% of alcohol-dependent people have cognitive impairment" and "about 7% meet dementia criteria". The Guide's figures: dementia in 10–24% of people who abuse alcohol; heavy drinking a contributing factor in about 24% of dementia cases.

Guide: substance-medication-induced-neurocognitive-disorder, Treatment and "Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome" (Wijnia, 2022; Sachdeva et al., 2016).

Known cause, no easy answer

Four subtypes where the cause is certain

Knowing the cause has not delivered the treatment. Huntington's management rests on expert consensus; every prion treatment has failed.

A rapidly progressive dementia may also be reversible: autoimmune encephalitis, lithium toxicity, low blood sugar, thiamine deficiency. Rule those out first — mistaking a treatable cause for an untreatable one is the costliest error in the chapter.

Ask: "Huntington's can be tested for decades before symptoms. Would you want to know?" Then the reveal: certainty about cause has bought very little treatment.

HIV adds a measurement lesson: under the Frascati criteria 15–22% of people without HIV test positive on extensive batteries, so the asymptomatic level may be manufactured by the thresholds.

From the 2025 deck and not in the Guide — not examinable as stated: "a third of people with Parkinson's develop another NCD", and the prion figures on transmission and one-year mortality.
