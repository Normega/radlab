Part one

Sexual dysfunctions

The threshold does the teaching

No distress, no diagnosis

Six months. Three-quarters or more of sexual situations. And clinically significant distress.

The source gives the reason directly: no one should be forced into being sexual if they do not want to be. Some people have low desire and are entirely content. Asexuality is an orientation, not a disorder.

The diagnosis exists for people who want to engage and find they cannot.

This is Lecture 1's distress criterion working properly for once — worth saying. The 43%-of-women "difficulty" figure comes later, and this slide is what stops it being misread.

And one subtype grid across all of them: lifelong or acquired · generalised or situational · mild to severe. Learn it once; it carries most of the treatment implication.

Make the grid concrete: situational + acquired points at the situation (relationship, medication, stressor); lifelong + generalised points somewhere else entirely. One example each and move.

Measurement, again

43% is not a prevalence

The most-quoted number in this literature: 43% of women and 31% of men reported a sexual difficulty in the past year.

A difficulty rate is not a diagnosis rate. The diagnosis requires six months, 75% of occasions, and distress. The survey asked about any difficulty, ever, in a year.

Same lesson as the prevalence page in your Guide: a number is a property of the manual, the instrument, the threshold and the sample — before it is a property of the world.

Students will have seen 43% in headlines. Deconstructing it is more useful than any new number, and it rehearses the skill the quizzes keep testing.

One worked example

The antidepressant problem

Sexual dysfunction is a common effect of serotonergic antidepressants — and the reported rate ranges from 3.9% to 72.7% depending on the agent and, critically, on whether anyone asks.

  • Spontaneous remission on continued treatment: ~10%
  • And the attribution problem: depression itself affects desire — so which is the drug and which is the disorder?

A side effect nobody asks about is a side effect that does not appear in the data.

Links back to L5 (antidepressants) and forward to the RCT segment: measurement choices create the phenomenon. Keep it to three minutes.

Part two

Gender dysphoria

The three distinctions that carry everything

The diagnosis names distress, not identity

  1. Being transgender is not a disorder.
  2. Gender nonconformity is not gender dysphoria. The critical element is clinically significant distress.
  3. The diagnosis attaches to the distress — discontent with the sex and gender assigned at birth — not to who the person is.

~0.6% of US adults identify as transgender; gender dysphoria diagnoses sit near 0.01%. Those two numbers being sixty-fold apart is the distinctions working.

Deliver the three slowly; they are the content, not a preamble. The 0.6% vs 0.01% gap is the cleanest empirical statement of "identity is not the diagnosis" available anywhere.

Why keep the diagnosis at all?

The honest argument, both directions

Against: a diagnosis in the DSM attaches psychiatric machinery — and historical stigma — to an experience of identity. The harm is real and documented.

For: the diagnosis is what unlocks access to care — and for many people, mental health symptoms improve following treatment of the dysphoria, e.g. with hormonal treatment.

Both sides are arguing about real harms. There is no answer that costs nothing — which is why this is a live public controversy and not a settled fact to memorise.

This mirrors the final exam's one prose question territory: no defensible single key. Model holding both sides without collapsing them. If asked your view, give it as a view and label it as one.

Protective factors deserve their sentence: family support and school safety measurably promote resilience. The risk numbers are not destiny.

Part three

Paraphilic disorders

The distinction that governs the chapter

A paraphilia is not a disorder

A paraphilia is an atypical, persistent sexual interest. Not innately pathological.

It becomes a disorder by one of two routes:

  1. It causes the person distress or functional impairment, or
  2. Acting on it harms or victimises a non-consenting person

Those are different routes. One is about suffering; the other is about harm. Which route applies determines what the diagnosis is even for.

The Guide calls confusing paraphilia with paraphilic disorder "the single most consequential error a student can make here." Say that. It is also the highest-yield exam distinction in the lecture.

The organising axis

Sort the eight by consent

A victim is intrinsic:

pedophilic · exhibitionistic · voyeuristic · frotteuristic

Disorder regardless of whether the person is distressed — the harm route.

Potentially consensual:

fetishistic · sadism/masochism · transvestic

Disorder only via distress, impairment, or non-consensual acting — the suffering route.

Consent is more informative than the DSM's own ordering — it tells you which route to disorder each diagnosis takes.

DSM-5 notes ~2% of males/1.3% of females may be involved in BDSM and over one in ten adults report a sadomasochistic experience — interest and behaviour are common; the disorders are not. That is the interest-vs-behaviour gap in one sentence.

Where the evidence is honest about its limits

What we don't know, and why we can't know it

  • Pedophilic disorder prevalence: essentially unknown. Severe stigma keeps people out of clinical samples, so published figures are unrepresentative by construction.
  • Voyeurism figures contradict themselves within a single source — your Guide flags them as not-quotable.
  • Reported paraphilia prevalence spreads from a few percent to 60% depending on whether you count interest, fantasy, or behaviour.

A textbook that says "we do not know" is being more scientific than one that supplies a number.

This is the Field Guide's declared-gaps philosophy in the most difficult content area. It is also why several paraphilia pages carry claimable amber gaps — mention it for anyone still owing contributions.

Design lesson 1

Why randomize?

Remember the greens powder from Lecture 1 — better compared to what?

If you had chosen your practice, the choosers and the practice would be tangled: maybe calm people pick one, anxious people the other. Any difference in December could be about who chose, not what they did.

Random assignment makes the two groups equivalent in expectation — on everything, including the things nobody thought to measure.

"Including the things nobody thought to measure" is the sentence to slow down for — it is why randomization beats matching, and it is exam material.

Design lesson 2

Why blind?

You don't know which arm you're in. You don't know the hypotheses.

  • If you knew, your expectations could produce the outcome — the placebo machinery from Lecture 2
  • On day 2 we asked how credible your practice felt. That's not filler: it lets us show the arms were equally believable, rather than assert it

Point at the CEQ items explicitly — students remember answering them and the penny drops that every question had a job.

Design lesson 3

We wrote our predictions down first

Before tonight's first session, the design, outcomes and analysis plan were pre-registered — publicly time-stamped where we cannot quietly edit them.

December 2, you'll see the pre-registration next to the results. Where the results match, you'll know we predicted it. Where they don't, you'll know that too — and that is the point.

Remember two-out-of-seventeen. Pre-registration is one of the field's answers to the file drawer.
