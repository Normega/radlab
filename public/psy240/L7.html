Part one

Sexual dysfunctions

The threshold does the teaching

No distress, no diagnosis

Six months. Three-quarters or more of sexual situations. And clinically significant distress.

The source gives the reason directly: no one should be forced into being sexual if they do not want to be. Some people have low desire and are entirely content. Asexuality is an orientation, not a disorder.

The diagnosis exists for people who want to engage and find they cannot.

This is Lecture 1's distress criterion working properly for once — worth saying. The 43%-of-women "difficulty" figure comes later, and this slide is what stops it being misread.

Where the map comes from

Three models of the response cycle

Masters and Johnson watched 382 women and 312 men through an estimated 10,000 cycles — and found the physiology of orgasm identical whether stimulation is clitoral or vaginal. Freud's distinction did not survive the laboratory.

Kaplan's version begins with desire, a stage Masters and Johnson do not have — and it is Kaplan's version the DSM follows. A fixed sequence also tends to read variation as deficit; the dual control model is built to avoid that.

Ask before the second build: "Which row does the DSM need that Masters and Johnson never observed?" Desire. Their four stages begin at excitement; the DSM's first row cannot be placed on their model.

All from sexual-dysfunctions, "The response-cycle models the categories rest on". The dual control row is the Guide's; the large individual differences in excitability are Rodriguez-Nieto et al. (2019) as cited there.

The rows are Kaplan's cycle from the last slide — desire → arousal → orgasm — with pain as a row of its own. The table is that model with one question attached: where does it break?

And one subtype grid across all of them: lifelong or acquired · generalised or situational · mild to severe. Learn it once; it carries most of the treatment implication.

Make the grid concrete: situational + acquired points at the situation (relationship, medication, stressor); lifelong + generalised points somewhere else entirely. One example each and move.

Measurement, again

43% is not a prevalence

The most-quoted number in this literature: 43% of women and 31% of men reported a sexual difficulty in the past year.

A difficulty rate is not a diagnosis rate. The diagnosis requires six months, 75% of occasions, and distress. The survey asked about any difficulty, ever, in a year.

Same lesson as the prevalence page in your Guide: a number is a property of the manual, the instrument, the threshold and the sample — before it is a property of the world.

And most sexual difficulties are temporary: they come with a period, a situation or a partner, and often resolve with or without therapy.

Students will have seen 43% in headlines. Deconstructing it is more useful than any new number, and it rehearses the skill the quizzes keep testing.

The desire row

One old diagnosis, split by sex

DSM-5 divided hypoactive sexual desire disorder in two — and the halves are not parallel.

Male hypoactive sexual desire disorderFemale sexual interest/arousal disorder
What is assesseddesire onlyinterest and arousal, merged
Thresholdclinician judges "deficient", allowing for age and culture — no countat least 3 of 6 criteria
Approved drugnone named for low desireflibanserin, premenopausal women

The rationale is empirical, not principled: in women, desire and arousal are harder to separate. Whether that reflects a real difference or how each sex was studied is an open question.

The largest single factor in developing sexual dysfunction, for straight and lesbian women alike: internalising the belief that sexual desire and pleasure are sinful (Peixoto & Nobre, 2014).

Before the box, ask: "Guess the largest single factor in the Peixoto and Nobre study." Most rooms guess hormones, age or relationship length. The answer is a belief — which is also what cognitive sexual therapy targets.

Sources: male-hypoactive-sexual-desire-disorder (Diagnosis; Treatment, "Note the asymmetry in pharmacology") and female-sexual-interest-arousal-disorder (Presentation; Diagnosis; Etiology; Contested). The FSIAD page calls this the diagnosis most often cited as a medicalisation case — the 43% slide is the other half of that argument. Asexuality is an orientation, not this diagnosis; say it again here if the room needs it.

The orgasm row

Three diagnoses, one phase

  • Female orgasmic disorder — delay, reduced intensity or absence despite normal arousal. Primary (never, 5–10% of women) or secondary (lost after having it — the more common form)
  • Delayed ejaculation — rare, and most often situational: with a partner, not through masturbation, which largely excludes a physical cause
  • Premature (early) ejaculation — within about one minute of penetration; one of the most common male dysfunctions, and it resists precise definition

Only 29% of women always have orgasms with a partner. A disorder rate, or a mismatch between practice and expectation?

Orgasmic difficulty is more common in younger, less sexually experienced women — pointing to learning and context. And sometimes there is no identifiable cause; the Guide says so rather than inventing one.

Put the 29% question to the room before the last build. It is the 43% lesson again: what the number measures versus what the diagnosis requires (six months, 75% of occasions, distress).

All from ejaculation-and-orgasmic-disorders (Presentation, Diagnosis, Epidemiology, Etiology). Do not use the source's "up to 67% of divorces" figure — the page flags it as unsourced and not to be taught. Delayed and premature ejaculation have no rates in the Guide.

The pain row

Pain that anticipation keeps alive

DSM-5 merged dyspareunia and vaginismus into genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder — because clinicians could not reliably tell the two apart.

Fear of pain alone meets a criterion — this is the anxiety disorders' structure. So the treatments are too: systematic desensitisation, cognitive restructuring, pelvic-floor exercises, sensate focus, mindfulness.

60% say it compromised their ability to enjoy life. Legacy prevalence: 3–25% for dyspareunia, 0.4–6.6% for vaginismus — the spread is the instrument. And the evidence base is thin: few RCTs, most of them small.

Ask before the avoidance build: "Where have we seen this loop before?" Specific phobia and panic (L3). Then: "What stops a phobia from extinguishing?" Avoidance — build it.

From genito-pelvic-pain-penetration-disorder (Diagnosis — the merger as a reliability fix; Etiology — the fear-avoidance model; Epidemiology; Treatment). The Guide is explicit that no trial has targeted all the symptoms; teach it as an honest thin evidence base, not "psychological treatment works". A second self-sustaining loop comes with erectile disorder shortly.

One worked example

The antidepressant problem

Sexual dysfunction is a common effect of serotonergic antidepressants — and the reported rate ranges from 3.9% to 72.7% depending on the agent and, critically, on whether anyone asks.

  • Spontaneous remission on continued treatment: ~10%
  • And the attribution problem: depression itself affects desire — so which is the drug and which is the disorder?

A side effect nobody asks about is a side effect that does not appear in the data.

Links back to L5 (antidepressants) and forward to the RCT segment: measurement choices create the phenomenon. Keep it to three minutes.

The arousal row · a case

Where does it fail — and where doesn't it?

A man loses his erection with his partner, but not during masturbation. Where do you look?

Situational points to psychological or relational factors; generalised points to a physical cause — nerve damage in prostate or colorectal surgery, diabetes, cardiovascular disease. Erectile difficulty is frequently the presenting sign of a vascular problem.

Sildenafil works — yet more than 50% of couples stop using medical aids within the first year: loss of spontaneity, "artificial intimacy", new performance demands.

Combined psychological and medical treatment outperforms medication alone, with less drop-out, even where a clear organic cause is established.

Take two or three answers to the case before the box. The subtype grid from the map slide is doing the diagnostic work here — this is the slide where "situational" stops being a label.

From erectile-disorder (Diagnosis; Etiology; Contested, Dewitte et al., 2021). Two more lines if there is time: psychological erectile difficulty has a very strong placebo response, so read any uncontrolled treatment claim against it; and depression carries a 70% increased risk of erectile dysfunction while erectile dysfunction carries a more than threefold risk of depression — direction unresolved. The discontinuation reasons are the bridge to the next slide.

The chapter's own loop

Watching yourself perform

Arousal falters once. The next time, part of the attention turns inward — is it working? — and observation replaces participation.

  • Anxiety about the performance produces exactly the failure being feared
  • The failure confirms the fear, and the loop tightens — panic's engine, relocated

Spectatoring. Add it to the collection: panic feeds on sensations, worry on relief-in-thought, compulsions on rituals — and this one on self-observation.

Two minutes. The course collects self-sustaining loops on purpose, and naming this one as "panic's engine, relocated" is what makes the psychological treatment on the next slide obvious before it is shown.

And it is treatable

The part the chapter is quietest about

  • Sensate focus — the psychological first line: touch with intercourse deliberately off the table, dismantling the performance by removing it. Aimed squarely at the loop on the last slide.
  • PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil) for erectile disorder; flibanserin for low desire in premenopausal women; for antidepressant-induced dysfunction, switch agents or add bupropion — waiting it out works for only one patient in ten

Students leave this chapter thinking it is a catalogue of problems. Every row of the map has a treatment.

One slide, deliberately — the Guide's sexual-dysfunctions, erectile-disorder and ejaculation-and-orgasmic-disorders pages carry the detail. The point the room must not leave without: every row of the map slide has a treatment — though the evidence behind each varies, and the pain row's is thinnest.

Removed from last draft: the squeeze technique and SSRIs for premature ejaculation. The Guide lists behavioural techniques for premature ejaculation as a declared gap (ejaculation-and-orgasmic-disorders, Needs research) — not examinable until someone fills it. Bupropion augmentation and switching are on that page's antidepressant table.

Part two

Gender dysphoria

The three distinctions that carry everything

The diagnosis names distress, not identity

  1. Being transgender is not a disorder.
  2. Gender nonconformity is not gender dysphoria. The critical element is clinically significant distress.
  3. The diagnosis attaches to the distress — discontent with the sex and gender assigned at birth — not to who the person is.

~0.6% of US adults identify as transgender; gender dysphoria diagnoses sit near 0.01%. Those two numbers being sixty-fold apart is the distinctions working.

Deliver the three slowly; they are the content, not a preamble. The 0.6% vs 0.01% gap is the cleanest empirical statement of "identity is not the diagnosis" available anywhere.

Why keep the diagnosis at all?

The honest argument, both directions

Against: a diagnosis in the DSM attaches psychiatric machinery — and historical stigma — to an experience of identity. The harm is real and documented.

For: the diagnosis is what unlocks access to care — and for many people, mental health symptoms improve following treatment of the dysphoria, e.g. with hormonal treatment.

Both sides are arguing about real harms. There is no answer that costs nothing — which is why this is a live public controversy and not a settled fact to memorise.

This mirrors the final exam's one prose question territory: no defensible single key. Model holding both sides without collapsing them. If asked your view, give it as a view and label it as one.

Protective factors deserve their sentence: family support and school safety measurably promote resilience. The risk numbers are not destiny.

Gender dysphoria · the criteria

What should happen to a diagnosis once treatment works?

2013: gender identity disorder renamed gender dysphoria and moved out of the sexual disorders into a chapter of its own.

Adolescents and adults: two or more of six for at least six months — e.g. a strong desire to be, or be treated as, another gender; incongruence between experienced gender and sex characteristics — plus clinically significant distress or impairment.

  • A separate child diagnosis — children have less ability to have insight into what they are experiencing, or to express it
  • A post-transition specifier — so people who have transitioned can keep receiving hormonal or other treatment

A diagnosis that lapsed on successful treatment would remove access to the treatment that made it lapse.

Ask the title question before the builds and take answers. Most students say "it goes away" — then show the specifier, and the logic of the previous slide's "for" column becomes concrete.

From gender-dysphoria (Diagnosis; Contested, "The reclassification"). The move out of the sexual disorders is why this lecture's three parts are three separate DSM chapters. Keep the language precise: the diagnosis names distress, not identity.

Gender dysphoria · causes and care

Why the comorbidity can't be read at face value

The social account: the suffering comes from stigma and victimisation. A more accepting society would mean less of it.

The biological account: genetic variation, prenatal hormones, brain structure and function — and the source says plainly that much of it is preliminary.

Between them sits a confound: discrimination, harassment, violence and denied care are common — so the effects of dysphoria cannot be separated from the effects of marginalisation. In the 2015 U.S. Transgender Survey, unemployment was three times the general rate; nearly a third lived at or below the poverty level.

Treatment is driven by the person's desired outcome, not a fixed protocol: hormones, surgeries, counselling for legal and social changes, family counselling. Followed to WPATH standards, it is effective and satisfaction is very high.

Ask: "If a group has high rates of depression, what would you need to know before calling the depression part of the condition?" Whether the group is also marginalised — and here it is. That is L1's confound lesson in the most consequential setting of the term.

From gender-dysphoria (Etiology; Epidemiology; Treatment). The Guide's adolescent-care evidence and ICD-11's reclassification as gender incongruence are declared gaps — do not improvise figures on either; point to the gap board. Support line if needed: student-support-resources lists 9-8-8 and U of T services.

Part three

Paraphilic disorders

The distinction that governs the chapter

A paraphilia is not a disorder

A paraphilia is an atypical, persistent sexual interest. Not innately pathological.

It becomes a disorder by one of two routes:

  1. It causes the person distress or functional impairment, or
  2. Acting on it harms or victimises a non-consenting person

Those are different routes. One is about suffering; the other is about harm. Which route applies determines what the diagnosis is even for.

The Guide calls confusing paraphilia with paraphilic disorder "the single most consequential error a student can make here." Say that. It is also the highest-yield exam distinction in the lecture.

The organising axis

Sort the eight by consent

A victim is intrinsic:

pedophilic · exhibitionistic · voyeuristic · frotteuristic

Disorder regardless of whether the person is distressed — the harm route.

Potentially consensual:

fetishistic · sadism/masochism · transvestic

Disorder only via distress, impairment, or non-consensual acting — the suffering route.

Consent is more informative than the DSM's own ordering — it tells you which route to disorder each diagnosis takes.

And when the harm route is taken, treatment targets recidivism, not the interest: CBT-based programmes, sometimes antiandrogens. How thin that evidence is gets its own slide.

DSM-5 notes 2.2% of males and 1.3% of females may be involved in BDSM and over one in ten adults report a sadomasochistic experience — interest and behaviour are common; the disorders are not. That is the interest-vs-behaviour gap in one sentence.

Frotteuristic disorder has no page of its own; the overview carries it: 10–14% of men seen clinically for paraphilias or hypersexuality, against frotteuristic acts in up to 30% of men generally. Sadism has the non-consent route and masochism does not — the person harmed would be the person diagnosed (sexual-sadism-and-masochism-disorders, Diagnosis). And consensual BDSM practitioners show high wellbeing, with no link to childhood sexual assault.

Pedophilic disorder

An offence is not a diagnosis

Arousal to prepubescent children — generally 13 or younger — equal to or greater than arousal to adults, for at least six months, acted on or causing distress or impairment. The person is at least 16, and at least five years older than the child.

Only about 40% of people convicted of sexual offences against children meet the criteria.

  • The rest are better explained by opportunity, antisociality, substance use — and arousal can exist in someone who has never offended, which is where prevention works
  • Prevention services for non-offending people who seek help: Germany's Dunkelfeld project, Stop It Now, Sweden's Preventell

Cause: unknown. A meta-analysis of 436 men with the disorder against 449 controls found no structural brain difference.

Set the register before this slide: clinical and precise, no anecdotes. Ask for a guess at the 40% before revealing — most guess nearly all. Both halves matter: equating the diagnosis with offending misdescribes 60% of offenders, and requiring an offence misses the people prevention can reach.

From pedophilic-disorder (Presentation; Diagnosis; Etiology; Treatment, "Prevention services"). The Ontario duty to report (CYFSA s. 125) is triggered by reasonable grounds to suspect a child is being or is at risk of being abused — not by a disclosure of urges alone; that is the space prevention services work in. Do not repeat the source passages the Guide flags (cultural relativism; the conflation with homosexuality) — the page names and corrects them.

Where the evidence is honest about its limits

What we don't know, and why we can't know it

  • Pedophilic disorder prevalence: essentially unknown. Severe stigma keeps people out of clinical samples, so published figures are unrepresentative by construction.
  • Voyeurism figures contradict themselves within a single source — your Guide flags them as not-quotable.
  • Reported paraphilia prevalence spreads from a few percent to 60% depending on whether you count interest, fantasy, or behaviour.

A textbook that says "we do not know" is being more scientific than one that supplies a number.

This is the Field Guide's declared-gaps philosophy in the most difficult content area. It is also why several paraphilia pages carry claimable amber gaps — mention it for anyone still owing contributions.

The diagnosis in court

How much agreement is enough to confine someone?

US Sexually Violent Predator laws can confine a person after their sentence ends — and a paraphilic diagnosis can serve as the required "mental condition".

In the Canadian files, offenders judged not sadists had more often tortured their victims. No centre followed the DSM criteria — each had its own definition. The experts' agreement on which criteria matter became a dimensional alternative, the Severe Sexual Sadism Scale.

Ask before the Canadian bar: "What agreement would you want before a diagnosis decides indefinite confinement — or parole?" Then show 0.14. Callback to reliability in L1/L2: κ is the same statistic, and here it is the hinge of a liberty decision.

From paraphilic-disorders (Contested, "The forensic stakes") and sexual-sadism-and-masochism-disorders (Contested, "The diagnosis in court") — Marshall & Kingston (2018). Reference material, not legal guidance; say so.

Treating paraphilic disorders

28 studies, 379 people

That is the entire pharmacological literature for the chapter — mostly case reports, and only three studies score above 5 on the Newcastle-Ottawa quality scale.

  • The side-effect ladder drives the choice: SSRIs (minimal) → steroidal antiandrogens (up to adrenal insufficiency and Cushing's) → GnRH agents (osteoporosis; suicidal ideation with degarelix)
  • The most-quoted number rests on one 1992 study: reoffending 18% on medroxyprogesterone, 35% after stopping, 58% untreated
  • In pedophilic disorder, drugs reduce sexual drive — but no randomised trial measured offending

Programmes for convicted offenders: effects "moderately positive" with large heterogeneity, and mandated participation works less well than near-voluntary. The field distinguishes managing a disorder from treating its cause.

Ask: "Why would this literature be so thin?" Answers to draw out: people rarely seek treatment voluntarily; placebo or no-treatment arms are ethically very hard in potential offenders; and follow-up collapses once symptoms remit.

From paraphilic-disorders (Treatment, Culos et al., 2024; "Rehabilitation frameworks", Heffernan & Ward, 2019) and pedophilic-disorder (Treatment, Landgren et al., 2022). The risk-need-responsivity and Good Lives models have their own Guide pages if anyone asks.

Design lesson 1

Why randomize?

Remember the greens powder from Lecture 1 — better compared to what?

If you had chosen your practice, the choosers and the practice would be tangled: maybe calm people pick one, anxious people the other. Any difference in December could be about who chose, not what they did.

Random assignment makes the two groups equivalent in expectation — on everything, including the things nobody thought to measure.

"Including the things nobody thought to measure" is the sentence to slow down for — it is why randomization beats matching, and it is exam material.

Design lesson 2

Why blind?

You don't know which arm you're in. You don't know the hypotheses.

  • If you knew, your expectations could produce the outcome — the placebo machinery from Lecture 2
  • On day 2 we asked how credible your practice felt. That's not filler: it lets us show the arms were equally believable, rather than assert it

Point at the CEQ items explicitly — students remember answering them and the penny drops that every question had a job.

Design lesson 3

We wrote our predictions down first

Before tonight's first session, the design, outcomes and analysis plan were pre-registered — publicly time-stamped where we cannot quietly edit them.

December 2, you'll see the pre-registration next to the results. Where the results match, you'll know we predicted it. Where they don't, you'll know that too — and that is the point.

Remember two-out-of-seventeen. Pre-registration is one of the field's answers to the file drawer.
