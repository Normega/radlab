Part one

Personality as pathology

What makes these different

The disorder is the person's pattern

An enduring pattern of inner experience and behaviour — cognition, affect, interpersonal functioning, impulse control — that is pervasive, inflexible, stable, and traces to adolescence or early adulthood.

Everything else this term was an episode or a state the person has. This chapter says the enduring style can itself be the disorder. That claim is exactly where all the trouble starts.

The state/trait line is the philosophical hinge of the day — and the reason the dimensional argument later has bite. Say "the trouble starts here" and mean it.

The map

Ten disorders, three clusters

A · odd/eccentricparanoid · schizoid · schizotypal
B · dramatic/erraticantisocial · borderline · histrionic · narcissistic
C · anxious/fearfulavoidant · dependent · obsessive-compulsive

DSM-5-TR itself concedes the clustering "has not been consistently validated." The manual prints the map and disclaims it on the same page.

Median prevalence across countries: Cluster A 3.6%, B 4.5%, C 2.8% — and 10.5% for any personality disorder, about one person in ten.

Don't drill all ten one by one — the next slides teach them as contrasts (why few relationships; attention versus admiration versus care), then the four differentials with teeth.

Shared features of all ten: distorted thinking, problematic emotional responses, over- or under-regulated impulse control, interpersonal difficulty; no diagnosis before 18. Medians: personality-disorder-clusters ("Median prevalence by cluster", DSM-5-TR).

Same surface, different mechanism

Few close relationships — but why?

Three Cluster A disorders and one from Cluster C can look identical from across the room. The diagnosis is the reason, not the behaviour.

Look at what separates them: suspicion, desire, fear — every one an inner state reported by the person. Two of these four boundaries are drawn on self-report alone.

Before the first build, ask: "A colleague has eaten lunch alone every day for three years and has no friends at work. Give me four different reasons that could be true." Take answers, then build the branches in order. Most rooms produce "doesn't trust anyone" and "shy" quickly; "simply doesn't want to" is the one they resist, and it is the schizoid mechanism.

Sources: the pairs table in personality-disorders ("The distinctions that actually separate them"); paranoid-personality-disorder and schizoid-personality-disorder (Diagnosis sections); schizotypal-personality-disorder (ideas of reference are the most prominent feature); categorical-vs-dimensional-personality-models ("The boundaries are drawn on inner states"). The avoidant branch is dashed because it belongs to Cluster C, which is itself a point about the clusters.

Three ways to need other people

Attention, admiration, or care?

DisorderWhat is soughtThe tell
Histrionic · Battention — uncomfortable unless at the centre of iteasily suggestible; treats casual acquaintances as intimates
Narcissistic · Badmiration, on the basis of assumed superioritygrandiosity together with a lack of empathy; exploits relationships
Dependent · Cbeing taken care ofcannot make small decisions alone; volunteers for unpleasant tasks to secure reassurance

The narcissist's self-esteem is inflated and fragile — dependent on how others see them, which is why the need for admiration never ends.

Notice that last claim is a theory of mechanism presented as description. Whether grandiosity masks fragility or is primary is exactly what the grandiose/vulnerable subtype research disputes.

Ask before revealing rows two and three: "Histrionic and narcissistic are both self-focused. What is the difference?" The Guide's answer is what is sought — attention versus admiration — plus the empathy deficit, which is not a histrionic feature.

Dependent is pulled forward from Cluster C because the contrast lands better in a set of three: all three need other people, for different things. When a dependent relationship ends, the person quickly seeks a replacement.

Sources: histrionic-personality-disorder (Presentation, Diagnosis); narcissistic-personality-disorder (Presentation; Contested for the subtype dispute); dependent-personality-disorder (Presentation). The 2025 deck's myth of Narcissus fits here in one sentence.

Four worth knowing well

The differentials with teeth

  • Schizotypal: ideas of reference and odd perception — the one PD with an identified biological mechanism shared with schizophrenia. A bridge to next week.
  • Antisocial: disregard for others' rights without remorse. Requires conduct-disorder features before 15, cannot be diagnosed before 18 — the diagnosis has a childhood.
  • Avoidant vs social anxiety: both fear evaluation. The PD's marker is the stable negative self-concept — "they'll reject me" versus "I am inadequate."
  • OCPD vs OCD: perfectionism and control without obsessions or compulsions. The person with OCPD usually thinks they're right; the person with OCD knows something is wrong.

The last two contrasts are classic exam differentials, and the ego-syntonic/dystonic point in the OCPD line generalises to the whole chapter: personality pathology usually walks in complaining about something else.

Cluster B · the forensic one

Antisocial PD: the absence of remorse

Persistent disregard for and violation of others' rights, with deceitfulness and impulsivity as hallmarks. The most consequential feature is the absence of remorse — harm rationalised as the victim's fault, minimised, or met with indifference.

  • Three times more common in men; one of the three personality disorders (with borderline and schizotypal) where substance use disorders most often co-occur
  • Shares a neurological profile with borderline: low serotonin activity, deficient prefrontal functioning, an overly reactive amygdala — one of only two disorder-specific biological findings in the chapter
  • Modelling and reinforcement: parents who give in to stop escalation reward the aggression they are trying to end
  • Treatment: "limited and generally not effective" — and many arrive compelled

"Psychopath" and "sociopath" are lay words, not DSM ones. Whether psychopathy is a narrower construct with its own affective features is a real, unsettled dispute.

Open with the question: "Is antisocial personality disorder the same thing as psychopathy?" Let the room split, then the warn box: the Guide's own answer is that the source uses the lay terms without engaging the literature separating them, so the honest position is "not established as the same".

The treatment line is a strong claim resting on one citation (Black, 2015) with no effect sizes; the Guide's Contested section calls it close to therapeutic nihilism. Say so rather than letting it read as settled.

Sources: antisocial-personality-disorder (Presentation, Epidemiology, Etiology, Treatment, Contested); personality-disorders (Etiology, the two exceptions). The conduct-disorder link is already on the differentials slide. Do not quote the Guide's line that ASPD's sixfold prevalence gap is the chapter's largest — narcissistic (0.0% vs 6.2%) and schizotypal (3.9% vs 0.6%) are wider on the Guide's own tables.

2025 material not usable: the underarousal and fearlessness hypotheses, MAOA, and the 50–80% of male offenders figure are not in the Guide — not examinable as stated.

Who gets the label

Where does the sex difference live?

Borderline PD is diagnosed more often in women. True?

  • In clinical samples, yes. In community samples, no difference. Women seek help more, so more of them reach the clinic.
  • Histrionic: more common in women in clinical settings; no difference under structured assessment
  • Histrionic and antisocial read as exaggerated feminine and masculine stereotypes — and diagnosis rates track the reading

Two places the bias can live: criterion bias — the criteria pathologise one gender's socialised style — and assessment bias — clinicians apply neutral criteria unevenly. Clinicians given identical vignettes differing only in the patient's stated sex gave different diagnoses (Ford & Widiger, 1989).

Take a show of hands on "true?" before the first reveal; most say yes. The clinical-versus-community split is the clearest case in the course of a sampling route manufacturing an epidemiological "fact".

Sources: borderline-personality-disorder (Epidemiology; Contested); histrionic-personality-disorder (Epidemiology; Contested); personality-disorders ("Gender and the categories" — criterion versus assessment bias, the vignette study). Read any sex difference on these pages with that caution attached: it may describe the disorder, the criteria, or the diagnostician.

One pattern across ten disorders

The disorder obstructs its own treatment

Cluster ARarely see themselves as needing help. Then the paranoid patient cannot trust the clinician, and the schizoid patient has no desire to engage — two different obstacles.
Cluster BAntisocial: often compelled. Narcissistic: arrives only for a secondary problem, such as depression. Histrionic: seeks treatment more than any other PD — and brings the demands into the room.
Cluster CThe most options, borrowed from anxiety and depression treatment — but retention is hard. Dependent patients hand the obligations of treatment to the clinician: the problem, reproduced in therapy.

The stakes. In a Quebec provincial cohort, a Cluster B diagnosis cost a mean of 13 years of life expectancy for men and 9 years for women (Cailhol et al., 2017).

Ask first: "Why might treating a personality disorder be harder than treating depression?" The Guide's answer is more specific than "they're resistant": the obstacle is the disorder itself, in a different form for each cluster. This is the chapter's most consistent treatment finding, and it is not about which therapy works.

The mortality figure turns Cluster B from an interpersonal problem into a survival one, and it sets up the next slide: borderline, the one disorder where treatment demonstrably works.

Sources: personality-disorders (Treatment; "Canadian data, and the mortality finding"); the individual pages' Treatment sections. 2025's "fundamental requirements" list (structure, alliance, validation…) overlaps the Guide's shared features of effective treatments — structured, focused on agency, active, validating, supervised — use the Guide's list if you mention it.

The one with a treatment story

Borderline PD, and what DBT changed

Instability of relationships, self-image and affect; frantic efforts to avoid abandonment; recurrent self-harm.

  • The one personality disorder with a clearly effective treatment — dialectical behaviour therapy, with the strongest long-term psychodynamic evidence as well
  • Once considered untreatable; now carrying the best outcome literature in the chapter

Remember mindfulness inside DBT from Lecture 5 — acceptance and change held together. A "hopeless" diagnosis acquired a treatment within one working lifetime.

Also the stigma flashpoint: "borderline" functions as an insult in clinical back-channels. Name that plainly — the label's career is part of the syllabus question about what diagnoses do to people.

Dependent: three estimates within 0.2 points — the most consistent in the chapter, and the rarest of the ten on all three. Two of the other rows disagree about whether the disorder exists at all.

Consistency is not validity. It may mark a well-defined construct — or a threshold so high that only unambiguous cases are counted. The Guide leaves that open.

Put the table up and ask: "One row is the odd one out. Which, and why?" Most will pick narcissistic for the zero. Push: the zero is the normal pattern here — wild disagreement. The odd row is the one that agrees.

OCPD is the most prevalent PD of all ten on NESARC's 7.9%, and middling on NCS-R's 2.4% — which disorder counts as "most common" depends on the survey you cite.

Sources: DSM-5-TR Table 13.1 as given in personality-disorders (Epidemiology) and dependent-personality-disorder (Epidemiology, Contested).

The structural argument

Categories or dimensions — the manual argues with itself

You have just seen the prevalences wobble — two disorders undetected by one national survey. Even the sex differences move: paranoid PD's reverses between surveys.

The trait domains descend from normal personality's five-factor model — openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism. Pathology as the extremes of dimensions everyone is on — that is the dimensional claim in one line.

You already know the alternative from Lecture 2: the AMPD in Section III — impairment plus five trait domains, from which six of the ten categories can be rebuilt. ICD-11 went further and abolished the types outright.

This chapter is where the categorical model is weakest, and the manual knows it.

The 0.0%-vs-1.8% pair is the cleanest measurement scandal in the course — recall L2's reliable-but-valid scale. One breath, then move to law.

Part two

The law

How we got to s.16

Each test a reaction to the last

The pendulum swings between knowing and controlling — and each swing followed a verdict the public hated.

Everything left of 1992 is English or American. Four US states — Idaho, Kansas, Montana, Utah — have abolished the plea. "Guilty but mentally ill" confuses jurors, has not reduced insanity verdicts, and differs from guilty in name only. Canada has neither.

Ask as each point builds: "Is this test about what they knew, or what they could control?" M'Naghten knowing; irresistible impulse controlling; Durham neither (too vague to say); ALI both; IDRA back to knowing after the Hinckley verdict. That rhythm is the lesson — the dates are for orientation, not memorising.

M'Naghten (1843) killed Peel's secretary while trying to kill Peel himself, and was acquitted on persecutory delusions; the tightened rule was the response to the outrage. The 2025 deck's two M'Naghten slides compress into that one sentence.

Sources: insanity-defense-standards (all five US tests, current scope); law-and-ethics ("Criminal commitment and the insanity defence" for GBMI; "Canadian law" for s.16, 1992). The insanity-defense-standards page is entirely US-built and says so. 2025's "about 1% plead, about 20% succeed, NGRI means longer confinement" is not in the Guide — not examinable as stated; the page lists exactly that data as a research need.

Canada, 1992

NCRMD — not criminally responsible on account of mental disorder

Criminal Code s.16: not criminally responsible if a mental disorder rendered the person incapable of appreciating the nature and quality of the act, or of knowing that it was wrong.

  • Canada has no "guilty but mentally ill" verdict, unlike several US states
  • An NCRMD verdict routes to a provincial Review Board — reviewed at least yearly — with three dispositions: hospital detention, conditional discharge, absolute discharge

Not a loophole and not a release: a structured, indefinitely reviewable system that ends when risk does.

Keep the companion doctrine straight: fitness to stand trial asks about now — can this person understand the proceedings and instruct counsel today? NCRMD asks about then — the moment of the act. Exams love this confusion; don't donate marks to it.

s.16 descends from the M'Naghten lineage on the previous slide. The Review Board mechanism is the real difference from the US, not the test wording. (That "appreciating" was chosen over "knowing" on purpose is not in the Guide — don't examine it.)

Fitness: the Guide's "Competency to stand trial" section gives only the US federal standard (rational and factual understanding; able to consult with counsel). Canadian fitness law is listed on that page as a research need, so keep the now/then contrast conceptual. An unfit accused is treated until fit, then tried — the doctrines run in sequence, which is the cleanest way to keep them apart.

Who is found NCRMD (National Trajectory Project): 70.9% psychotic-spectrum, 72% with a prior psychiatric hospitalisation. Source: law-and-ethics ("Canadian law, and other jurisdictions").

The data against the stereotype

The National Trajectory Project: 1,800 NCRMD cases

  • Homicide and attempted murder: under 7% of NCRMD verdicts; death-related offences 3.2%
  • The largest categories: threats (27.4%) and assaults (26.5%)
  • Where there was a victim: family and partners 33.7%; strangers 22.7%
  • For homicide specifically, 60.8% of victims were family or partners

The random stranger attack that anchors the public image is the least typical case in the data.

Recall L1's Burczycka slide — people with mental illness are twice as likely to be victimised. These two datasets belong together and between them dissolve most of the folk criminology.

The clinician's collision

Tarasoff: where confidentiality ends

Tarasoff (1976): when a client makes a credible threat against an identifiable person, the duty to protect can override confidentiality. Thompson (1980) narrowed it to specific threats.

That is a US rule — and not even a uniform one: states split between mandatory, permissive and no duty. Canada took a different road, next slide.

Notice what the rule does: it makes the clinician servant of two duties — to the client, and to a person the clinician has never met.

Connect back to L1's Case 4 (the man with a plan and no distress): this is the machinery that case was waiting for.

Tarasoff II (1976) broadened the duty to warn into a duty to protect, dischargeable by warning, notifying police or arranging hospitalisation. Johnson et al. (2014): 23 states mandatory by statute, 10 by common law, 11 permissive, 6 silent — and 76% of psychologists misdescribed their own state's law despite 87% having had Tarasoff training. Sources: tarasoff-duty-to-warn; law-and-ethics (duty-to-warn section).

Canada, 1999 · Supreme Court

Smith v. Jones: a permission, not a duty

A psychiatrist retained by the defence heard an accused describe a detailed plan to attack an identified group. The trial judge said he had a duty to disclose. The Court of Appeal changed that to a permission — and the Supreme Court affirmed the permission.

FactorWhat it asks
Claritya risk to an identifiable person or group? ("anyone he meets" may be too vague)
Seriousnessa risk of death or serious bodily harm?
Imminencea sense of urgency — though the danger may lie some time in the future

Weighted, not counted. And even when met, disclose only what public safety needs.

The dissent: disclose the opinion, not the accused's own words — otherwise dangerous people stop confiding, go unidentified, and public safety suffers. He was only diagnosed because he felt safe to talk.

Ask: "California says a clinician must warn. What does Canada say?" Most students assume Canada copied Tarasoff. It did not: California imposes a duty, Canada grants a permission. That changes where the professional's liability and discretion sit.

Second difference: Smith v. Jones is a solicitor-client privilege case (the psychiatrist was retained by defence counsel), where Tarasoff grew out of a therapeutic relationship and a negligence claim. The Court considered Tarasoff directly, so the divergence is a considered choice.

Source: law-and-ethics ("The Canadian position: Smith v Jones (1999)"), which works from the judgment itself.

The other doorway

Civil commitment: dangerousness does the work

Criminal law asks about responsibility for a past act. Civil commitment asks about risk of a future one — detention without any offence at all.

  • Two authorities do the detaining: parens patriae — the state as protector of those who cannot protect themselves — and police power, the state as protector of everyone else
  • The criterion is typically danger to self or others plus mental disorder — the criterion Lecture 1 showed is not even part of the DSM's definition
  • And prediction of rare events is exactly what Lecture 2 taught us to be humble about

So the strongest legal power in mental health law rests on the weakest criterion in the definitional toolkit, applied to a population more likely to be harmed than harming. Hold all three of those at once — that is the honest position.

This slide is the lecture's thesis. Deliver it slowly. Patients'-rights machinery (PPAO in Ontario, rights advice on detention) is the counterweight — one minute, from the Guide's page. Two sentences of that minute: a capable patient can refuse treatment even while detained (Starson, three slides on) — and the historical backdrop is deinstitutionalization: the asylums emptied faster than community care was built, and the shortfall is visible in homelessness today. That is moral treatment's failure shape from L1, run a second time — works, scales badly, underfunded, applied unequally.

The hinge of civil commitment

Can dangerousness be predicted?

  • The definition is vague. Physical harm, clearly. Psychological abuse? Property damage? Unsettled.
  • The best predictor is often inadmissible. Past criminal activity predicts future dangerousness reasonably well, and frequently cannot be introduced in court.
  • Behaviour depends on context. Settled in one setting, enraged in the next.

Mental illness is a weak risk factor for violence: about 4% of criminal violence is attributable to people with mental illness, and they are about three times more likely to be targets of violence than perpetrators.

Yet 70% of US news coverage of serious mental illness and gun violence, 1997–2012, centred on extreme events and specific shootings.

Ask before the box: "What share of violent crime do you think is committed by people with a mental illness?" Take guesses — they will run far above 4%. Then reveal, and pair it with the National Trajectory Project slide: most NCRMD cases are threats and assaults, mostly against family.

Honesty clause, from the same Guide section: people reporting diagnosable levels of symptoms also report more involvement in violence than the general population (Rozel & Mulvey, 2017) — "weak risk factor" is not "no risk factor". Say it.

The asymmetry that makes this an ethical problem: criminal conviction needs proof beyond reasonable doubt; the US civil-commitment standard is "clear and convincing" proof, about 75% certainty — the lower bar for people who have done nothing wrong. US framing (Bridley & Daffin); the Ontario route is the next slide.

Sources: law-and-ethics ("Dangerousness and the prediction problem": Metzl & MacLeish 2015; Choe et al. 2008; McGinty et al. 2014 — US data). "Overview: two systems" for the burden-of-proof asymmetry.

Ontario · Mental Health Act s.15

Form 1: one signature, 72 hours

No petition, no hearing, no judge. One physician — not necessarily a psychiatrist — who has personally examined the person within seven days.

A Form 1 cannot be appealed to the Consent and Capacity Board. Ontario puts review after detention; the US model puts a judge before it — speed traded against pre-deprivation review.

Box A is the serious-harm test. Box B allows assessment of an incapable person whose recurrent disorder has responded to treatment before — no prediction of harm required. Prevention of deterioration, or coercion beyond dangerousness?

Ask first: "In Ontario, who decides that someone can be held for a psychiatric assessment against their will?" Most will say a judge or a psychiatrist. The answer is any physician, alone. Then build the exits.

Box B is the discussion point: it sidesteps the prediction problem on the previous slide by not requiring a prediction at all. Put the question to the room without resolving it. Also worth one sentence: a person brought in by police under s.17 without a Form 1 or Form 2 is not automatically a psychiatric patient, and there is no automatic authority to hold them.

For a psychologist this is structural: they cannot sign a Form 1; their route runs through a physician. PPAO (the Psychiatric Patient Advocate Office) takes questions about detentions — the number is on the Guide page, re-check it each term.

Source: law-and-ethics ("How this actually works in Ontario: the Form 1"; "When the risk is to the patient"). Course reference material, not legal advice — the page says the same of itself.

What detention does not take · and whom the system takes

Starson and Gladue

Starson v. Swayze (SCC, 2003)

A capable patient may refuse psychiatric treatment even while detained. Capacity, not the wisdom of the choice, is the test.

Detention and treatment are separate legal questions.

R. v. Gladue (SCC, 1999)

Sentencing judges must consider an Indigenous person's background — colonialism, displacement, residential schools — and alternatives to prison.

Indigenous people: about 5% of Canadians, about a third of federal prisoners.

Given the trauma- and substance-related disorder rates elsewhere in the Guide, over-incarceration is a mental-health issue wearing a criminal-justice costume.

Starson first, as a question: "His doctors thought refusing medication was a bad decision. Should that be enough to override him?" The Supreme Court said no — his refusal was capable though his physicians believed it unwise. The 2025 deck told the full story (Professor Starson's case history, the Joan Rivers threats, the later deterioration and treatment order); none of that detail is in the Guide, so tell it as a story, not as examinable fact.

Gladue interprets Criminal Code s.718.2(e); Gladue reports carry the background analysis into sentencing, and restorative justice is among the alternatives they open. The Guide's figures (about 5% and about a third, Office of the Correctional Investigator) supersede last year's 3% and 20%. Last year's "8% lower recidivism" for restorative justice is not in the Guide — not examinable as stated; the page lists outcome evidence as a research need.

Source: law-and-ethics ("Gladue, restorative justice, and who the system actually detains").

Ontario · call it before the reveal

Must, may, or no duty?

You learn in session that…The duty
a 14-year-old may be being harmed at homeMUST — immediately, on suspicion, yourself; an offence for professionals (CYFSA s.125)
a client credibly plans serious harm to an identifiable personMAY — a permission to disclose (PHIPA; Smith v. Jones)
another regulated health professional sexually abused a clientMUST — report to that professional's College
a 74-year-old in her own home is being financially exploitedNO DUTY — the elder-abuse duty attaches to the setting
the same, but she lives in a long-term care homeMUST

And a 16-year-old? The mandatory child-protection duty stops at 16; for 16- and 17-year-olds reporting is permitted, not required. Telling your supervisor is not reporting.

Run it as a room vote: read each row, hands up for must / may / none, then reveal. The fourth row is where the room goes wrong, and in the dangerous direction — many US states have general adult-protective duties; Ontario does not. The justification is capacity and autonomy: a competent adult may make choices others consider unwise.

CYFSA s.125 details worth one breath each: the threshold is reasonable suspicion ("neither firm evidence nor the need to investigate"); it names psychologists; the fine is up to $5,000; it overrides confidentiality and PHIPA but preserves solicitor-client privilege; the duty is ongoing (a further report even if the child has been reported before); and you may not rely on anyone else to report for you.

Why the last rule exists: in Tufford and Lee's survey of 439 Ontario social workers, only 48.8%–79.8% would definitely report depending on the vignette, and a colleague's opinion was the top-rated influence. The legislature wrote s.125(3) against exactly that behaviour.

Source: law-and-ethics ("Mandated reporting"; "The statute: CYFSA 2017, section 125"; "Elder abuse"; "Reporting other professionals" — the RHPA duty as CRPO states it; "The PHIPA test"). Course reference, not legal advice.

Canadian Code of Ethics for Psychologists · 4th ed., 2017

Four principles — ranked

Most codes list values and leave the balancing to you. The CPA Code says which generally wins. Guess which comes last.

PrincipleWeight when principles conflict
I · Respect for the Dignity of Persons and Peopleshighest — except where there is a clear and imminent danger of bodily harm
II · Responsible Caringsecond
III · Integrity in Relationshipsthird
IV · Responsibility to Societylowest

The dignity of persons "should not be sacrificed to a vision of the greater good of society." And Principle I's one exception — clear and imminent danger of bodily harm — is the same threshold Smith v. Jones set. Law and ethics point the same way, and both are narrow.

The ask is on the slide: "which comes last?" Most guess Integrity. Responsibility to Society last is a substantive claim — an explicit refusal to let utilitarian reasoning override the individual — and it is the sentence to quote when commitment, duty to warn or forensic assessment turns up as an essay question.

Two further points from the Guide if time allows: the Code sets a ten-step decision process, and a psychologist who shows every reasonable effort was made to apply it is deemed to have followed it — the Code judges the deliberation, not only the answer. And the Code is not the enforcement mechanism; provincial regulators define misconduct.

2025's autonomy / non-maleficence / beneficence / fidelity / justice list is a general bioethics framing; the Guide teaches the CPA's four ranked principles instead, so teach these.

Source: law-and-ethics ("The Canadian Code of Ethics for Psychologists").
