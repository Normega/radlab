Part one

Eating disorders

The chapter's one dividing line

Is body image driving it, or not?

Body image central:

anorexia nervosa · bulimia nervosa · binge-eating disorder

Body image absent:

ARFID — restriction from sensory aversion, fear of choking, or indifference to food, with none of the weight/shape concern

Everything else in the chapter follows from that split — including why ARFID's treatment looks nothing like anorexia's.

ARFID is the diagnosis most of the room has never heard of and some will recognise personally ("picky eating" taken to impairment). One respectful minute.

The differential in two constructs

Binge and compensation sort the big three

Binge?Compensate?Weight
Anorexiasometimessometimessignificantly low
Bulimiayesyestypically normal range
Binge-eating disorderyesnoany

Weight status separates anorexia from bulimia — a person who binges, purges, and is significantly underweight gets the anorexia diagnosis (binge-purge type), not bulimia. The compensation column separates bulimia from BED.

And the normal-weight row is not the safe row. Purging drives electrolyte disturbance, and electrolyte disturbance stops hearts. Bulimia harms medically at any weight — which is exactly the presentation a "they look fine" heuristic dismisses.

This table is the EMQ material for the final. The anorexia-trumps-bulimia rule is the one they get wrong.

A binge needs both a clearly large amount and loss of control — and it is the loss of control that separates it from ordinary overeating. Say it before someone asks.

Who gets them

Which one has the most even sex ratio?

WomenMen
Anorexia · at any one time0.3–0.4%0.1%
Bulimia · at any one time1.0%0.1%
Binge-eating disorder · ever develop it0.2–3.5%0.9–2.0%

Binge-eating disorder is the closest to even — and it is the one with the least body-image content. If this pathology were purely female, that is not where the gap would narrow. The male anorexia and bulimia figures may partly count who comes forward for treatment.

Muscle dysmorphia runs the same over-valuation of shape in the other direction — a drive for size, not thinness — and is most common in men. And note the attention mismatch: anorexia is the rarest of the three, and the deadliest.

Ask before the last row: "which of the three do you think comes closest to even?" Most will guess anorexia or bulimia. Then reveal binge-eating disorder.

Honest caveat to say out loud: the rows mix measures (point prevalence for anorexia and bulimia, "develop it" for BED), so compare the women/men ratio within a row, not magnitudes across rows. Source: feeding-and-eating-disorders (Epidemiology), muscle-dysmorphia. Don't quote the overview's "roughly 10:1" for anorexia — its own figures give about 3–4:1 (reported as a Guide error).

Bulimia's engine

The binge doesn't keep it going — the aftermath does

Common triggers for a binge: negative mood, interpersonal stress, dieting, boredom, bad feelings about weight and shape.

Which is why CBT for bulimia starts with a journal of eating, hunger and feelings to find the triggers, then targets thoughts like "I can't stop once I start" — and uses exposure with response prevention to block the purge itself.

At least weekly for three months. Shame keeps it hidden: binges happen in secret, and the cycle often runs for years before anyone seeks help.

Ask before the second half of the loop: "what happens right after a binge — and why would that make the next one more likely?" Build the loop from their answers.

The Guide's framing: post-binge disgust and thoughts of failure sustain the cycle — the maintaining mechanism is the aftermath, not the binge (feeding-and-eating-disorders, Etiology; bulimia-nervosa, Presentation and Treatment; binge-eating-and-compensatory-behavior). ERP for purging is reported as particularly useful where OCD is comorbid — a bridge to the OCD-variant slide.

Most common, recognised last

Binge-eating disorder: what separates it from overeating?

Recurrent binges — a clearly large amount and loss of control — with no compensation, at least weekly for three months, and marked distress about it. Plus three or more of:

eating unusually fast · eating past comfortable fullness · large amounts without hunger · eating alone from embarrassment · disgusted, depressed or guilty afterwards

Loss of control, not quantity. A criterion built on volume alone would medicalise appetite. People with BED have worse quality of life than people with obesity but no BED — the comparison that separates the disorder from the weight it usually comes with.

DSM-5 loosened the threshold from DSM-IV's twice weekly for six months — and the change barely moved prevalence.

The sharpest objection: over-valuation of shape and weight is present in about 60% of BED patients and predicts worse outcome — yet it is not in the criteria.

Open with the question in the title: "what makes a binge different from a big holiday dinner?" Someone will say "the amount" — the answer is that the amount is necessary but loss of control is what makes it a disorder.

Promoted from a research appendix to a full diagnosis in DSM-5. All from binge-eating-disorder (Diagnosis; Contested, Amianto et al. 2015). Avoid quoting the page's "three times more common than anorexia and bulimia" — it doesn't reconcile with the prevalence table on the same page (reported).

Deaths come from both directions: the medical consequences of starvation, and suicide. Treatment is not cosmetic — it is survival.

Deliver the numbers plainly and stop. No imagery, no case description of methods — the numbers alone reset how seriously the room takes the next fifteen minutes.

Where the deaths come from

The body keeps the record

Starvation

lanugo — fine downy hair, the body insulating itself · loss of menstruation · slow heart rate, low blood pressure · thinning bones, toward fractures

Purging — at any weight, in either diagnosis

enamel erosion on the inner tooth surfaces · swollen parotid glands · Russell's sign: calluses on the knuckles

The shared pathway: electrolytes, potassium above all. Low potassium produces cardiac arrhythmia — the common route of much of the medical death toll.

Every sign here shows up in an ordinary medical exam — Lecture 2's physical-exam rule again. And recovery needs supervision: refeeding too fast is itself dangerous (refeeding syndrome).

Ask: "which of these would a family doctor notice at a routine visit?" All of them — which is the point.

Vocabulary for the final: lanugo, amenorrhea (no longer a diagnostic criterion, still clinically standard), bradycardia and hypotension, osteopenia, Russell's sign. Sources: anorexia-nervosa and bulimia-nervosa, "Medical signs and complications" (Mehler & Brown 2015; Mehler & Rylander 2015).

Causes · the twin numbers

Taught as a pair, with very different genetics

Twin concordance: if one twin has the disorder, how often does the other?

Anorexia is strongly familial — first-degree relatives are up to six times as likely to be diagnosed. Bulimia's identical-twin figure is a third of anorexia's.

What the three share: the transdiagnostic model — low self-esteem → over-valuation of weight and shape → restricting, bingeing or purging → lower self-esteem. Its best evidence is that people move between the diagnoses over time. Perfectionism is linked to anorexia specifically.

Ask before revealing the bulimia bars: "guess bulimia's identical-twin figure." Most guess something near anorexia's.

Two cautions from the Guide. Hormonal disruption is common in anorexia but the arrow is open — starvation itself changes hormones — so don't teach it as a cause. And the thin-ideal/media account is the one students arrive with; the Guide explicitly does not adjudicate it. Family patterns (enmeshed, critical) are correlational and absent in many families — the reason FBT refuses to blame.

Sources: anorexia-nervosa, bulimia-nervosa (Etiology), feeding-and-eating-disorders, transdiagnostic-model-of-eating-disorders. Last year's "heritability 0.56" and serotonin/hormone lines are not in the Guide — not examinable as stated.

The best-evidenced adolescent treatment

Family-based therapy hands refeeding to the parents

  1. Phase 1: parents take complete control of eating — the family refeeds, the therapist coaches
  2. Phase 2: control returns to the adolescent, gradually, as weight restores
  3. Phase 3: ordinary adolescent autonomy — identity, independence, the developmental work the illness interrupted

Weight restoration at one year: 50–60%. Untested in older patients — the evidence is adolescent-specific.

Note what FBT rejects: the idea that the family caused the illness. Parents are recruited as the treatment, not blamed as the cause.

FBT runs 16–18 outpatient sessions. Its evidence stops at adolescence; for older patients it is undetermined — the adult picture is next.

The no-blame stance is historically loaded — older etiological stories blamed mothers. FBT's success is part of what buried that. Worth one sentence of history.

The adult line matters because most of this room is past FBT's evidence window. Sources: family-based-therapy, anorexia-nervosa (Treatment). Removed from the previous draft: "CBT-E" and "fluoxetine as the adjunct in bulimia" — neither is in the Guide.

Adult treatment · no single school

Guess which therapy has anorexia's strongest trials

Best supportedWhat the trials show
AnorexiaPsychodynamic (focal)52% improved vs 21% in routine care. ANTOP trial: same weight gain as CBT, better global recovery, and inpatient care for 23% vs 35% (CBT) and 41% (usual care)
BulimiaCBTslightly ahead of psychodynamic therapy. Interpersonal therapy (IPT-E) after CBT non-response: slower, equal at long-term follow-up
Binge-eatingCBTbinge remission 50–60% — with little weight change. Fluoxetine beats placebo, with smaller effects than CBT

The psychodynamic lead is contested: ANTOP's usual care likely included unmanualised psychodynamic work, and allegiance effects run through this literature. Age moderates everything — younger patients do better with family-based treatment, older ones with individual therapy.

Ask the title question and take a quick show of hands: CBT, psychodynamic, medication? Nearly everyone says CBT. Reveal anorexia first — it is one of the few places in this course where psychodynamic therapy holds the strongest trial evidence.

Sources: anorexia-nervosa (Treatment, Contested), bulimia-nervosa (Treatment), binge-eating-disorder (Treatment, Contested), feeding-and-eating-disorders (Treatment). For BED the Guide's Module 10 summary says antidepressants help comorbid depression rather than the eating disorder itself; the fluoxetine-vs-placebo result is from the Contested section — teach both halves.

The feeding disorder

ARFID: not a milder anorexia — a different kind of thing

Three routes to restriction, none of them about weight or shape:

  1. Sensory — texture, smell, colour, taste, temperature
  2. Conditioned aversion — after choking, repeated vomiting, a painful procedure
  3. No interest in eating at all

Someone can be severely underweight and tube-fed with no weight concern whatsoever. That absence is the check to run on any suspected case.

A sex ratio that dissolves: ARFID looks male-dominated because it is common in autistic children. Without autism, it occurs about equally in boys and girls.

Treatment is behavioural: reinforce eating a previously refused food (differential reinforcement), and train parents to run it at meals — breaking the cycle of refusal, frustration and mealtime tension.

Ask: "a clinic sees mostly boys with ARFID — is it a male disorder?" Let them find the third variable. It is the cleanest example this term of a demographic pattern fully explained by comorbidity.

New to DSM-5, replacing feeding disorder of infancy or early childhood. Prevalence largely unknown (0.3% in one Australian 15+ sample). Source: avoidant-restrictive-food-intake-disorder.

A live argument

Are eating disorders an OCD variant?

The case for: shape/weight preoccupations behave like obsessions; food rituals behave like compulsions; comorbidity is high.

The case against: the transdiagnostic model — low self-esteem driving over-evaluation of weight and shape — explains the eating disorders together, without OCD.

DSM-5-TR keeps them in separate chapters. Remember Lecture 3: chapter membership is a hypothesis, and this is another live one.

Callback to the OCD-chapter-is-a-hypothesis material. If a student wants a contribution topic, this debate page has open gaps.

Sleep

The map, by what goes wrong

Six ways sleep breaks

Insomnia disordersleep won't come, or won't stay
Hypersomnolencesleep is abundant and never enough
Narcolepsysleep intrudes into waking — irresistibly
Obstructive sleep apneabreathing stops, 10–20+ seconds at a time

Narcolepsy is the odd one out: every other row is sleep being absent, disturbed or displaced. Narcolepsy is sleep invading.

Fast tour, one line each; the slides that follow do the real work. The next slide is the only sleep physiology in the room all term, and the parasomnia split after break 2 hangs off it.

If anyone asks which manual: sleep medicine uses its own classification (ICSD-3), and ICD-11 puts these disorders outside the mental-disorders chapter altogether (sleep-wake-disorders, Contested).

The physiology you need

A night is not one state

Sleep cycles roughly every 90 minutes between non-REM — down through light stages into deep slow-wave sleep — and REM, where dreaming concentrates and the muscles are paralysed.

So "when in the night does it happen?" is a diagnostic question: deep non-REM fills the first cycles, and REM lengthens toward morning.

Ask before the labels: "if sleep terrors come out of deep sleep, when in the night should a parent expect them?" The first few hours. Then the REM label.

The hypnogram is stylised, not a recording. Two regulators: the circadian clock and a homeostatic drive (sleep rebound after deprivation). The pons runs REM; the hypothalamus and thalamus run slow-wave sleep — which is why the two parasomnia families are different problems. Source: sleep-wake-disorders ("Normal sleep", "Sleep architecture in brief"). Last year's 20/30/50% stage proportions are not in the Guide.

The odd one out

Narcolepsy is REM sleep at the wrong time

  • Sleep that cannot be resisted — a minute to half an hour, waking refreshed
  • Cataplexy: sudden loss of muscle tone, up to full collapse — REM's paralysis, arriving while awake
  • About a third have vivid, dream-like hallucinations during attacks
  • Set off by arousal and strong emotion — the reverse of what stress does to most sleepers

The biology is unusually specific: reduced hypocretin signalling. Yet the standard drugs, stimulants, don't act on hypocretin — and in one case only a stimulant plus a REM-suppressing antidepressant worked.

Average time from first symptom to diagnosis: 8 to 19 years. Onset peaks around 15, and it gets mistaken for ADHD, depression, insomnia — and, because of the hallucinations, schizophrenia.

Ask before the box: "narcolepsy has a known mechanism and effective treatment — so how long do you think diagnosis takes?" Take guesses, then reveal.

Rare: 25–50 per 100,000. Presenting with cataplexy shortens the delay. In children, sleepiness can look like inattention or hyperactivity, so they get assessed for ADHD. This slide is the answer to the exercise's "man who collapses laughing". Source: narcolepsy (Presentation, Epidemiology, Treatment, Contested). Last year's prevalence figure and chromosome-6 line are not in the Guide.

The one everyone misses

Apnea: the patient is the last to know

Breathing stops for 10–20 seconds or longer. The sleeper rouses briefly, over and over through the night, and remembers none of it.

  • Presents as daytime exhaustion, poor concentration, low mood — which looks exactly like depression
  • Many people are referred by a partner who heard the snoring and the silences, not by their own complaint — and at any weight: the overweight stereotype is how the rest go undiagnosed

Lecture 2's physical-exam rule, in action: an organic condition producing psychiatric-looking symptoms. Screen for sleep before treating "depression" that doesn't respond.

And it has a device that works: CPAP — pressurised air holding the airway open, effective from mild to severe cases. Adherence, not efficacy, is the treatment problem.

The strongest single reason this chapter is in an abnormal psych course. Someone in this room will one day catch a misdiagnosed apnea because of this slide.

Obstructive = the airway blocks (mechanical); central = the brain's breathing signal fails (neurological). Apnea may also worsen cardiovascular disease. Source: obstructive-sleep-apnea-hypopnea. The Guide gives no prevalence figure — last year's "6% of Canadians" is not in it.

Circadian rhythm disorders

The sleep is normal. The timing isn't.

Delayed phaseasleep 2–4 a.m., up 10 a.m.–1 p.m.adolescents and young adults — the delay peaks near age 20
Advanced phaseasleep 6–9 p.m., up 2–5 a.m.older adults · ~1%
Non-24-hoursleep drifts later every dayup to 70% of blind people
Shift workthe clock against the roster~15% of full-time workers do shift work
Jet lagtemporary, after crossing time zonesabout a day per zone to adjust

The test: symptoms vanish when the schedule is unconstrained — holidays, weekends, retirement. Let a delayed-phase student sleep from 3 to 11 and they sleep perfectly well.

Which is why later school start times improved sleep, lateness and grades — treating the schedule, not the person. The clock itself runs about 24.18 hours, so light has to reset it every day.

Ask: "hands up if, left alone on a weekend, you'd sleep 3 to 11." Then the test line: that is what separates a circadian disorder from insomnia.

Endogenous (the clock is at fault: delayed, advanced, non-24, irregular) vs exogenous (shift work, jet lag: the clock is fine, the schedule is impossible). Diagnosed from 7–14+ days of sleep diaries and wrist actigraphy, not one night. Non-24 in blind people depends on whether the melanopsin light pathway survives, not on image vision. Melatonin: 0.1–0.3 mg gives physiological levels — far below retail doses. Source: circadian-rhythm-sleep-wake-disorders (ICSD-3, six subtypes; irregular sleep-wake rhythm left off the table for space).

Circadian logic

Light and melatonin push in opposite directions

The clock is set by two levers whose phase-response curves sit roughly 180° apart: light exposure and melatonin.

Same lever, different hour, opposite effect — evening light delays the clock; morning light advances it.

Which means timing is the treatment. The jet-lagged, the shift worker and the delayed-phase student all need the same two levers — pulled at different times.

Practical beat: ask the room what late-night phone light does to a delayed-phase student. They know. Now they know why.

Reading a guideline honestly

A table ranks what has been tested, not what works

The hypersomnolence guideline: one strong recommendation resting on a single RCT, while narcolepsy has nine trials behind its rankings.

And whole populations — psychiatric hypersomnia among them — fall between two specialties' guidelines, so no one's table covers them.

Absence from the table is absence of evidence — not evidence the treatment fails.

Two-out-of-seventeen's gentler cousin. This is also the standing example for spot-the-limitation items on the final.

Parasomnias

Sort them by the stage they come from

StageWhat happens
Sleepwalkingdeep non-REMcomplex behaviour, up to driving a car — eyes open, unreachable
Sleep terrorsdeep non-REMpanic, screaming, trying to escape; can't be consoled
REM sleep behaviour disorderREMthe paralysis fails, so dreams are acted out — kicking, punching, injuring a partner

The common thread: no memory of the episode.

REM sleep behaviour disorder is an early marker for neurodegenerative disease, Parkinson's especially — a sleep disorder flagging a neurological one.

Benzodiazepines didn't stop sleepwalking in one study — but every patient whose sleepwalking came with disordered breathing improved markedly once the breathing was treated. Sleep terrors generally need no treatment; REM behaviour disorder gets clonazepam, often with melatonin, and a safer bedroom.

Callback to the night-of-sleep diagram: ask which half of the night each row belongs to. Then ask why the stage matters for someone who kicks in their sleep — the answer is the Parkinson's marker.

Nightmare disorder belongs to REM (sleep-wake-disorders, architecture section); the parasomnias page itself has no nightmare material, so keep it to that one line. Don't teach restless legs as a parasomnia: DSM-5-TR classes it separately, as the page itself flags. Source: parasomnias (Presentation, Diagnosis, Treatment; Guilleminault et al. 2005). Last year's sexsomnia and sleep-eating items are not in the Guide.

Supreme Court of Canada · 1992

R v Parks: is sleepwalking a disease of the mind?

One night Kenneth Parks drove 23 km to his in-laws' home, killed his mother-in-law and injured his father-in-law — then drove to a police station and reported it. Five defence experts testified that he was sleepwalking. The Crown called none.

Convicted, not criminally responsible, or acquitted?

Acquitted — and the acquittal stood. An involuntary act earns a full acquittal unless it stems from a "disease of the mind", which yields not criminally responsible instead. On this record, the Crown had not shown that sleepwalking was one.

"Disease of the mind" is a legal term, not a medical one: experts inform it, the judge decides it. And the holding is narrow — different evidence could go the other way. In a US case, Scott Falater's sleepwalking defence failed: first-degree murder, 1999.

Brief content note: this is a homicide case. Take a hand vote on the three options before revealing — most pick "not criminally responsible".

The point for this course: a diagnostic category and a legal category can share a name without being the same object. The Court rejected the internal-cause test for sleepwalking because internal and external causes blur during sleep, and found no likelihood of recurrence. Sources: sleep-wake-disorders (R v Parks subsection, course reference material, not legal advice); parasomnias (Contested, Falater).

The common one

Insomnia, and the trap of trying harder

Long delays falling asleep; waking repeatedly; the bed becoming a place where not sleeping happens.

The maintenance loop: effort to sleep → arousal → failure → the bed itself becomes a cue for wakefulness → more effort. Sleep is the one goal that recedes when pursued.

Which is why CBT-I attacks the conditioning — leave the bed when sleep won't come, restrict time in bed — and why it comes first: it dismantles the loop rather than sedating past it.

Name the medication half too: many sleep medications produce dependence, alter the sleep cycle, and can increase insomnia over time. And the longest controlled trial of nightly benzodiazepine use ran eight weeks — for a drug people take for years.

Note the deep rhyme with panic's misinterpretation loop and OCD's relief loop — third appearance of a self-sustaining cycle this term. Students who spot the pattern have the exam's mechanism questions half-solved.

Scale: insomnia is the most common sleep disorder, in a chapter where 30–50% of people experience some sleep disorder in their lives. The Guide gives no prevalence for insomnia disorder itself, so don't quote one (last year's "one-fourth of the population" is not in it).

Worth one sentence: DSM-5 merged "primary" and "secondary" insomnia because clinicians couldn't tell them apart — and insomnia predicts later depression as well as following it, so treat it in its own right. Sources: insomnia-disorder (Etiology, Treatment, Contested), sleep-wake-disorders (Treatment).
