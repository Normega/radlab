Where is the line?

Exercise A · 12 minutes

Four people

For each: disorder, or not? Commit before you hear anyone else.

  • Answer all four on the dashboard
  • We look at the class split before anyone argues
  • Then the only question that matters: what made you say that?

Case 1

A first-year student stops going to class

She moved across the province in September and did well for a month. Now she skips lectures, has dropped out of her friend group, and spends most of the day in bed. She says it's homesickness.

Disorder, or an ordinary reaction?

Homesickness in week six is ordinary. What makes this clinical is not the sadness — it is that it has persisted and spread across every domain until she is functioning in none of them.

This is the textbook's own opening case. Most rooms split near evenly, which is the point. Draw out "how long" and "how much of her life" as the two dimensions doing the work.

Case 2

A man grieves his wife

Six weeks after her death he cries most days, has lost weight, sleeps badly, and cannot concentrate at work. He says he does not want to feel better yet, because feeling better would mean letting her go.

Distress is not in question. Is this disorder?

Distress alone cannot define abnormality. Grief produces genuine suffering in someone whose response is working exactly as it should.

Push on the last sentence of the vignette — the refusal to feel better is often what tips a room toward "disorder", and it is the most human thing in the case.

Flag lightly: DSM-5-TR does have prolonged grief disorder, gated at twelve months. We do that properly in Lecture 4. Today it is here to break the distress criterion.

Case 3

A woman sees her late mother

Twice since the funeral she has seen her mother sitting in the kitchen, and spoken to her. She is not frightened. In her community, visits from the recently dead are expected and are considered a comfort.

Hallucination. Disorder?

In some cultures visions are normal and positively valued. The behaviour has not changed — only who is judging it.

Do not rush past the discomfort here. Some students will want a rule that survives culture; there isn't one, and that is the lesson.

If it comes up: yes, DSM has a culture-and-context caveat. It is a caveat, not an algorithm.

Case 4

A man plans to hurt someone

He is not distressed. He is not impaired — he holds a job and has friends. He has described, calmly and specifically, an intention to harm a colleague.

Dangerous. Disordered?

Dangerousness is not part of the DSM's definition of a mental disorder. Clinicians add it. Having a disorder does not make a person dangerous, and being dangerous does not make a person ill.

This is the criterion students are most surprised to lose. Note that the duty to report expressed intent to harm is real and separate — a legal and ethical obligation, not a diagnosis. Lecture 10 does duty-to-warn properly.

What you just used

Four criteria, and not one of them survives alone

They are combined by clinical judgement, not by a decision rule. The DSM itself says no definition captures every disorder in the book.

Point back at the criteria the room generated in Exercise A — they will have named most of these without the vocabulary. That is the moment the lecture earns its keep.

Canada · worth stating out loud

Who is actually in danger

The dangerousness criterion asks whether a person is a risk to others. The Canadian data runs the other way.

One in ten people with mental-health-related disabilities in Canada reported experiencing violence in the past year — double the rate in the general population.

Burczycka (2018)

People with mental illness in Canada are also more likely to face social isolation, unemployment, lower educational attainment and lower income.

Say plainly: the population the dangerousness criterion is applied to is at twice the general risk of being harmed. This is the fact to repeat whenever a news cycle runs the other way.

Part two

How we got here

The through-line

Explanation determines treatment

How a society explains disordered behaviour decides what it does to the people who show it.

Same behaviour. Three centuries. Three completely different things done to the person.

Do not teach the history as a list of dates — the Field Guide has the dates. Teach it as the claim above, and make them supply examples.

When unpacking the psychological box, name-drop its two branches in one breath each: psychoanalysis (Freud's id/ego/superego, and defence mechanisms — repression, projection — vocabulary that recurs all term) and the humanistic answer to it (Rogers, unconditional positive regard). Both have Guide pages; the room just needs to have heard the names.

The reformer

Benjamin Rush, father of American psychiatry

Argued for humane treatment and respect for patients when almost nobody did.

  • Practised bloodletting and purging
  • Invented a "tranquilizing chair"
  • Consulted astrology

Even a reformer could not escape the beliefs of his time.

So: which of our current practices is the tranquilizing chair?

Let the last line hang. Do not answer it — the whole course is the answer, and you want them holding the question in Lecture 8 when they meet harm-reduction debates, and in Lecture 10 at NCRMD.

If someone says "ECT", that is a good answer and Lecture 5 handles it seriously rather than as a punchline.

The reform that worked, then failed

Moral treatment

Pinel removed the chains. Tuke built the York Retreat. Patients improved; some were released.

And within a century it had collapsed. Why?

  • It was scale-dependent. It worked at 200 patients or fewer. Post-war immigration pushed facilities past 1,000.
  • It was rationed by prejudice. Immigrants were denied the treatment given to native-born citizens even where resources existed.
  • It did not work for everyone — and nothing else had been invented yet.

Works · scales badly · underfunded · applied unequally. Hold that pattern. Deinstitutionalization repeats it exactly, a century later.

This is the most transferable thing in the history section — a failure shape rather than a fact. Ask them for a modern example before revealing the last line; someone usually says wait times or shelter beds.

The proof that made biology credible

General paresis, in four steps

  1. 1825 — Bayle describes the behavioural and cognitive symptoms of advanced syphilis
  2. 1897 — Krafft-Ebing inoculates paretic patients with syphilitic matter. Nothing happens.
  3. 1906 — Wassermann develops a blood test
  4. 1917 — Wagner-Jauregg induces malarial fever and paresis remits

Step 2 is the clever one. Why does nothing happening count as evidence?

Because no reaction implies prior exposure — they had already had syphilis. A negative result, read correctly, established the cause.

Ask the room why step 2 is evidence before revealing. It is the first piece of real inferential reasoning in the course, and it rewards sitting still for thirty seconds.

Do not moralise about the ethics of inoculating patients — but do not skip it either. One sentence: no review board today would permit it, and the finding was still true.

Part three

Three models, no winner

The move this course makes

Stop asking which model is right

Biological, psychological, sociocultural. Each explains something. None explains everything.

A single cause rarely acts alone. As causes arise they become part of the person and start conditioning the effect of everything after them — so by the time someone presents for help, the cause is the accumulated set, not any one member of it.

The question is never "which model." It is which causal strands are best evidenced for this condition — and the answer changes disorder by disorder.

Say explicitly that the Field Guide is built this way: each disorder page states separately which level of explanation is actually best evidenced for it. That is why the pages disagree with each other, and it is not sloppiness.

The framework that does the work

Diathesis–stress

The half usually left out is the green box. This model is not only about accumulating risk.

The protective-factor half is what makes the framework usable rather than fatalistic. Do not skip it even when running late.

One spoken sentence on the biology of the stress arrow: chronic stress runs through the HPA axis and its cortisol cascade — the Guide has a dedicated hpa-axis page, and Lectures 4, 5 and 11 all lean on it.

Why the framework is needed

The question a purely genetic account cannot answer

Identical twins share their genome. One develops schizophrenia; the other does not.

48%

twin concordance, schizophrenia

70–85%

heritability, tic disorders

Every one of these numbers leaves a gap that only an interaction model closes.

And it runs the other way too: fewer than 10% of severely neglected children develop reactive attachment disorder; only about 8% of trauma-exposed Canadians develop PTSD.

"Why do most exposed people not develop it?" is a proper scientific question — and only this framework lets you ask it.

The reversal in the second half is the part students remember. Most of the course's disorder pages have a version of this gap; point forward to PTSD in Lecture 4.

The interaction runs deeper

Genes pick environments

Diathesis–stress treats the vulnerability and the stress as independent ingredients. They are not: genotype shapes which stressors a person meets.

  • A child with an irritable temperament draws harsher parenting — the "environmental" cause is partly downstream of the genes
  • A sensation-seeking genotype walks into more accidents, more substances, more chaotic relationships

Gene–environment correlation: the vulnerability and the trigger arrive holding hands.

This is the gene–environment slide the break-2 note lets you drop when running late — diathesis-stress is the one that must land. If kept, one epigenetics sentence fits here: environments also reach back and regulate gene expression, so the arrow runs both ways.

A caution you will need all term

"Chemical imbalance" is a belief, not a finding

The textbook reports it as a widely held belief within mental health rather than an established mechanism — and then immediately supplements it with circuit- and growth-based evidence.

The monoamine hypothesis came from drugs. In the 1950s, tuberculosis drugs that raised monoamines lifted mood; blood-pressure drugs that lowered norepinephrine induced depression.

That a drug changes a symptom does not establish what caused it. Headaches are not an aspirin deficiency.

You still need the cast of characters, because the drugs act on them: serotonin (mood, impulse), dopamine (reward and motor — Lecture 11's star), norepinephrine (arousal, alarm), GABA and glutamate (the brakes and the gas of the whole system).

This is a reverse-inference error, and it recurs in Lecture 5 with antidepressants and Lecture 11 with dopamine. Naming it now means you can just say "aspirin deficiency" later and the room will get it.

The cast line is deliberately shallow — four names, one job each, said out loud. The Guide's pharmacology pages carry the depth; the room just needs the nouns before Lecture 5.

The psychological tradition's engine

Two kinds of learning, one minute each

Classical — Pavlov. A neutral cue paired with an event comes to trigger the response on its own: conditioned stimulus, conditioned response. Break the pairing and the response fades — extinction.

Operant — Skinner. Behaviour is shaped by its consequences: reinforced behaviour repeats. And crucially — whatever relieves discomfort is reinforced too.

Half the disorders ahead run on these two gears: phobias are conditioned alarms; compulsions and avoidance are maintained by the relief they produce. Exposure therapy is extinction, engineered.

Sixty seconds per panel — the payoff line is the forward pointer, not the mechanism detail. Little Albert and systematic desensitization are in the Guide (little-albert-study, systematic-desensitization); name-check, don't retell.
