Part one

The state, and the spectrum carved from it

First distinction

Psychosis is not a diagnosis

It is a state — loss of contact with reality — expressed through five symptom domains: delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour, negative symptoms.

The spectrum is carved mainly by duration of that state:

brief psychotic disorder <1 month · schizophreniform 1–6 months · schizophrenia ≥6 months · plus delusional disorder (delusions alone, functioning preserved) and schizoaffective (psychosis + sustained mood episode)

Same organising move as the mood chapter: symptoms give you the state; the calendar gives you the diagnosis.

The duration carve is the EMQ backbone for the final. Schizophreniform's two-thirds conversion figure makes "the calendar diagnoses" vivid.

The stakes

Four numbers before any theory

  • 0.3–0.7% of the general population — in a class this size, statistically, someone
  • 5–6% die by suicide; around 20% attempt at least once
  • Life expectancy is markedly reduced — medication effects and their metabolic costs are part of why
  • 13.5% meet recovery criteria — the honest denominator under every treatment claim that follows

All four figures are the Guide's (APA 2022 via the Module 12 page). Deliver flat and unhurried — this is the stakes slide, not the fear slide. The 13.5% recovery figure is the one to hold in reserve for the treatment slide.

The distinction with prognostic teeth

Positive and negative symptoms

Positive — exaggerations of normal processes: delusions, hallucinations, disorganisation

respond best to antipsychotics

Negative — reductions: of speech (alogia), expression (flat affect), initiation (avolition), pleasure (anhedonia), social contact (asociality)

predict prognosis — and respond poorly to medication

The symptoms that frighten the public are the treatable ones. The quiet ones carry the outcome.

Delusion content is culturally shaped (persecutory commonest) — echo of L1's culture material. Keep hallucination phenomenology respectful; some of the room has heard voices.

The five negative-symptom terms are the Guide's vocabulary — say each once out loud; they are fair game on the final.

The calendar, again

Onset has a shape

  • Prodromal: subtle oddness and early negative symptoms — often late teens, usually read as something else
  • Active: the positive symptoms arrive — late teens to mid-30s, slightly earlier in men. This is the phase the duration spectrum carves.
  • Residual: positive symptoms recede; negative and cognitive symptoms stay — and they, not the voices, drive most of the lifelong impairment

Earlier onset predicts worse prognosis — which is why prevention research lives in the prodrome.

Three phases, one line each. The prodrome point sets up early-intervention as a live research frontier; the residual point re-lands "the quiet ones carry the outcome" from the previous slide.

Etiology — everything at once

The disorder the integrative model was built for

  • Genetics: 48% identical-twin concordance against 17% fraternal — massive, and famously not 100%
  • Brain structure: enlarged ventricles, reduced medial-temporal and orbitofrontal volume — consistent findings, none of them diagnostic
  • Stress cascade: vulnerability × precipitating stress, with the HPA axis as proposed mediator — strong evidence for stress in relapse, weaker for onset
  • The social environment: family hostility, criticism and overinvolvement — expressed emotion — roughly doubles relapse risk

Lecture 1 promised no single model would win. Here is the disorder that proves it.

The 48% figure is the diathesis-stress anchor from week 1 — close the loop explicitly. Expressed emotion sets up family intervention two slides on.

Mechanism, by intervention

The dopamine argument

  • Drugs that block dopamine receptors relieve positive symptoms — discovered by accident with chlorpromazine in the 1950s
  • Drugs that boost dopamine — L-dopa, amphetamines — provoke or worsen psychosis
  • Two arrows through the same system, opposite directions: mechanism inferred by intervention — the same move your RCT makes tonight

And the limit: the dopamine story is a story about positive symptoms. Negative symptoms barely respond to any dopamine drug — the mechanism map runs out exactly where the prognosis lives.

This is the course's cleanest mechanism-by-intervention argument — name the inference pattern explicitly, then cash it in during the RCT debrief. Atypicals act on serotonin as well as dopamine, which is why they do somewhat better on negative symptoms (Guide: antipsychotic-medications page).

Treatment, honestly

What the numbers actually say

  • Atypical antipsychotics are first-line — and 50–75% of patients discontinue
  • Tardive dyskinesia on conventionals: ~10% — the side-effect profile is a treatment outcome, not a footnote
  • Negative symptoms: poorly served by every drug class we have

Which is why the psychosocial arm matters: family interventions targeting expressed emotion reduce rehospitalisation; CBT for psychosis targets the distress of symptoms rather than their existence.

The discontinuation rate is the single most clinically honest number in the lecture — hold it next to L8's remission rates as the two "numbers nobody quotes" of the term.

CBTp targeting distress-not-symptoms is a genuinely different treatment philosophy; one minute.

The case that integrates

Rachel

The Guide's integrative case: first break at 21 during exams, a family with high expressed emotion, partial response to medication, negative symptoms persisting.

  • Which criteria from Lecture 1 does she meet — and which fail her?
  • Which diagnosis does the calendar give her — and what would it have been one month after onset?

Part two · 45 minutes

The reveal

What you were in

The two arms

Arm A — sense foraging. Interactive attention practices: exploring sensory experience through the games some of you met — attending outward, contingently, playfully.

Arm B — stress reappraisal. Video lessons and written exercises: reinterpreting stress responses as resources — threat to challenge, mindset to mechanism.

Bottom-up attention versus top-down belief. Two genuinely different theories of how five minutes a day could change a stressful month.

Poll first: "which arm do you think you were in?" — the guess-rate is itself a blinding check, and the room loves it. THEN reveal, then show the CEQ credibility comparison to demonstrate the arms were matched.

What we predicted, and what happened

The pre-registration, side by side with the data

  • H1: both arms improve on perceived stress across the month
  • H2: each arm moves its own mechanism — mindset scores in the reappraisal arm, sensory attention in the foraging arm

[Results land here on the night — filled from the analysis, not before.] Whatever they show: the predictions were time-stamped in October, and you're seeing them next to the outcomes. Matches count. Misses count more.

Insert the real figures the weekend before: PSS trajectories by arm, the mechanism panel, completion stats. If a hypothesis failed, teach the failure with more enthusiasm than the successes — it is the term's best credibility move.

Design critique — you do it

Now break your own study

You know the tools. Use them on us.

  • No passive control — could any daily five-minute ritual explain both arms improving?
  • Self-report outcomes in a self-aware sample — expectancy, demand, and what the CEQ can and cannot rule out
  • Attrition: who dropped out, and is what remains still a random sample of you?
  • December stress ≠ October stress — what does the calendar confound?

Let the room generate these before revealing; they will get most of them, which is the point of the term. The attrition question connects to ITT from the L2 material for anyone heading to the final.
