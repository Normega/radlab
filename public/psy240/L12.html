Part one

The state, and the spectrum carved from it

First distinction

Psychosis is not a diagnosis

It is a state — loss of contact with reality — expressed through five symptom domains: delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour, negative symptoms.

The spectrum is carved mainly by duration of that state:

brief psychotic disorder 1 day – 1 month · schizophreniform 1–6 months · schizophrenia ≥6 months · plus delusional disorder (delusions alone for ≥1 month, functioning preserved) and schizoaffective (psychosis + sustained mood episode)

Same organising move as the mood chapter: symptoms give you the state; the calendar gives you the diagnosis.

The duration carve is the EMQ backbone for the final. Schizophreniform's two-thirds conversion figure makes "the calendar diagnoses" vivid.

The stakes

Four numbers before any theory

  • 0.3–0.7% of the general population — in a class this size, statistically, someone
  • 5–6% die by suicide; around 20% attempt at least once
  • Life expectancy is markedly reduced — medication effects and their metabolic costs are part of why
  • 13.5% meet recovery criteria — the honest denominator under every treatment claim that follows

All four figures are the Guide's (APA 2022 via the Module 12 page). If asked about comorbidity: substance use disorder is high (the Module 12 summary names cannabis in particular), and panic disorder and OCD are elevated; schizotypal or paranoid personality disorder sometimes precedes onset (schizophrenia page, Comorbidity). Deliver flat and unhurried — this is the stakes slide, not the fear slide. The 13.5% recovery figure is the one to hold in reserve for the treatment slide.

The distinction with prognostic teeth

Positive and negative symptoms

Positive — exaggerations of normal processes: delusions, hallucinations, disorganisation

respond best to antipsychotics

Negative — reductions: of speech (alogia), expression (flat affect), initiation (avolition), pleasure (anhedonia), social contact (asociality)

predict prognosis — and respond poorly to medication

The symptoms that frighten the public are the treatable ones. The quiet ones carry the outcome.

Delusion content is culturally shaped (persecutory commonest) — echo of L1's culture material. Keep hallucination phenomenology respectful; some of the room has heard voices.

The five negative-symptom terms are the Guide's vocabulary — say each once out loud; they are fair game on the final.

Positive symptoms, up close · 1

A delusion is fixed, not just false

A belief held despite contradicting evidence, sustained by a lack of insight. The contents vary; the fixity is what they share.

  • Persecution — being harmed, harassed, plotted against. The most common.
  • Grandeur — exceptional ability, wealth, fame, divine status
  • Reference — gestures, comments, cues aimed specifically at me
  • Control — others steer my thoughts, feelings or actions
  • Thought broadcasting — my thoughts are audible to everyone
  • Thought withdrawal — my thoughts are being removed

The content is borrowed from the life around it. A person raised in a highly religious family is more likely to present with religious grandiose delusions. The fixity is the symptom; the content is biography and culture.

Ask before revealing anything past the definition: "Which delusion do you think is commonest?" Expect grandeur. Persecution is the commonest (Arango & Carpenter, 2010) — delusions and schizophrenia pages.

The culture line is the echo of L1: the same belief form, filled from whatever the person's world supplies. The Guide also notes persecutory content is the subtype most associated with aggression or hostility; if it comes up, say it as the Guide does and give no rate — the Guide has none.

Positive symptoms, up close · 2

Voices, and speech that loses the thread

Hallucinations — perception without a stimulus, in any sense

about 50% of patients report auditory · 15% visual · 5% tactile

Typically clear voices addressing the person or talking to each other — comforting, commanding or malicious.

Most people recognise the voices as unreal and try to carry on normally around them.

Disorganised speech

  • circumstantial — detail upon detail, eventually answers
  • tangential — never arrives at the point
  • derailment — illogical links between thoughts
  • word salad — incoherence

Disorganised behaviour runs from childlike silliness to unpredictable agitation. Catatonia is its commonest form: negativism, mutism or stupor, rigidity, posturing — or the hyperactive variant, catatonic excitement.

Demo before the speech list: read two made-up answers to "Where did you grow up?" and ask the room to name the difference. (1) "My parents met in Hamilton, the house had a blue door, the school was two blocks down past the bakery — anyway, Mississauga." (2) "Mississauga has a lot of roads. The Romans built roads. The Romans had a god for everything." The first arrives (circumstantial); the second never does (tangential). Both examples are invented for the demo.

Figures: DeLeon, Cuesta & Peralta (1993) via the schizophrenia page — the Module 12 summary flags the source as dated. The "most recognise them as unreal" line is the Guide's and it matters for the room; some students have heard voices.

The calendar, again

Onset has a shape

  • Prodromal: subtle oddness and early negative symptoms — early signs often resemble depression, and are read as something else
  • Active: the positive symptoms arrive — late teens to mid-30s, slightly earlier in men. This is the phase the duration spectrum carves.
  • Residual: positive symptoms recede; negative and cognitive symptoms stay — and they, not the voices, drive much of the lifelong impairment

Earlier onset predicts worse prognosis — which is why prevention research lives in the prodrome.

Men and women are at equal risk; men typically present with more negative symptoms, women with more affect-laden ones.

Three phases, one line each. The Guide gives no age for the prodrome itself — onset of the disorder is late teens to mid-30s, gradual, early symptoms resembling depression (schizophrenia page, Epidemiology). The sex difference is from the same section and the positive-and-negative-symptoms page. The prodrome point sets up early-intervention as a live research frontier; the residual point re-lands "the quiet ones carry the outcome" from the previous slide.

The calendar, precisely

What the six months are made of

Four months of withdrawal, then six weeks of voices and conviction: the active phase is met, the six months are not — schizophreniform, for now. Two more weeks, and the same unchanged person has schizophrenia.

About two-thirds of schizophreniform diagnoses convert. The Guide's word for the category is a waiting room.

Show the three phases, then ask: "Four months of withdrawal, then six weeks of voices — which diagnosis?" Let them argue before the bracket appears. The point to land is that the prodrome counts toward the six months, and that the diagnosis changes on a date while the person does not.

Criteria paraphrased from the schizophrenia page (Diagnosis): two of five domains over one month, one of them delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech; significant impairment; some sign for at least six months in total; prodromal and residual symptoms are subthreshold except negative symptoms. Two-thirds conversion: schizophreniform-disorder page; "waiting room": psychosis-and-the-schizophrenia-spectrum, Contested.

The shortest carve

A diagnosis made in the past tense

Brief psychotic disorder: one day to one month of psychosis, then full return to premorbid functioning. At onset it is indistinguishable from a first episode of schizophrenia — only the ending decides.

A remission rate is a function of how long you looked. That 23-month study saw three of its four brief-psychotic-disorder patients fully remit; on this curve, two years is where transition is still climbing.

Across studies the diagnosis is revised in 23% to 87% of cases. A label that changes that often marks a stage of uncertainty, not a condition.

Show the first three bars, then ask: "10, 19, 30 — guess the five-year figure." Reveal the dashed line (Correll et al., 2008, mean follow-up 22.8 months), then the 54% bar.

All from the brief-psychotic-disorder page. The cohort is Fusar-Poli et al. (2017): 80 ultra-high-risk help-seekers in London with Brief Limited Intermittent Psychotic Symptoms — episodes resolving within a week — of whom 68% also met ICD-10 acute and transient psychotic disorder. Same clinical territory, not the identical DSM category; say so if asked. Correll's brief-psychotic-disorder subgroup was four people — say the sample size before the finding. Do not quote Correll's remission percentages: the page's "60% vs 4.8%" does not match its own three-of-four (reported to the Guide).

The odd one out

A delusion that doesn't disorganise a life

Delusional disorder: one or more delusions for at least a month — and nothing else. No hallucinations, no disorganisation, no negative symptoms; daily functioning largely intact. Prevalence 0.2–0.3%.

Subtypes: erotomanic · grandiose · jealous · persecutory · somatic · mixed · unspecified — plus a bizarre specifier for content that could not come from ordinary life

Delusional disorderSchizophrenia
Onsetmiddle age and beyondlate teens to mid-30s
Cognition outside the delusionusually unimpairedprominently impaired
Effective haloperidol dose4.7 mg/day12.7 mg/day

The delusions here are, by construction, the believable ones — infidelity, persecution, illness, being loved. The diagnosis rests on a judgement of plausibility, and Lecture 1's deviance criterion is back in the room.

Ask before the box: "A partner is convinced, without evidence, that they are being cheated on. Symptom or opinion — and who decides?" That is the jealous subtype, and the question has no clean answer.

All from the delusional-disorder page (Gonzalez-Rodriguez & Seeman, 2022, for the table). The authors' reading of late onset: the illness begins in a brain and a life that developed normally, which is why cognition is preserved. Do not quote the page's 0.48–1% schizophrenia prevalence — it disagrees with the 0.3–0.7% used everywhere else in the Guide (reported).

Where two chapters meet

Psychosis plus a mood episode: three answers

The deciding question is not whether mood symptoms occur — it is which outlasts which. The timeline, again, does the diagnosing.

Inside schizoaffective disorder a depressive episode needs pervasive low mood, not just lost interest — anhedonia is already a schizophrenia symptom. Reliability is poor, and whether it is a distinct disorder has been argued for decades.

Ask first: "Depression, and voices. Which diagnosis?" The honest answer is "not enough information" — and the fork shows the information you would need. Reveal the two outer answers, then the middle one.

From the schizoaffective-disorder page (the two-week rule and the pervasive-mood point), the schizophrenia page ("psychotic features persist beyond the remission of depressive symptoms") and psychosis-and-the-schizophrenia-spectrum, Contested (reliability poor; distinct entity, schizophrenia subtype or severe mood disorder).

Etiology — everything at once

The disorder the integrative model was built for

  • Genetics: 48% identical-twin concordance against 17% fraternal — massive, and famously not 100%
  • Brain structure: enlarged ventricles, reduced medial-temporal and orbitofrontal volume — consistent findings, none of them diagnostic
  • Stress cascade: vulnerability × precipitating stress, with the HPA axis as proposed mediator — strong evidence for stress in relapse, weaker for onset
  • The social environment: family hostility, criticism and overinvolvement — expressed emotion — roughly doubles relapse risk

Lecture 1 promised no single model would win. Here is the disorder that proves it.

The 48% figure is the diathesis-stress anchor from week 1 — close the loop explicitly. The family studies add a gradient: the more a relative's brain resembles the patient's, the higher that relative's own risk (Scognamiglio & Houenou, 2014; schizophrenia page) — polygenic liability, not a switch. Expressed emotion sets up family intervention two slides on.

Etiology, the psychological half

From vulnerability to episode

Beck and Rector's (2005) diathesis-stress chain, written as a sequence:

  1. Premorbid neurocognitive impairment
  2. → vulnerability to aversive life events — homelessness, family conflict
  3. → dysfunctional attitudes → cognitive distortions
  4. → HPA hyperactivation: persistently raised glucocorticoids acting on already abnormal brain structures, altering dopamine activity
  5. → psychosis

The evidence for stress is strong for relapse; for onset it is still being worked out.

Expressed emotion runs both ways. Even low-expressed-emotion families report more conflict and communication difficulty from the burden of the illness. It is a causal variable in relapse — not a verdict on parents.

Ask once the chain is up: "Pick the link you would break." Then preview: CBT for psychosis works on the attitudes and distortions; family intervention on the aversive environment; medication on the dopamine end. Nothing on the list reaches link 1.

From the schizophrenia page (Etiology: cognitive, stress cascade, sociocultural), the hpa-axis page and the expressed-emotion page, whose "reverse direction" section is the source for the box (Friedrich et al., 2015; Kurtz, 2015). The old "schizophrenogenic mother" idea from the 2025 deck is not in the Guide — if a student raises it, the box is the answer, but do not teach the history as examinable.

Mechanism, by intervention

The dopamine argument

  • Drugs that block dopamine receptors relieve positive symptoms — first seen with chlorpromazine (Thorazine), an antihistamine derivative, in the 1950s
  • Drugs that boost dopamine — L-dopa, amphetamines — provoke or worsen psychosis
  • Two arrows through the same system, opposite directions: mechanism inferred by intervention — the same move your RCT makes tonight

And the limit: the dopamine story is a story about positive symptoms. Negative symptoms barely respond to any dopamine drug — the mechanism map runs out exactly where the prognosis lives.

This is the course's cleanest mechanism-by-intervention argument — name the inference pattern explicitly, then cash it in during the RCT debrief. Atypicals act on serotonin as well as dopamine, which is why they do somewhat better on negative symptoms (Guide: antipsychotic-medications page).

Mechanism, turned into medicine

Two generations of antipsychotic — and why there are two

Conventional · first generationAtypical · second generation

The second generation exists because the first caused a specific, sometimes irreversible harm — tardive dyskinesia, involuntary movements of the tongue, mouth and face. Clozapine, the first atypical, arrived in the 1970s.

Atypicals are first-line. Conventionals above the clinically effective dose add side effects without adding benefit.

Ask before the last row: "The first generation already quiets voices. Why build a second?" The answer is the hidden row.

From antipsychotic-medications and the schizophrenia page (Treatment). Clozapine's specific role in treatment resistance and its monitoring requirement sit in the page's "Needs research" list — don't teach them as examinable. Note for Guide editors: the page lists "Thorazine and chlorpromazine" as two agents; they are one drug (brand and generic), as the schizophrenia page says.

Treatment, honestly

What the numbers actually say

  • Atypical antipsychotics are first-line — and 50–75% of patients discontinue
  • Side effects are why they stop — the side-effect profile is a treatment outcome, not a footnote
  • Negative symptoms: poorly served by every drug class we have

Which is why the psychosocial arm matters: family interventions targeting expressed emotion reduce rehospitalisation; CBT for psychosis targets the distress of symptoms rather than their existence.

The discontinuation rate is the single most clinically honest number in the lecture — hold it next to L8's remission rates as the two "numbers nobody quotes" of the term.

CBTp targeting distress-not-symptoms is a genuinely different treatment philosophy; one minute.

The category has a history

Same patients, two manuals

Early 1970s: the US diagnosed with the broad, clinically based DSM-II. Europe used ICD-9.

Schizophrenia was diagnosed far more often in the US than in Europe. A prevalence figure is partly a property of the manual.

  • Kraepelin — poor outcome, chronicity, avolition
  • DSM-I and II — broader, drawing on Bleuler: interpersonal pathology and negative symptoms
  • DSM-III to IV — Schneider's first-rank symptoms, plus chronicity and lost functioning. DSM-5 keeps IV's core definition.

The manual is not the only variable. Schizophrenia diagnosis shows documented racial and ethnic bias (Neighbors et al., 2003). Different diagnosis rates by race do not mean different severity or prevalence — clinician interpretation drives much of the gap.

Ask first: "Fly a patient from New York to London in 1972. Does the illness change?" No. The diagnosis might. That is L2's reliability-and-validity argument with a real population behind it.

From the schizophrenia page (Contested: the diagnostic history and the US–Europe comparison; Davies, 2025: "the exact definition of schizophrenia still remains elusive") and multicultural-psychology (Neighbors et al., 2003; Schwartz & Blankenship, 2014). The derivation of Bleuler's name for the disorder and Kraepelin's "dementia praecox" are not in the Guide — mention freely, but they are not examinable as stated.

The chapter's open question

One spectrum, or several disorders?

For one spectrum

  • One symptom picture, cut by the clock
  • Schizotypal personality disorder shares schizophrenia's high dopamine activity and enlarged ventricles — and sometimes precedes onset
  • Dopamine dysregulation appears in delusional disorder too

For several

  • The causal story is written about schizophrenia, then assumed for the rest
  • The duration cut-offs are conventions, not natural boundaries
  • Delusional disorder differs in onset, cognition and drug response
  • Schizoaffective disorder's reliability is poor

Same symptoms, different clocks, one borrowed etiology: the spectrum is asserted more than it is shown.

Quick vote before the right column: "one thing or several?" Then reveal and ask whether anyone changed their vote. Callback to L10: schizotypal was the one personality disorder with a specific biological finding.

From psychosis-and-the-schizophrenia-spectrum (Etiology and Contested), schizotypal-personality-disorder (Etiology: Lener et al., 2015; the schizophrenia page's comorbidity section for "sometimes precedes onset") and delusional-disorder (Contested). The Guide's single most consequential unknown here: what separates the third who remit within six months from the two-thirds who convert.

Part two · 45 minutes

The reveal

What you were in

The two arms

Arm A — sense foraging. Interactive attention practices: exploring sensory experience through the games some of you met — attending outward, contingently, playfully.

Arm B — stress reappraisal. Video lessons and written exercises: reinterpreting stress responses as resources — threat to challenge, mindset to mechanism.

Bottom-up attention versus top-down belief. Two genuinely different theories of how five minutes a day could change a stressful month.

Poll first: "which arm do you think you were in?" — the guess-rate is itself a blinding check, and the room loves it. THEN reveal, then show the CEQ credibility comparison to demonstrate the arms were matched.

What we predicted, and what happened

The pre-registration, side by side with the data

  • H1: both arms improve on perceived stress across the month
  • H2: each arm moves its own mechanism — mindset scores in the reappraisal arm, sensory attention in the foraging arm

[Results land here on the night — filled from the analysis, not before.] Whatever they show: the predictions were time-stamped in October, and you're seeing them next to the outcomes. Matches count. Misses count more.

Insert the real figures the weekend before: PSS trajectories by arm, the mechanism panel, completion stats. If a hypothesis failed, teach the failure with more enthusiasm than the successes — it is the term's best credibility move.

Design critique — you do it

Now break your own study

You know the tools. Use them on us.

  • No passive control — could any daily five-minute ritual explain both arms improving?
  • Self-report outcomes in a self-aware sample — expectancy, demand, and what the CEQ can and cannot rule out
  • Attrition: who dropped out, and is what remains still a random sample of you?
  • December stress ≠ October stress — what does the calendar confound?

Let the room generate these before revealing; they will get most of them, which is the point of the term. The attrition question connects to ITT from the L2 material for anyone heading to the final.
