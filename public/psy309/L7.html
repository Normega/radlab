Part one

Three debts, and who pays them

What "A causes B" owes you

  • Covariance — A and B travel together. Any correlation can pay this.
  • Temporal precedence — A comes before B. Longitudinal designs can partly pay this.
  • Internal validity — nothing else explains the link. Correlational research can chip away, never retire it — you cannot control for what you didn't measure.

The experiment pays all three, with two structural moves.

Move one

Manipulation

You set the causal variable instead of measuring it.

Assign this participant to caffeine, that one to decaf.

This buys temporal precedence outright. You created A at a known moment, then measured B after it.

No cross-lagged inference. No "which came first." You were there — you did it.

Move two · the crown jewel

Random assignment

Any pre-existing difference between your groups — age, personality, sleep, wealth, genetics, things nobody has named — is a potential selection effect.

A coin flip severs every one of those links at once.

Whatever a person brings with them, chance distributes it evenly across conditions — on average. Any single randomization can be unlucky; the procedure has no systematic direction.

This is why one experiment can outrank a hundred regressions. Regression evicts the third variables someone thought to measure. Random assignment evicts them all — including the unimagined ones.

Say this slowly. Five minutes of talking, not a bullet. Explicitly connect back to the L4 R exercise: you removed a confound because you knew to measure C. This handles the ones you don't know about.

Two randoms, never blur them

GovernsBuys
Random samplingWho gets inExternal validity
Random assignmentWho gets which conditionInternal validity

Independent: a study can have both, either, or neither. A convenience sample, randomly assigned, supports a causal claim about somebody while leaving "about whom?" open.

The comparison group is not optional

An experiment without one is personal experience wearing a lab coat.

"Everyone improved after the intervention" means nothing without knowing what happened to people who didn't get it.

  • No-treatment control
  • Placebo control — everything except the active ingredient
  • Another active condition — "compared to the current best method," often the more interesting question

Choosing the comparison is choosing the claim. Caffeine-vs-decaf and caffeine-vs-nothing answer different questions.

Three job titles

IV — what you manipulate. Its levels are the conditions. Caffeine/decaf is one IV with two conditions, not two IVs.

DV — what you measure. Everything from the measurement weeks applies: reliability, validity, scale.

Control variables — what you hold constant. Same room, same time, same script.

A control variable (held constant) is not a control group (a level of the IV). The group experiences a condition; control variables experience nothing at all.

Manipulation check: did your "stress condition" actually make anyone stressed? Its absence is a standing weakness to flag.

Exercise · 30 minutes

Break This Study

A protocol with five things wrong with it. Find them.

For each problem:

  1. Name it precisely — the technical term
  2. Which validity it threatens
  3. How you'd fix it

Scoring: 1 point per fault found. 2 points if named precisely.

"Something's off about the groups" = 1.
"Selection effect from self-selected sessions" = 2.

Check-in position 2, 20-minute countdown. The protocol is on Quercus. Announce the scoring up front — it is the cheapest way to move a class from vague critique to the vocabulary the test rewards.

Does background music improve studying?

60 students from a third-year psych course signed up via a shared sheet listing two sessions and chose whichever suited their timetable. The Tuesday 9am session became the music group (n = 31); Thursday 4pm became silence (n = 29).

The music group studied in the department lounge — sofas, natural light — with instrumental music. The silence group used a standard classroom. Same chapter, 30 minutes.

After studying, a 10-question quiz written by the researcher. Mean 9.1 / 10; 22 participants scored a perfect 10.

The researcher ran both sessions, greeted participants, explained the hypothesis that "music helps you focus," and marked the quizzes.

Result: music 9.4 vs silence 8.8, p = .04. Conclusion: install speakers in study spaces university-wide.

Leave this on screen for the full 20 minutes. Circulate. Don't confirm finds — say "write it down precisely."

The five

  1. Selection effect — participants chose their session. 9am people differ from 4pm people.
  2. Design confound — the music group also got the comfortable lounge.
  3. Ceiling effect — mean 9.1/10, 22 perfect scores. No room to differ.
  4. Demand characteristics + no masking — he told them the hypothesis, then marked the quizzes himself.
  5. A second design confound — time of day is perfectly confounded with condition.

Bonus credit: over-generalization to the whole university · p = .04 with no effect size or CI · a home-made, unvalidated quiz.

Now: if you fix everything, what does the study cost?

That last question is the real content. Same room, same time, random assignment, masked marking, harder test — a much more expensive study answering a narrower question. That trade IS experimental design.

Part three

Everything else that can go wrong

Within or between?

Between-groups. Different people, different conditions. Safe from contamination; mandatory when one condition spoils the other.

The bill: people differ enormously, and all of it lands in your noise term.

Within-groups. Everyone does everything; each person is their own control.

Maria's caffeinated RT is compared to Maria's decaf RT. Her chronic speed subtracts itself out — far less noise, far fewer participants.

The bill: order effects. Practice, fatigue, carryover. Same order for everyone = order is confounded with condition.

Counterbalance: half get A→B, half B→A. Order effects still exist but no longer line up with condition — the same move randomization performs on selection effects.

Within if you can, between if you must.

The impostor

Measure. Treat. Measure again. Celebrate the improvement.

The design of every testimonial and too many program evaluations.

It looks within-subjects but has no comparison condition — so everything else that happened between the two measurements is fully confounded with the treatment.

The repair is the answer to most problems in this course: a control group also improves — maturation, history and regression happen to them too. The difference between the two rises is the treatment.

The rogues' gallery

  • Maturation — they were going to change anyway
  • History — something else happened to everyone
  • Regression to the mean — select on extremity and the average drifts to the middle with no intervention at all
  • Attrition — the leavers take their scores with them
  • Testing & instrumentation — the measure changed, not the person

The master key: would this threat affect a control group equally? If yes, the comparison design handles it. If no — you've found the interesting weakness.

Over-learn regression to the mean. "We treated the most severe cases and they improved" is the weakest sentence in program evaluation, and it follows you into every domain that selects on extremes.

Ninety-second drill

Name the threats

“A campus program recruits the 20 most-stressed students from a screening. After eight weeks of workshops, their average stress has dropped sharply. The program is declared a success.”

  • Regression to the mean — selected because extreme; the luck that selected them doesn’t repeat.
  • Maturation / history — eight weeks passed; the term’s rhythm changed for everyone.
  • No comparison group — the one-group pretest/posttest, in the wild.
  • The fix: randomize the 20 into workshop-now vs waitlist — both regress, both mature; the difference is the program.

This is the Test 2 short-answer shape exactly. Reveal after the room proposes; precision is the currency — "regression to the mean" scores, "they'd get better anyway" half-scores.

Minds that know things

Placebo effects — expecting improvement produces some. Real machinery, not faking.

Demand characteristics — participants read what the study wants and oblige.

Experimenter expectancy — Clever Hans, at full scale.

Blinding. Single: participants don't know. Double: nor do the people running sessions and scoring outcomes.

Same move as random assignment — take the knowledge away from anyone whose behaviour could carry it. In any Method section, "double-blind, placebo-controlled" is two structural safeguards being claimed.

When nothing happens

Three suspects before you conclude "no effect" — check the phone before deciding nobody's calling.

  1. The manipulation was too weak. $0 vs $1 when it takes $20 to move anyone.
  2. The DV couldn't register it. Ceiling, floor, or a scale too coarse.
  3. Noise buried it. Too few participants, unreliable measures, a between design where individual differences swamp the signal.

Rule those out and a null becomes evidence. "d = 0.02, 95% CI [−.08, .12]" says: if there's an effect, it's trivial. That's a real scientific claim.

Never "we proved there is no effect." You saw in October what low power does to null results.
