Part one

Three debts, and who pays them

What "A causes B" owes you

  • Covariance: A and B travel together. Any correlation can pay this.
  • Temporal precedence: A comes before B. Longitudinal designs can partly pay this.
  • Internal validity: nothing else explains the link. Correlational research can chip away at this but never retire it, because you cannot control for what you didn't measure.

The experiment pays all three, with two structural moves.

Move one

Manipulation

You set the causal variable instead of measuring it.

This participant writes down three things they’re grateful for. That one writes down three things that happened today.

This buys temporal precedence outright. You created the gratitude at a known moment, then measured optimism after it.

There's no cross-lagged inference and no "which came first." You were there, and you did it.

This is the course's running example from the Practical 3–5 worked examples (gratitude vs neutral journaling, optimism DV, perceived stress covariate). Keeping one study all lecture lets them map every idea onto their own Study 2.

Move two · the crown jewel

Random assignment

Any pre-existing difference between your groups is a potential selection effect: age, personality, sleep, wealth, genetics, things nobody has named.

A coin flip severs every one of those links at once.

Whatever a person brings with them, chance spreads it evenly across conditions on average. Any single randomization can be unlucky, but the procedure has no systematic direction.

Say this slowly: five minutes of talking, not a bullet. The next slide puts it side by side with the L4 R exercise, which is the argument the whole course has been building toward.

Two ways to get rid of C

Statistical control (L4)Random assignment (today)
HowMeasure C, put it in the modelFlip a coin for every participant
RemovesThe Cs someone thought to measure, and measured wellEvery C: measured, unmeasured, unimagined
RequiresOnly dataThat you can set A, ethically and practically

This is why one experiment can outrank a hundred regressions. And why we still run regressions: nobody can randomly assign smoking, poverty, or a childhood.

The "measured well" in the middle row matters too. A covariate measured with error only partly removes its confound, which connects back to the reliability weeks.

Two randoms, never blur them

GovernsBuys
Random samplingWho gets inExternal validity
Random assignmentWho gets which conditionInternal validity

They're independent: a study can have both, either, or neither. A convenience sample that is randomly assigned supports a causal claim about somebody, and leaves "about whom?" open.

Your Study 2 is exactly that: undergraduates (convenience), randomly assigned (causal). Say so in your Discussion.

The comparison group is not optional

An experiment without one is personal experience wearing a lab coat.

"Everyone got more optimistic after journaling" means nothing until you know what happened to people who didn't journal about gratitude.

  • No-treatment control: no journal at all
  • Placebo / active control: everything except the active ingredient, such as writing about today’s events
  • Another real treatment: "compared with the current best method," which is often the more interesting question

Choosing the comparison is choosing the claim. Gratitude-vs-neutral-writing and gratitude-vs-nothing answer different questions.

Gratitude vs nothing confounds gratitude with writing, with ten minutes of reflection, and with being in a study that asked you to do something. The neutral-journal control holds all of those constant, so only the content differs.

The experiment’s effect size

Cohen’s d: yes, experimental vs control

d = (Mgratitude − Mneutral) ÷ SD

It answers how far apart the two conditions ended up, in units of how much people vary anyway.

  • The worked example: optimism SD ≈ 10, gratitude +5 points, so d = 0.5.
  • d = 0.5 means the average gratitude writer scores higher than about 69% of neutral writers.
  • It is Study 2’s version of Study 1’s r: both say how much, which p never does.

Answers the Oct 13 break question directly. The 69% is U3 = Φ(0.5) = .69. For conversion between the two: d = 2r ÷ √(1 − r²), so r = .24 is about d = 0.5. Practical 5 powers Study 2 from this d.

Your Study 2

Your covariate changes jobs

Study 1 (correlational): stress and optimism are both measured. A third variable could produce the r, so the covariate is a suspect.

Study 2 (experimental): random assignment makes condition independent of stress, on average. Stress can’t confound the effect of journaling.

So in Study 2 a covariate has two jobs left:

  1. Soak up noise: account for the optimism differences stress explains, and the effect of journaling gets easier to see.
  2. Moderate: does gratitude help stressed people more? That’s Tier 2 (low vs high stress) or Tier 3 (stress as a continuous moderator).

A covariate adjusts the effect; a moderator changes it. Same variable, different question: the distinction from last week’s debrief, now inside your own design.

Several students named covariates and moderators as still unclear on Oct 13, after the Test 1 debrief. This is the payoff: the reason a pre-randomization covariate is safe in an experiment is the reason random assignment works at all. Practical 5's tiers map onto job 2.

Does background music improve studying?

60 students from a third-year psych course signed up via a shared sheet listing two sessions and chose whichever suited their timetable. The Tuesday 9am session became the music group (n = 31); Thursday 4pm became silence (n = 29).

The music group studied in the department lounge (sofas, natural light) with instrumental music. The silence group used a standard classroom. Same chapter, 30 minutes.

After studying, a 10-question quiz written by the researcher. Mean 9.1 / 10; 22 participants scored a perfect 10.

The researcher ran both sessions, greeted participants, explained the hypothesis that "music helps you focus," and marked the quizzes.

Result: music 9.4 vs silence 8.8, p = .04. Conclusion: install speakers in study spaces university-wide.

Leave this on screen for the full 20 minutes. Circulate. Don't confirm finds; say "write it down precisely."

The five

  1. Selection effect: participants chose their session, and 9am people differ from 4pm people.
  2. Design confound: the music group also got the comfortable lounge.
  3. Ceiling effect: mean 9.1/10 and 22 perfect scores leave no room to differ.
  4. Demand characteristics + no masking: he told them the hypothesis, then marked the quizzes himself.
  5. A second design confound: time of day is perfectly confounded with condition.

Bonus credit: over-generalization to the whole university · p = .04 with no effect size or CI · a home-made, unvalidated quiz.

Now: if you fix everything, what does the study cost?

That last question is the real content. Same room, same time, random assignment, masked marking and a harder test make a much more expensive study that answers a narrower question. That trade IS experimental design.

Part three

Everything else that can go wrong

Within or between?

Between-groups. Different people take different conditions: safe from contamination, and mandatory when one condition spoils the other.

The bill: people differ enormously, and all of it lands in your noise term.

Within-groups. Everyone does everything, so each person is their own control.

Maria’s mood after a funny video vs Maria’s mood after a neutral one: her baseline cheerfulness subtracts itself out.

The bill: order effects. Practice, fatigue, carryover. If everyone gets the same order, order is confounded with condition.

Counterbalance: half get A→B, half B→A, so order no longer lines up with condition.

Could your Study 2 be within? Not the gratitude one: you can’t un-count your blessings. When carryover is permanent, between is mandatory.

The impostor

Measure. Treat. Measure again. Celebrate the improvement.

It's the design of every testimonial and too many program evaluations.

It looks within-subjects but has no comparison condition, so everything else that happened between the two measurements is fully confounded with the treatment.

The repair is the answer to most problems in this course: a control group improves too, because maturation, history and regression happen to them as well. The difference between the two rises is the treatment.

The rogues' gallery

  • Maturation: they were going to change anyway
  • History: something else happened to everyone
  • Regression to the mean: select on extremity and the average drifts to the middle with no intervention at all
  • Attrition: the leavers take their scores with them
  • Testing & instrumentation: the measure changed, not the person

The master key: would this threat affect a control group equally? If yes, the comparison design handles it. If no, you've found the interesting weakness.

Over-learn regression to the mean. "We treated the most severe cases and they improved" is the weakest sentence in program evaluation, and it follows you into every domain that selects on extremes.

Attrition is the one the master key does not always handle: if the stressed people drop out of the gratitude condition (too much homework) but not the neutral one, the groups stop being randomized at the end.

Ninety-second drill

Name the threats

“A campus program recruits the 20 most-stressed students from a screening. After eight weeks of workshops, their average stress has dropped sharply. The program is declared a success.”

  • Regression to the mean: selected because extreme; that luck doesn’t repeat.
  • Maturation / history: eight weeks of term passed for everyone.
  • No comparison group: the one-group pretest/posttest, in the wild.
  • The fix: randomize the 20 to workshop-now vs waitlist. Both regress, both mature; the difference is the program.

This is exactly the shape of a Test 2 short answer. Reveal after the room proposes. Precision is the currency: "regression to the mean" scores, "they'd get better anyway" half-scores.

Minds that know things

Placebo effects: expecting improvement produces some. It's real machinery, not faking.

Demand characteristics: participants read what the study wants and oblige.

Experimenter expectancy: Clever Hans, at full scale.

Blinding. Single: participants don't know. Double: neither do the people running sessions and scoring outcomes.

It's the same move as random assignment: take the knowledge away from anyone whose behaviour could carry it. In a Method section, "double-blind, placebo-controlled" claims two structural safeguards.

Your gratitude participants can’t be blind to what they wrote. You can describe both journals as “reflective writing” and keep the hypothesis to yourself, which is the music study’s fault #4, repaired.

When nothing happens

Before you conclude "no effect," check three suspects. Check the phone before deciding nobody's calling.

  1. The manipulation was too weak. One gratitude sentence, once, when it takes a week of journaling to move anyone. (Your manipulation check tells you.)
  2. The DV couldn't register it. Ceiling, floor, or a scale too coarse.
  3. Noise buried it. Too few participants, unreliable measures, or a between design where individual differences swamp the signal.

Rule those out and a null becomes evidence. "d = 0.02, 95% CI [−.08, .12]" says: if there's an effect, it's trivial. That's a real scientific claim.

So is a narrower CI better? It’s more precise, not more right, and precision is exactly what lets a null mean something. Last week at n = 20 your power was 25%: a null from that study says almost nothing.

Answers the Oct 13 break question about CI width. The 25% is L5_sampling.R at n = 20, true r = .30. Never say "we proved there is no effect."
