2 · Set up one file, once

  1. Make a new folder for the paper. Put in it psy309_apa_reference.docx and either the worked example or PSY309_template.Rmd. Knit it before you change anything, so you know the setup works.
  2. Paste in your practical sections in paper order: Introduction, Method (Study 1, Study 2, Data simulation and analysis plan), Results (Study 1, Study 2), General Discussion, References, Appendix.
  3. One setup chunk at the top, loading every package once. Delete the extra library() lines that came in with each practical. Never put install.packages() in a chunk.
  4. One plan chunk holding every value you simulate from: means, SDs, your expected r and d, the moderation, both seeds, and the power analyses. The Method reports these values with inline code, and the simulation uses them, so the two can never disagree.
  5. Knit after every working session, not on the night. A paper that knitted on Monday and fails at 11:40 pm on the 8th almost always fails over something small: a missing image, a renamed variable, a chunk label used twice.
```{r plan}
dv_mean  <- 50;  dv_sd  <- 10      # YOUR DV
cov_mean <- 20;  cov_sd <- 4       # YOUR covariate
r_expected <- -.31;  seed1 <- 2026 # Study 1 (Practical 4)
d_expected <- 0.5;   seed2 <- 4026 # Study 2 (Practical 5)
n1 <- ceiling(pwr.r.test(r = abs(r_expected), power = .80)$n)
```

Chunk labels must be unique across the whole file. When two practicals both used ```{r setup} or ```{r study1}, the knit stops with “duplicate label”. Rename one.

3 · Preregistered means fixed before you look

Your Method’s Data simulation and analysis plan is a preregistration: everything a reader needs to know was decided before any data existed. It fixes four things for each study (the L11 lab block page has the full version):

  • One primary DV, and who is in the analysis.
  • The n, and the power analysis behind it.
  • The values, the model and the seed you simulated from.
  • One primary analysis. For Study 2, that is your tier.

Everything else you run is exploratory, and the paper says so, in the plan and again in the Results. Running a Tier 3 regression alongside your Tier 2 ANOVA is welcome. Presenting it as if it had been the plan is not.

Never shop for a seed. If your seed gives a non-significant result, that is your result. Report it and discuss it. Trying seeds until p < .05 is exactly the p-hacking we measured in Lecture 9, done to your own simulation. A null result reported honestly can earn full marks. A seed shopped for significance cannot.

A real study would also fix an exclusion rule and a stopping rule in advance. Your simulated participants can’t drop out, so you don’t need them, but one sentence saying what you’d use for a real study shows you know they exist.

4 · Make the two studies agree

Study 1 and Study 2 are the same people, the same measures and the same theory. Readers notice when they don’t match.

  • Same means and SDs. The DV and the covariate should be simulated from the same values in both studies.
  • Same relationship. If you are at Tier 2 or 3, the covariate’s slope in Study 2 should be the Study 1 correlation in your measures’ units: 
    b_cov <- r_expected * dv_sd / cov_sd   # -.31 * 10 / 4 = -0.78
  • Study 1 rebuilt exactly. Keep your Practical 4 seed and code, so the numbers in the paper match the ones you already submitted, unless your feedback asked you to change them. If you change a value, say why in one sentence.
  • The same names everywhere. If your construct is “exploration” in the Introduction, it isn’t “curiosity” in the Results. Define each construct once, early, and say how it is measured.

5 · Results that write themselves

Every number in your Results is inline R code. Type nothing by hand. Then if you fix a value in the plan chunk, every sentence updates when you knit.

As predicted by Hypothesis 1, stress was negatively correlated with optimism,
*r*(`r s1$parameter`) = `r apa(s1$estimate)`,
95% CI [`r apa(s1$conf.int[1])`, `r apa(s1$conf.int[2])`], *p* `r p_apa(s1$p.value)`
(Figure 1).
  • Let R choose the words too. `r ifelse(s1$p.value < .05, "a significant", "no significant")` keeps a sentence true whatever the data say.
  • Order: the descriptives (table), then the primary test, then what it means in plain words, tied to its hypothesis by number. Exploratory analyses come last, labelled.
  • APA details: no leading zero on r, p or η² (the apa() function does it); p < .001 rather than p = .000; report an effect size and, for a correlation, its 95% CI. Report t as positive, with abs(), and say in words which group was higher.
  • Figures: saved with ggsave(..., dpi = 300), shown with knitr::include_graphics(), numbered, captioned, and mentioned in the text before they appear. Error bars are ±1 SE, and the caption says so.
  • Say “simulated” once, clearly, in the Method. The Results can then report findings in the past tense, like any results section.

6 · Discussing results you simulated

Your data came out of your own hypotheses, so they can’t confirm them. The General Discussion does something more useful: it says what your results would mean if a real study found them, and what a real study could find that would prove you wrong. Write it as a series of short sections, each starting with a bold run-in heading:

  1. Opening. Restate the question and what the studies were designed to show. Say once that the data are simulated, so the results are a demonstration of the design, not evidence.
  2. One paragraph or two per hypothesis. The finding in plain words (no statistics needed here). Then what it would mean, set against the papers in your Introduction. For Study 1, also say what a correlation can’t tell you: direction, and third variables. Name one.
  3. What would count against your hypotheses. For each hypothesis, the result that would show you were wrong. This is what makes a preregistered prediction worth testing, and it is the paragraph most papers forget.
  4. Constraints on generality. Who were your imaginary participants, and who weren’t they? Then the limits of the design: self-report, one time point, the validities each study traded away. Tier 2: what categorizing your covariate gained and cost.
  5. What simulating taught you. Writing a hypothesis as an equation forces decisions. Which ones surprised you? Did the power analysis change your design?
  6. Future directions and implications. The next study you would run, and who could use the answer.
  7. Conclusion. Three or four sentences a reader could repeat to a friend.

Sentences that do the work

“Because our data were simulated from our hypotheses, the results show what the studies would find if those hypotheses are right, and how clearly the planned samples would detect it.”

“If a real sample showed the same association, it would fit the view that … (Author, Year). It would not tell us whether … causes …, or whether a third variable such as … drives both.”

“A gratitude effect of the same size in both stress groups would contradict Hypothesis 3.”

“We have no basis for extending these findings to …, because our participants were ….”

If your planted effect didn’t reach significance, the Discussion is easier, not harder: say what sample would have been needed, and what that teaches about planning a real study.

7 · Where the marks go, and where they leak

The Quercus rubric scores each section as Excellent, Good, Adequate or Weak: every element present and done well, every element present with one thin, an element missing or several thin, or most missing. Across the practicals, marks leaked in the same few places:

LeakFix
A construct named but never defined, or never tied to its measureDefine each construct the first time it appears, and name its measure in the Method.
Claims about the literature with no in-text citationEvery factual sentence about prior research gets (Author, Year), and every citation appears in the References.
Template or worked-example text left inSearch your knitted file for “gratitude”, “optimism”, “PSS”, “YOUR” and “Reflection” before you submit.
Numbers typed by hand, or not matching the codeInline R for every statistic.
A figure that is never referred to, or has no caption“(Figure 2)” in the sentence that reports the result it shows.
A Discussion that repeats the ResultsInterpret instead: what it would mean, what would count against it, who it applies to.
Too short or too long12–14 pages, counted from the first page of the Introduction to the end of the General Discussion.

8 · The knit check, before you submit

  • It knits in a fresh R session (Session → Restart R, then Knit).
  • The Method says the data were simulated in R, and gives the values, the model and the seed for each study.
  • One primary analysis per study is named in the plan; anything else is labelled exploratory.
  • Every statistic in the Results is inline R code.
  • Figures 1 and 2 are captioned and referred to in the text.
  • No code in the body. It prints in the appendix.
  • The Reflection sections are gone, and so is every line of template or example text.
  • 12–14 pages, Introduction to the end of the Discussion. Title, references and appendix don’t count.
  • References are APA 7, alphabetical, and match the in-text citations one for one.
