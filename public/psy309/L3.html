Part one

What kind of number is that?

Scales of measurement

ScaleWhat the levels areLegal moves
CategoricalNames. Program, condition, language.Count, compare frequencies
OrdinalRanks, unequal spacingMedian, rank statistics
IntervalEqual spacing, no true zeroMean, SD, differences
RatioEqual spacing and a true zeroAll of the above, plus ratios

The classic crime: a mean of a categorical variable. "Average program of study = 2.4" — a number with the appearance and nutritional value of information.

Keep this fast; it's in the Field Guide. The part worth your voice is the Likert slide next.

The Likert question — psychology's worst-kept secret

A 1-to-7 agreement scale is ordinal by the strict rules. Who certified that 5→6 is the same distance as 6→7?

And yet we routinely average Likert items into scale scores and analyze them as quantitative.

The defensible version: a multi-item scale — seven items averaged — behaves far more like a continuous quantity than any single item does.

Know both the rule and the working convention, and know which one you're invoking. Single item: be humble. Validated multi-item scale: proceed with a clear conscience.

Where this stops being philosophy

In R, scale type is a data type — and mismatches produce two classic disasters.

  • The polite disaster: categories stored as numbers. R computes a nonsense mean without complaint.
  • The loud disaster: numbers stored as text. Your correlation errors out, or a plot orders "10" before "9".

Half of practical debugging is str(mydata) — asking R what scale it thinks each column is.

Three ways to measure anything

Self-report — asking people.
Superpower: access to inner experience. Distortion: people can't always know, and don't always tell.

Observation — watching behaviour.
Superpower: the behaviour–report gap. Distortion: observers bend, and the observed perform.

Physiology — recording the body.
Superpower: independent of self-knowledge and self-presentation. Distortion: rarely construct-specific — heart rate reads the same for anxiety, excitement, caffeine, and stairs.

Convergence. Every window distorts, so look through more than one. When they disagree, you've learned something real.

Reliability: does the measure agree with itself?

A bathroom scale reading 68, then 74, then 61 kg in one minute has a problem — and you can diagnose it without knowing your true weight.

  • Test–retest — across time. Only meaningful if theory says the thing is stable.
  • Interrater — across observers. Wherever human judgment enters, this is the whole ballgame.
  • Internal consistency — across items. Cronbach's alpha; ~.70+ for research use.

All three are the same machinery — correlation, applied to pairs: time 1 with 2, rater A with B, item with item.

Point forward: interrater reliability is not abstract for them — five peers will rate their poster in December, and the fairness of that grade depends on it. We come back to this on the last day.

20 minutes on the script. Don't preview the finding — let the item-total correlations do the talking.

What did you find?

  • Alpha across all seven: around .70. Published value for this scale: ≈.87. Why is ours worse?
  • Item–total correlations: two items near zero, the rest .6–.7.
  • Drop those two: alpha jumps to ≈.88. Right onto the published value.

The reveal. Items 4 and 6 — "I usually get enough sleep" and "I enjoy cold weather" — were never part of the scale. Readable, answerable, sensible-looking items measuring something else.

The reliability statistics found them without being told.

Reveal one line at a time. The moment students realize two items were planted is the moment internal consistency stops being a formula.

Debrief

  • Did alpha tell you which items were the problem? No — alpha is one number. The item–total correlations did. This is why "α = .82" alone is a thin report.
  • If we'd never included the ringers, would alpha have told you the scale was good? It would have told you the items hang together. Which is not the same as measuring what you meant.
  • A tarot deck read consistently is reliable.

Reliability is the floor, not the ceiling. The harder question is next.

Part three

Valid — and for whom?

Measurement validity comes in grades

Subjective (judgment, no data yet):

Face — does it look right?
Content — does it cover all the parts your definition names?

Empirical (data required):

Criterion — does it predict relevant behaviour? (known-groups: does it separate groups it obviously should?)
Convergent — correlates with kindred measures
Discriminant — doesn't correlate with things theory says are different

Discriminant is the underrated one. Plenty of "new" constructs turn out to be old constructs in new fonts.

The one-way street

A measure can be reliable without being valid — but it cannot be valid without being reliable.

An unreliable measure is mostly noise, and noise can't be measuring the construct. Or anything else.

Examiners find this pairing as irresistible as I do.

The observer is part of the instrument

Observer bias — coders see what they expect. Rats labelled "maze-bright" ran faster for students timing them, though the labels were random. (Rosenthal & Fode, 1963)

Observer effects — expectations change the behaviour itself.

Clever Hans could do arithmetic — tapping out answers to astonished crowds. Pfungst (1911) found the trick: the questioner tensed as Hans approached the right count, and relaxed when he hit it.

Hans couldn't add. He could read humans magnificently. When the questioner didn't know the answer, the genius evaporated.

The fix, and it's cheap: masking. A coder who doesn't know the condition can't bend toward it.

Clever Hans is the cheapest mnemonic in the course — one horse, both observer problems. It returns at full scale in L7 as blinding.

Who did you ask?

  • Convenience sampling — whoever's easy to reach. Easy-to-reach people differ in exactly the ways that make them easy to reach.
  • Self-selection — whoever volunteers. Strong feelings volunteer; indifference scrolls past.

External validity comes from how you sampled, not how many.

A random sample of 1,000 supports ±3%. A convenience sample of 100,000 supports nothing — its errors don't cancel with size, they entrench.

Two randoms, never blur them: random sampling = who gets in (external validity). Random assignment = who gets which condition (internal validity). Favourite exam trap.

Flag the two-randoms distinction hard. It separates students who memorized from students who understood, which is why we keep asking it.
