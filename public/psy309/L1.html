Part one

Why we don't just trust experience

"I tried it and it worked."

The most persuasive sentence in the English language.

Today's job is to make you permanently suspicious of it — including when you're the one saying it.

Ask for examples out loud before moving on: supplements, study techniques, sleep hacks, breakup advice. Get three from the room. You'll use them later.

Defect one

Experience has no comparison group

You start a greens powder in September. By October you feel better.

Better compared to what?

Compared to the version of you who didn't buy it and lived through the same October — a person who does not exist.

Scared Straight. At-risk teens taken into prisons to be frightened out of crime. Spread across North America for decades on everyone's testimony: the teens seemed shaken, the organizers felt effective.

Randomized studies eventually supplied the missing comparison group. The programmes slightly increased later offending.

Petrosino, Turpin-Petrosino & Buehler (2003), Cochrane Review

Hold the greens-powder slide for a beat before revealing Scared Straight — let them feel that the logic is obvious before you show them a case where an entire policy field missed it for thirty years.

Key line: experience cannot show you what would have happened anyway. A comparison group is how research sees it for you.

Defect two

Experience is confounded

Even when the change is real, everything changed at once.

  • You started the greens powder…
  • …and it stopped being September
  • …and your course load settled
  • …and the sun came back after reading week

A confound is an alternative explanation tangled around the thing you're crediting. Life doesn't run controlled trials on itself.

This is the concept the whole course keeps returning to. Name it now, lightly; L4 does third variables properly and L6 does design confounds.

Defect two, continued

Research untangles on purpose

Generations of intuition said venting anger releases it — the kettle metaphor.

Bushman (2002) randomized angered participants to:

  • sit quietly for two minutes
  • punch a bag as exercise
  • punch a bag while picturing the person who insulted them

The venters were the most aggressive.

Not the least. The kettle metaphor was a good story; the comparison groups were better.

Ask the room to predict before revealing. Most will guess "least aggressive" or "no difference." This is the same item that opens the calibration quiz in forty minutes — and it lands harder the second time.

Defect three

Research is probabilistic — and that's not a weakness

"My grandmother smoked a pack a day and lived to 94."

True. And it refutes nothing.

  • Findings describe what happens more often, on average — not to every person every time.
  • Smoking raises the probability of lung cancer enormously. It does not schedule it.
  • Your grandmother isn't a counterexample. She's one of the data points the probability already includes.

It cuts both ways: an exception doesn't overturn a finding — and a finding doesn't promise you individual results.

Worth being careful here: students often hear "probabilistic" as "unreliable." Frame it as precision about what is being claimed, not hedging.

So why does experience feel so convincing?

Because your mind is an enthusiastic pattern-detector with no quality-control department.

Five specific failure modes — in the reading, and in the quiz you're about to take.

Research methods are not a set of rules for distrusting other people. They're prosthetics for the places where every mind, including yours, reliably fails.

This is the thesis of the course. Say it slowly. Then break.

Item 1

After being insulted, which group was most aggressive?

  1. Sat quietly for two minutes
  2. Punched a bag as exercise
  3. Punched a bag while picturing the person
  4. No meaningful difference

→ 3. The catharsis group. (Bushman, 2002)

They saw this an hour ago. If the room gets it right, say so — that's learning, visible, in one session.

Item 2

100 studies from top journals, directly replicated. What fraction found a significant effect in the same direction?

  1. About a third
  2. About 85%
  3. About 10%
  4. About 65%

→ 1. About a third. (Open Science Collaboration, 2015)

Replication effect sizes averaged about half the originals. Remember that number — in October you'll derive it yourselves.

Plant the flag for L5 and L9 here. Do not editorialize about "psychology is broken" — the framing is that the field checked, which most fields haven't.

Item 3

~400 students had their daily conversations recorded and words counted. What did they find?

  1. Women spoke roughly three times as many words
  2. No meaningful difference
  3. Women spoke somewhat more
  4. Men spoke somewhat more

→ 2. No meaningful difference. (Mehl et al., 2007)

≈16,215 words/day for women; ≈15,669 for men. Where did you first hear the 20,000-vs-7,000 claim?

That last question is the one to actually ask the room. Answers will be: a book, a documentary, a friend, "everyone knows." That list is exactly the sources hierarchy you teach after the next break.

Item 4

The marshmallow test, retested in a larger and more diverse sample with family background controlled:

  1. Stronger than the original
  2. About the same
  3. Reversed
  4. Less than half as strong, and largely explained by family background

→ 4. (Watts, Duncan & Quan, 2018)

A third variable was doing much of the work. That's Lecture 4, arriving early.

Worth naming that the original finding is still taught, still in TED talks, still in parenting books. Findings outlive their evidence in the culture.

Item 5

Seventeen labs re-ran the "pen in your teeth makes cartoons funnier" study with a pre-registered protocol. What happened?

  1. It replicated strongly
  2. It failed to replicate
  3. It replicated weakly but consistently
  4. The effect reversed

→ 2. It failed to replicate. (Wagenmakers et al., 2016)

Pre-registered. Hold that word — it's the fix, and it's what your practical assignments quietly are.

First mention of preregistration. Say the word, don't explain it yet. L5 and L9 do the work.

Trace the Claim

Your pair gets a psychology news article. Find the paper behind it.

  1. Read the article. Write its claim in one sentence.
  2. Find the actual paper (the Journal Reference is at the bottom).

What we found

Three pairs, in this order:

  1. One where the headline was fair
  2. One where an association became causal
  3. One where the sample won't carry the claim

You just found a claim, traced it to its evidence, classified it, and asked whether the evidence supports it.

That's the whole course. On day one.

Pick the three pairs while circulating, not on the fly. Starting with a fair headline is important — this is not a media witch hunt.

Part three

Where science actually lives

The habitat

Journal articles — first-hand, peer-reviewed, the primary source.

  • Empirical: new data. Abstract → Intro → Method → Results → Discussion.
  • Review: synthesizes many studies. A meta-analysis does it statistically.

Also real, in descending order of filtration:

  • Chapters in edited books
  • Preprints (no filter yet — read accordingly)
  • Conference posters — science in progress, like yours in December

Keep this brisk — it's in the Field Guide. The part worth your voice is the next slide.

Peer review is a filter, not an oracle

  • It means qualified skeptics thought it was worth publishing.
  • It does not mean the finding is true — reviewers don't re-run studies.
  • One study is one study, wherever it's published. (You saw the replication number.)

Predatory journals mimic real ones and will publish nearly anything for a fee. The tell: they seem delighted to have you. Real peer review is never delighted.

The predatory-journal line reliably gets a laugh and reliably sticks. Mention that they'll start getting these spam invitations the moment they publish anything.

Reading a paper in three passes

  • Pass 1 — triage (2 min). Abstract, figures, first paragraph of the Discussion. Most papers exit your life here. That's the system working.
  • Pass 2 — comprehension (15–20 min). Aims paragraph, then Method, then Results with the figures beside you.
  • Pass 3 — interrogation. What kind of claim, and does the design pay for it? That's what this course trains.

It is normal to understand 60% on pass two. Read for the design and the direction. The design is usually simpler than the notation.

Say the 60% line with conviction — a lot of students privately believe everyone else is reading these fluently.
