PSY309 · Lecture 9 · Poster

Your poster, by Monday noon

Due Monday 30 November at noon on Quercus: one .pptx with your narration recorded into it. Peer review opens in class on Tuesday 1 December.

1 · What you hand in

  • Either format. One poster slide that you walk us through, or a short deck of up to ten slides with one section per slide. The template is on Quercus.
  • About five minutes of your voice. Read from a script if you like: nobody sees you, and a script keeps you to time.
  • Rated on two things: informative (could a viewer explain your design, your result and its limits afterwards?) and engaging (did it hold them?). The prof and TAs give half the grade and five classmates give the other half.

2 · Ten beats, about 30 seconds each

BeatAlready in
1A real-life hook: an image, a headline, a question. Your why, not your method.Practical 1
2What we know, in two or three cited sentencesPracticals 1–2
3The gap: what we don’t know yetPractical 3 outline
4Your question and both hypotheses: Study 1 (correlational) and Study 2 (experimental)Practical 3
5Methods: who took part, what you measured, what you manipulatedPracticals 2–3
6Study 1: Figure 1, the r, and one plain-language sentencePractical 4
7Study 2: Figure 2, your tier’s test, and one plain-language sentencePractical 5
8What we learned about the questionNew
9Limits: which validities each study traded awayNew
10What you’d run nextNew

Beats 8–10 are your final paper’s General Discussion in miniature. Whatever you write for them now, you will reuse on December 8.

3 · Four rules that decide the grade

  1. The figure is the hero. Your voice carries the words, so the slide needs your plots: raw points, error bars, and axis labels big enough to read.
  2. Pass the sound-off test. With the audio muted, could someone still find your question, your two results and your takeaway?
  3. Make it readable on a laptop. Your reviewers watch on a laptop screen, and a whole poster on one slide is unreadable there. If you’re walking through one poster, duplicate the slide and zoom or crop into each section as you talk about it.
  4. Say it’s simulated, and pitch the claim at its true size. “Our simulated data show…” Reviewers rate the communication, not the effect size. An honest modest result beats an oversold big one. Causal language goes with your manipulation only, never with your covariate.

4 · Recording it

Use desktop PowerPoint. Microsoft 365 is free with your U of T account. Google Slides and Keynote can’t put narration into a .pptx: build there if you like, then download as .pptx and record in PowerPoint.

Windows

  1. Open your poster. Go to the Record tab → From Beginning. No Record tab? Use Slide Show → Record, or turn the tab on under File → Options → Customize Ribbon.
  2. Turn the camera off if you like. Check that the microphone (bottom right) is the one you mean to use.
  3. Press the red button, wait for the countdown, and talk. Use the arrow key to move to the next slide.
  4. Press stop when you’re done, then close the recording window.

Mac

  1. Open your poster. Go to Slide Show → Record Slide Show (newer versions also have a Record tab).
  2. Talk, and use the arrow key to move to the next slide. End the show when you’re done, and keep the timings if PowerPoint asks.

Either way

  • Each slide records its own clip. Wait a beat after each slide change before you speak, because narration isn’t captured during the transition.
  • Fluffed one slide? Re-record that slide only. You never have to redo the whole thing.
  • One poster slide, five minutes? That’s one long clip. It works, but one slip means redoing it all, so the duplicated, zoomed-in slides from rule 3 also make recording easier.
  • A large file? On Windows, File → Info → Compress Media shrinks the audio.

5 · Before you upload

  • Every recorded slide shows a speaker icon in its corner. No icon means no audio.
  • Saved as .pptx, not .ppsx, .pdf or a video.
  • Closed, reopened, and played from the beginning with the sound on. Only this test counts.
  • Around five minutes in total.
  • Both hypotheses, both figures, and a sentence saying the data are simulated.
  • Uploaded to Quercus before noon Monday. Aim for Sunday, so a failed upload is a problem rather than a penalty.
