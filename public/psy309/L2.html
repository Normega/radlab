Part one

Every rule is a scar

Research ethics didn't fall from the sky

Every clause in a modern ethics policy is a response to something researchers actually did.

Three stories: two indefensible, one genuinely hard. You need to be able to handle both kinds.

1932–1972

Tuskegee

  • The U.S. Public Health Service studied the progression of untreated syphilis in ~400 Black men in Alabama.
  • They were not told they had syphilis. They were told they were treated for "bad blood."
  • When penicillin became the standard cure in the 1940s, it was withheld.
  • It ended in 1972 — because the press found out. Not because anyone inside stopped it.

Three failures at once: deceived rather than informed · actively harmed when preventing it was cheap · the burden fell entirely on a population the benefits would never reach.

Respect · Welfare · Justice. Hold those three.

Do not rush this. Forty years is longer than these students have been alive. The reveal box names the three principles before they're formally introduced — the framework should feel like it was derived from the case, because historically it was.

1942–1952 · Canada

And it happened here

  • Canadian government researchers ran nutrition experiments on ~1,300 Indigenous people, including ~1,000 children in residential schools.
  • Researchers found malnourished communities and hungry children — and treated them as a baseline.
  • Some groups received supplements while control groups were deliberately left malnourished. Milk rations kept low for years. Dental care reportedly withheld because gum health was an outcome measure.

Mosby (2013), Histoire sociale / Social History

Same three failures. Canadian soil. Federal institutions. When TCPS2 devotes a chapter to research with Indigenous peoples, this is why.

The temptation after Tuskegee is to file research abuse under "elsewhere, long ago." This slide exists to close that exit. Canadian research ethics was not imported; it was earned here.

1963

Milgram: the hard case

Participants believed they were delivering escalating shocks to a fellow participant at an experimenter's calm insistence.

About two-thirds continued to the maximum voltage.

Nobody was physically harmed. Participants sweated, trembled, pleaded to stop — and continued.

Why it's hard, not simply bad:

The finding mattered. And it could not have been obtained honestly — you cannot study obedience in people who know the obedience is fake.

How much distress can knowledge be worth? Who decides? What is owed afterward?

Reasonable people disagree about Milgram. Nobody gets to disagree about Tuskegee.

Resist letting the room settle into "all old research was bad." The Milgram case is where ethical reasoning actually happens, and the REB exercise in an hour is built on exactly this kind of judgment.

What the scars built

Belmont (1979) — three principles:

  • Respect for persons — autonomy, informed consent, protection where autonomy is constrained
  • Beneficence — an honest accounting of risk against benefit
  • Justice — whoever bears the burden should share the benefit

APA adds two:

  • Fidelity and responsibility
  • Integrity — accurate, truthful, honest

Integrity is why p-hacking is an ethics violation, not just a statistical bad habit. We come back to that in November.

The Canadian framework

TCPS2 — the one that actually governs you

  • Respect for Persons — consent is ongoing, not a signature
  • Concern for Welfare — including privacy, social standing, economic circumstances
  • Justice — fair distribution of burdens and benefits

Tri-Agency policy. U of T takes that funding, so TCPS2 governs a fourth-year thesis survey exactly as it governs a $2M trial.

The REB reviews research before it happens: an independent committee, proportionate to risk.

Most psychology studies are minimal risk and get delegated review.

Doing a thesis? The TCPS2 CORE tutorial is free, takes an afternoon, and goes on your CV.

Chapter 9 (research with First Nations, Inuit and Métis peoples) is worth naming here explicitly — community engagement, not just individual consent. Protocol D in the exercise turns on it.

The lesson underneath all three stories

Every researcher in those cases believed they were doing something worthwhile.

Sincerity was never the missing ingredient. Independent judgment was.

Which is why the system never again lets researchers be the only judges of their own studies. In forty minutes, you're the judges.

Exercise · 35 minutes

You are the Research Ethics Board

Your team gets one protocol. 20 minutes.

  1. Read it.
  2. Verdict: approve / approve with changes / reject.
  3. Name the TCPS2 principle most at stake — and the specific feature that puts it there.
  4. If approving with changes: changes concrete enough to act on tomorrow.

Submit through the dashboard, then defend it in 90 seconds.

Two teams have the same protocol. If you disagree with each other, that's the interesting part — don't smooth it over.

Protocols are on Quercus by team number. Open check-in position 2 with the 20-minute countdown. Circulate; do not answer "is this the right answer?" — say "defend whichever you pick."

Protocol A

Group decision-making under social pressure

120 undergraduates, course credit. Told the study is about "how groups solve visual problems." Four confederates give unanimously wrong answers on 12 of 18 trials; the measure is conformity. Debriefed afterwards, offered data withdrawal.

Risk stated: "Minimal. Some may feel mild embarrassment when the deception is revealed."

Likely REB verdict: approve with changes. Justify the deception explicitly, strengthen the debriefing, and write an honest risk statement — "mild embarrassment" undersells discovering you abandoned the evidence of your own eyes eleven times.

Protocol B

Substance use and academic outcomes

800 students, $500 prize draw. Online survey: alcohol, cannabis, unprescribed stimulants, use before exams, academic discipline, mental health history.

Stored with student number for later transcript linking. Lives on an RA's laptop and a shared Drive folder. Kept indefinitely "for future studies." Consent form says "confidential."

Risk stated: "None; this is only a survey."

Likely REB verdict: reject as written. Identifiable illegal-behaviour data · unconsented record linkage · insecure storage · indefinite retention · a confidentiality claim that isn't true.

"It's only a survey" is the most common risk misjudgement in student research.

Protocol C

A twelve-week wellbeing programme

90 students scoring above a clinical cut-off for depression. Randomized to the programme or to a waitlist that waits twelve months.

Rationale: "A waitlist control is necessary to establish that improvement isn't simply the passage of time or regression to the mean."

Not in the protocol: any procedure if scores worsen · crisis protocol · referral pathway · stopping rule.

Likely verdict: approve with changes. The rationale for the control group is methodologically correct — say so. The problem isn't the design, it's the care: shorten the waitlist, add monitoring, a crisis protocol, referral, stopping rules.

This is the protocol that separates the strongest teams. If a team says "reject — withholding treatment is unethical," push back: without a control group you cannot know the programme works at all, and running an ineffective programme on everyone is its own harm.

Protocol D

Secondary analysis of community health data

No new data collection. A de-identified provincial dataset covering three First Nations communities. Analysis of diabetes, depression and substance use rates; community-level comparisons published with community names.

Rationale: "De-identified and already collected — no risk, no individual consent required." Community engagement: none.

Likely verdict: reject pending community engagement, a data governance agreement, and a publication plan the communities have agreed to.

De-identification protects individuals. Publishing "the community with the highest rate of X" harms a community. The consent question is collective — and TCPS2 Chapter 9 exists because of the history in this room's first hour. See also OCAP®.

End the defences on D. It contains no dishonesty and no obvious victim, which is exactly why it's the hardest — and why it lands.

Part three

From ideas to things you can count

The vocabulary the rest of the course is written in

Variable — anything with at least two levels. One level isn't a variable; it's a constant.

Measured vs manipulated — observed as found, or set by the researcher. This distinction is the hinge of the entire causal enterprise.

Construct — the abstract thing you care about. Nobody has ever seen a "stress."

Conceptual definition — what you mean by it, carefully.

Operational definition — exactly how it's measured or manipulated in this study.

Name the whole dimension, not one level. In "students who exercise sleep better," the variable isn't exercising — it's exercise amount.

Three kinds of claims

ClaimVariablesVerb tells you
FrequencyOne, measured"41% report…"
AssociationTwo, both measuredlinked to, tied to, predicts
CausalOne manipulated, one measuredboosts, cuts, curbs, triggers

The hedge trap. "Mindfulness may sharpen attention." The hedge softens the tone, not the claim. A causal claim gets no discount for saying maybe.

They used this on real headlines last week. Ask what they found — several will have a causal-verb-on-correlational-study example ready, which is a better example than any of mine.

Four validities: the interrogation kit

  • Construct — did they measure what they claim? Matters for every claim, always.
  • External — who and where does it generalize to? How they sampled, not how many.
  • Statistical — how strong, how precise, does it replicate?
  • Internal — could anything else explain this? The price of admission for causal claims.

No study achieves all four. A tight lab experiment buys internal validity by being artificial. A huge representative survey buys external validity and can't touch causation.

So the question is never "does this study have weaknesses?" — they all do. It's which validities did they prioritize, and does that fit the claim?

This slide is the spine of the whole course. If the REB exercise overran and something has to give, give up the operationalization round — not this.

Exercise · 25 minutes

Operationalization speed round

  1. 8 min. Your team's construct. Write a conceptual definition — one careful sentence — and three operationalizations: a self-report, a behavioural/observational one, and one other.
  2. 8 min. Pass to the next team. They attack: for each operationalization, what else might it be measuring?
  3. 9 min. Read out: definition, best operationalization, sharpest attack received.

Constructs: authenticity · burnout · grit · belonging · self-control · curiosity · loneliness · resilience · motivation · flourishing

Check-in position 3. The attack round is the part that teaches — being told your "grit" scale might be measuring conscientiousness is worth more than reading that it might.
