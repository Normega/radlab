Part one

Refusing to choose

The design of nearly every clinical trial

Take the within-versus-between trade-off — and refuse to choose.

A mixed design has at least one between-subjects factor and at least one within-subjects factor.

People randomized to groups (between). Everyone measured over time (within). Understand one mixed design deeply and you can read most of the intervention literature.

Time × Group

Randomize to gratitude or neutral journaling (between). Measure optimism before and after two weeks (within). Now run the factorial checklist, and watch what each effect means:

  • Main effect of Time — everyone changed. Uninteresting alone: maturation, history and regression all live here.
  • Main effect of Group — the groups differ on average. Also not the story.
  • The Time × Group interaction — did the groups change differently?

That cell is the finding. A clinical trial's entire causal claim lives in one interaction.

This is the course's running example (gratitude vs neutral journaling, optimism) with a pretest added. Students' Study 2 measures optimism once, after; adding a pretest turns it into a mixed design, which is a ready-made "future directions" sentence for the final paper's General Discussion.

How it's written

Optimism ~ Time * Group + (1|Participant)

Fixed effects — Time, Group, their interaction. The questions you're asking.

Random effect — (1|Participant). The design's within-ness, formally acknowledged.

That last term gets its own section after the break. First: a dataset that should disturb you.

One line in an ad

A stress-relief app's ad shows one line: users' average stress falling steadily from week 1 to week 8. What is the strongest critique of the claim that this graph shows the app works?

A. None needed: a decline this steady across eight weeks is consistent enough to show a causal effect.
B. Users chose to download the app, so the result can't generalize to people who wouldn't.
C. No comparison group: maturation, history or regression to the mean could each draw this line alone.
D. The drop must be regression to the mean, since people download stress apps when stress peaks.

C. One falling line is a one-group pretest/posttest: regression is only one of several rivals, and ruling them all out takes a second line, the Time × Group interaction.

Reading the graph

Two lines over time.

  • Parallel — both groups drift alike. No treatment effect, whatever the slopes.
  • Converging or diverging — the interaction. The finding.

Once you have this template, intervention studies read at a glance — and so do their abuses. A single improving line with no comparison group is the one-group pretest/posttest again, with better production values.

The trick is that departments differ in two ways at once: some students study more, and those same departments grade harder.

Three positive numbers, one negative number, one dataset. Which one is "the" answer?

Neither number is "the" answer

A student asks: "Should I study more?"

A within-department question. The answer is yes — emphatically.

A dean asks: "If students across the university studied more, would average grades rise?"

A between-department question, tangled up with who studies where.

The paradox isn't that the numbers disagree. It's that we casually assume one number could answer both questions.

The repair is structural: model the grouping. Give each department its own baseline: department as a factor (what you just ran), or (1|Department).

The person-level version matters for psychology's soul: a variable's relationship ACROSS people and WITHIN a person are different facts. Most theories are about the within-person one; most data are between-person.

Part two

Everyone starts somewhere different

The picture that explains everything

Plot each participant's scores across the study as their own thin line. Forty lines.

  • The lines start at wildly different heights. Some people are simply happier, calmer, faster, more anxious — before anyone manipulates anything.
  • The lines slope more similarly than they start. Your manipulation's push is usually modest compared to the standing differences between people.

Think of something where people's baselines vary more than any experiment could move them. Almost everything you care about qualifies.

Fixed and random, in one sentence each

Fixed effects are the questions you're asking — the average slope, the group difference, the interaction. Assumed to be the same process for everyone; estimated once.

Random effects acknowledge that each participant carries their own baseline. (1|Participant) says: give every person their own intercept, and don't charge those differences against the treatment's account.

That's the examinable core. No lmer() on the test.

What acknowledging baselines buys

  • Power. Baseline differences are usually the biggest source of variance; giving them their own term removes them from the noise your effect must shout over. Same magic as the within-subjects design — expressed as a model instead of a procedure.
  • Honesty about repeated measures. Two measurements from the same person are not two independent people. Treating them as such counts evidence twice and shrinks p-values fraudulently.
  • The Simpson repair. Modelling within-person effects separately from between-person differences is the disaggregation you just watched.

A paired t-test is secretly a tiny mixed model. So is every "each person is their own control" argument you've met.

Why this is now psychology's default

Once you see (1|Participant), you see the pattern everywhere: students in classrooms, trials in subjects, patients in clinics, measurements in days.

Real data comes in clusters whose members resemble each other. The model that admits it wins.

You won't fit these this term. But when your thesis dataset arrives with repeated measures — and it will — you'll recognize the shape and know why the analysis looks like that.

Exercise B · 25 minutes · Test 2 prep

Sort the third variable

Ten scenarios. Mediator, moderator, or confound?

The mnemonic:
Moderation = when / for whom
Mediation = why
Confound = controlling for

The test that decides it — the arrows:

Mediator: receives an arrow from X.
Confound: sends arrows to both X and Y, receives none.
Moderator: receives nothing; changes the size of X→Y across its own levels.

Vote alone on your phone first, justify second. Five rounds of two. On Test 2 the marks are for the arrows, not the label.

The two that matter

Item 9 — the expensive mistake

Poverty predicts poorer health. A researcher notes poverty produces chronic stress, controls for chronic stress, finds the poverty–health link shrinks sharply, and concludes poverty matters less than believed.

Chronic stress is a mediator being treated as a confound.

Controlling it away doesn't purify the estimate — it amputates the explanation and then reports the effect as small. With real policy consequences.

What should they have run instead? A mediation analysis: how much of poverty's effect travels through stress.

The two that matter

Item 10 — the honest complication

Students who attend office hours earn better grades. Conscientiousness predicts attending office hours — and studying more, submitting on time, asking for help.

Confound. But push on it: if conscientious students benefit more from office hours than others do, it's also a moderator.

A variable's role is a claim about causal structure, not a property of the variable.

The same variable can play different roles in different questions. Which is why the arrows are the whole game.

Where should the cutoff sit?

Dr. Sirocco designs a 5-minute reading screen for every Grade 1 child. Any child flagged gets a 20-minute follow-up assessment; a child who is missed goes a year without reading support. Where should the flagging cutoff sit?

A. Strict, favouring specificity: as in a trial, flagging a child who reads fine is the worse error.
B. At p = .05, because alpha fixes the error rates for every screening decision automatically.
C. Wherever it makes the two kinds of error equally common, since neither one matters more.
D. Lenient, favouring sensitivity: a false flag costs 20 minutes, a miss costs a year of support.

D. Lenient: when a false alarm costs a 20-minute follow-up and a miss costs a year of reading support, you tune for sensitivity, like airport security, not like a criminal trial.

What the coin does not buy

Dr. Grater randomly assigns 80 students to a new flashcard app or to their usual notes. App users also get a friendly weekly check-in text from her assistant; note users get nothing. The app group scores higher. Does random assignment rule out every other explanation?

A. Yes: random assignment balances everything, so the app is the only difference between the groups.
B. No: the coin balanced only who joined each group; the weekly texts are a later design confound.
C. No: with just 40 per group, random assignment balances nothing, so this is really a selection effect.

A program that worked?

A campus wellness office invites students to sign up for a four-week therapy-dog program. The 48 students who sign up complete a stress scale (0–40) before the first session and again after the fourth weekly session, which falls just after midterms end. Mean stress drops from 29 to 21 (p = .01). The office concludes that the program reduces stress and asks for funding to expand it.

A. What design is this? Name it, and say what it lacks that would let the office make a causal claim. (1 mark)

B. Think about who signed up, and when. Name a threat this creates, and explain how it alone could produce the drop. (1 mark)

C. The second measurement falls just after midterms end. Name the threat this creates, and explain how it could produce the drop. (1 mark)

D. The office reports only that the drop is significant (p = .01). Name what else you would need to judge the size of the change, and say why p alone is not enough. (1 mark)

E. Redesign the study so the office could conclude that the dogs reduce stress. Describe the design, and say which result would be the evidence. (1 mark)

The test's format exactly: five boxed parts, one mark each, about two sentences each, roughly 12 minutes for the whole question. Alone first, then pairs. The next slide reveals one part per press.

Practice short answer 1 of 2 · the marks

Five marks, five named concepts

  • A · One-group pretest/posttest. No comparison group, so nothing shows what stress would have done without the dogs.
  • B · Regression to the mean. Students sign up when their stress peaks; their next score drifts back toward their usual level, with no treatment at all.
  • C · History. Midterms end between the two measurements, and stress falls then for every student, dogs or no dogs.
  • D · An effect size and a confidence interval. p says a drop this big is unlikely by chance, not how big it is or how precisely it’s known.
  • E · Randomize sign-ups to dogs now vs a waitlist, measure both before and after. The Time × Group interaction is the evidence: the waitlist shares regression and the end of midterms.

The dogs may well work: this study cannot know.

Practice short answer 2 of 2 · 5 marks

A walk that changed nothing

Dr. Thistlewaite tests whether a walk in nature boosts creativity. Seventy students are randomly assigned, 35 per condition, to a 10-minute treadmill walk in the lab while watching a video on a phone held at arm’s length: a forest trail or an office hallway. Afterwards everyone rates “How much did the walk feel like being in nature?” (1–7): forest M = 2.4, hallway M = 2.1. Everyone then has 15 minutes for 10 classic insight puzzles; 74% in each condition solve none. On a creativity questionnaire at sign-up, students in both conditions span the whole range. Finally: “How creative do you feel right now?” (Not / Somewhat / Very). Neither measure differs (puzzles: d = 0.06, p = .80). Conclusion: “Nature does not boost creativity.”

A. Look at the nature rating. Name a reason the study could find nothing even if nature does boost creativity, and say what you would change. (1 mark)

B. Look at the puzzle scores. Name the problem they reveal, and say what you would change. (1 mark)

C. Think about how much students differ in creativity and how the conditions were formed. Give a third, distinct reason, and say how you would fix it. (1 mark)

D. A colleague says: “Just rerun it with 300 students per condition.” Would that fix the problems in A and B? Explain why or why not. (1 mark)

E. The “How creative do you feel right now?” item also showed nothing. Name its weakness, and say how you would improve it. (1 mark)

Family 2 from "What's on it" (a null result, distinct reasons, each with a fix). Give them the time on paper or phones, alone first, then pairs. Next slide reveals one part per press. Not on Test 2: none of these facts is in the test pool.

Practice short answer 2 of 2 · the marks

Five marks, five named problems

  • A · The manipulation never landed. The check shows both walks felt about equally un-natural (2.4 vs 2.1 of 7), so the IV barely differed. Fix: a real trail vs a hallway, or an immersive video, confirmed by the check.
  • B · Floor effect. 74% solve nothing in both conditions: the DV has no room to register a gain. Fix: graded or easier puzzles, piloted so scores spread. Plot first.
  • C · Noise from individual differences. Between subjects, the huge spread in baseline creativity sits in the error term and swamps a modest effect. Fix: measure each person twice (within subjects, counterbalanced, or a pretest): today’s random intercept.
  • D · No. More people fix noise, not a manipulation that barely differed or a test nobody can pass. It would measure “nothing” more precisely.
  • E · Insensitive, coarse scale. A student who feels a bit more creative still says “Somewhat”. Fix: a 0–100 slider or a multi-item scale.

Same answer shape every time: name it, point to the detail, fix it. “The study was flawed” earns nothing.
