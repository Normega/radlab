Part one

What does a 7 mean?

Why we calibrate before we grade

Five randomly assigned classmates determine about half of each poster's grade.

Those five have never agreed on what a 7 means.

Pooling only helps if the raters are measuring the same thing. Otherwise you're averaging noise and calling it a grade.

In week 3 you computed interrater reliability as an abstraction. Today you are the raters, the stakes are your classmates' marks, and the same statistic decides whether this is fair.

Say this out loud before starting. It converts the exercise from an administrative warm-up into the term's closing argument.

Look at the spread

Yours first: check-in 5, both scores. Then what it usually looks like:

Are you disagreeing about the poster, or about what the numbers mean?

Almost always the second. That distinction is the entire content of interrater reliability — discovered, rather than defined.

Two rules before you start

  • Judge the communication, not the result. A modest or null-shaped finding presented honestly can outscore an oversold one. You've spent a term learning what over-claiming looks like — this is where that counts.
  • Use the range. Scoring everything an 8 isn't kindness, it's abdication — and it's visible, since divergent scores get checked against the group average.

Your own peer-review grade depends on being useful: specific comments, defensible scores. Not harsh, not generous — useful.

From Final_Paper_2026.md. The old slide described a single study and a 500–1000-word introduction; the paper is two studies and the introduction is about four pages. The 12–14-page total is the requirement; the section lengths are guides.

The discussion is where the term pays off

  • Each hypothesis, in plain language, set against the literature from your introduction.
  • Constraints on generalizability. Who were your imaginary participants, and who weren’t they?
  • Which validities did each study trade away? Study 1 can’t rule out a third variable; Study 2 can, for the manipulation only.
  • Tier 2: what did categorizing your covariate gain, and cost? Your Practical 5 reflection is a first draft.

Naming your own study’s limits precisely is not weakness. It’s what separates a research paper from an advertisement.

The worked example makes the third bullet concrete: stress predicts lower optimism in Study 1 but nobody assigned stress, so in Study 2 the causal claim is about gratitude journaling, and stress only says for whom it works better.

RMarkdown, in practice

  • No code in the body. The template prints it all in the appendix, which must knit and reproduce every number.
  • Every statistic is inline R. Type a number by hand and it stops matching your analysis.
  • Set a seed, and report it in the Method with the values you simulated from.
  • Keep psy309_apa_reference.docx beside the .Rmd. Knit early and often.

The classic disaster is a document that knits on Monday and dies at 11:40pm on Tuesday over an image path or a missing package.

Watch: I’m going to break one now and fix it in front of you.

Actually do this live: open a minimal .Rmd, knit to .docx, break it (a bad image path in include_graphics, or the reference .docx moved out of the folder), fix it. Two minutes, worth more than any slide saying "knit early." The old slide said "show the analysis code"; the course template puts all code in the appendix, so the body has none.
