Part one

What does a 4 mean?

Why we calibrate before we grade

Five randomly assigned classmates determine about half of each poster’s grade.

Those five have never agreed on what a 4 means.

Pooling only helps if the raters are measuring the same thing. Otherwise you’re averaging noise and calling it a grade.

In week 3 you computed interrater reliability as an abstraction. This week you are the raters, the stakes are your classmates’ marks, and the same statistic decides whether this is fair.

Say this before the rubric. It turns the reviews from an administrative chore into the term’s closing argument.

Two criteria, each out of 5

Informative. Does it tell the whole research story, clearly and accurately? The question and why it matters; both hypotheses; how each study was done; the results of both studies, with Figures 1 and 2; what the findings mean.

Engaging. Does it hold a viewer and make the story easy to follow? Narration a classmate outside the topic can follow; a readable layout, not walls of text; legible figures; a clear, lively delivery.

That’s the Quercus rubric, word for word. Your two scores add to the poster’s peer mark out of 10, and each review needs a comment.

Quoted from the Research Poster rubric on Quercus (Informative /5, Engaging /5). The old in-room version of this deck scored each criterion out of 10; the rubric is out of 5.

Compare yours with mine

I’ll give my two scores and the row of the table each one came from.

  • Within 1 of mine? You’re calibrated. Rate your five the same way.
  • 2 or more apart? Find the row where we split, and read both cells again.

Were we disagreeing about the poster, or about what the numbers mean?

Almost always the second. That distinction is the entire content of interrater reliability, discovered rather than defined.

Talk through your own two scores and the strength and improvement you’d write. Name the table row each score comes from. That reasoning, not the scores, is what students copy.

Two rules before you start

  • Judge the communication, not the result. A modest or null-shaped finding presented honestly can outscore an oversold one. You’ve spent a term learning what over-claiming looks like; this is where that counts.
  • Use the range. Scoring everything a 4 isn’t kindness, it’s abdication. Five posters genuinely differ, and scores far from the group average get checked.

Your own peer-review grade depends on being useful: specific comments, scores in line with the other reviewers. Not harsh, not generous: useful.

  • Then write the Results in the past tense, like any results section.

Tiers 2 and 3

Simulating an interaction: one line, four terms

Optimism = dv_mean +                                  # neutral writer, average stress
           b_cov * (Stress - cov_mean) +              # main effect: stress
           b_iv  * Gratitude +                        # main effect: gratitude
           b_int * (Stress - cov_mean) * Gratitude +  # interaction
           rnorm(n(), 0, noise_sd)                    # everything else
  • Code the manipulation 0/1, centre the covariate. Then every term means something about an average person.
  • b_cov: the stress slope for neutral writers. From Study 1: r_expected * dv_sd / cov_sd = −0.78.
  • b_iv: the gratitude effect at average stress. From your d: d_expected * dv_sd = 5.
  • b_int: how much the gratitude effect changes per point of stress. That’s the interaction.

Go term by term, and plug in Gratitude = 0, then 1. With Gratitude = 0 the last two terms vanish: that’s a neutral writer, whose optimism depends on stress only. With Gratitude = 1 you add 5, plus 0.60 for every point of stress above average. Students most often confuse the main-effect terms with “the effect overall”; with centring they are the effect at average stress.

Choosing b_int: decide the effect at low and high

Don’t guess the interaction directly. Decide what your hypothesis says the manipulation does at low stress (−1 SD) and at high stress (+1 SD):

Low stress (16)High stress (24)
Gratitude effect2.6 points7.4 points
gap   <- 4.8                  # 7.4 - 2.6
b_int <- gap / (2 * cov_sd)   # = 0.60, spread over 8 stress points
  • The sign follows the hypothesis: positive if the manipulation helps more as the covariate rises, negative if it helps less.
  • Check before you simulate: the effect at stress s is b_iv + b_int * (s - cov_mean). At 16: 5 − 2.4 = 2.6. At 24: 5 + 2.4 = 7.4. Then check that Figure 2 shows it.
  • Interactions need about 4× the sample of a main effect (Gelman et al., 2020). A rough rule, and fine to use.

This is the slide for students who know their hypothesis in words but can’t turn it into a number. The average of the two effects is b_iv (5), and the gap over the distance is b_int. The worked example’s plan chunk now does exactly this.

If your result isn’t significant

Two honest options, and either can earn full marks.

Report it. Then say what it tells you about the design. Usually: the planned sample can’t reliably detect the effect you planted.

A preregistration that shows a design won’t work has done its job, before a single real participant was spent on it.

Change the seed to show what a significant result looks like, and say so in the plan, with what the first seed showed.

That first result is information about your power. It isn’t something to hide.

The one thing you can’t do is change the seed and present the new result as if it were the first. That’s the p-hacking from Lecture 9, done to your own simulation.

The discussion is where the term pays off

  • Each hypothesis, in plain language, and what it would mean if a real study found it, set against the literature in your Introduction.
  • What would count against each hypothesis. The result that would show you were wrong.
  • Constraints on generality. Who were your imaginary participants, and who weren’t they? Which validities did each study trade away?
  • Tier 2: what did categorizing your covariate gain, and cost? Your Practical 5 reflection is a first draft.

Naming your own study’s limits precisely is not weakness. It’s what separates a research paper from an advertisement.

The worked example makes the validity point concrete: stress predicts lower optimism in Study 1, but nobody assigned stress, so in Study 2 the causal claim is about gratitude journaling, and stress only says for whom it works better.

RMarkdown, in practice

  • No code in the body. It all prints in the appendix, which must knit and reproduce every number.
  • Every statistic is inline R. Type a number by hand and it stops matching your analysis.
  • One plan chunk at the top holds every value and both seeds; the Method reports them inline.
  • Keep psy309_apa_reference.docx beside the .Rmd. Knit early and often.

The classic disaster is a document that knits on Monday and dies at 11:40pm on Tuesday over an image path or a missing package.
