Part one

What does a 7 mean?

Why we calibrate before we grade

Five randomly assigned classmates determine about half of each poster's grade.

Those five have never agreed on what a 7 means.

Pooling only helps if the raters are measuring the same thing. Otherwise you're averaging noise and calling it a grade.

In week 3 you computed interrater reliability as an abstraction. Today you are the raters, the stakes are your classmates' marks, and the same statistic decides whether this is fair.

Say this out loud before starting. It converts the exercise from an administrative warm-up into the term's closing argument.

Look at the spread

  • Who gave it a 9? Say why.
  • Who gave it a 5? Say why.

Are you disagreeing about the poster, or about what the numbers mean?

Almost always the second. That distinction is the entire content of interrater reliability — discovered, rather than defined.

Take the 9 first, then the 5. Let them hear each other's reasoning before you put the rubric up.

Two rules before you start

  • Judge the communication, not the result. A modest or null-shaped finding presented honestly can outscore an oversold one. You've spent a term learning what over-claiming looks like — this is where that counts.
  • Use the range. Scoring everything an 8 isn't kindness, it's abdication — and it's visible, since divergent scores get checked against the group average.

Your own peer-review grade depends on being useful: specific comments, defensible scores. Not harsh, not generous — useful.

Part two

The last thing you'll write

The final paper, section by section

Introduction (~500–1000 words). A funnel. Introduce IV, DV, covariate and the literature linking them. The last paragraph states your purpose and names your operationalized variables.

Method. Subheadings: Participants, Materials, Procedure, Statistical Analyses. Enough detail to recreate the simulated data — or actually run the study.

Results. Under subheadings: figure with caption → the statistics → a plain-sentence interpretation linking numbers to figure. Effect sizes and intervals, not just tests. Say why each follow-up analysis is run.

Discussion. Interpret against your introduction's literature. Then the honesty budget.

The discussion is where the term pays off

  • Which validities did your design trade away?
  • Your constraints on generality — who, where, when should this hold?
  • What did categorizing your continuous covariate add — and what did it cost — compared with keeping it continuous?

Naming your own study's limits precisely is not weakness. It's the thing that separates a research paper from an advertisement.

RMarkdown, in practice

  • Show the analysis code — it's part of the science being demonstrated.
  • Hide the plumbing — figure-generation and wrangling can run with echo=FALSE; describe the process in the Method.
  • Set a seed. Every knit reproduces identical numbers.
  • Knit early and often.

The classic disaster is a document that knits on Monday and dies at 11:40pm Thursday over an image path or a missing package.

Watch: I'm going to break one now and fix it in front of you.

Actually do this live — open a minimal .Rmd, knit to .docx, break it (bad image path), fix it. Two minutes, worth more than any slide saying "knit early."

Reuse is the design, not an offence

Your practicals were the paper, drafted in five instalments.

P1 stated the question. P2 fixed the measures. P3 the manipulation and covariate. P4–P5 the predicted effects and the analysis plan.

You have been writing a preregistration all term. Now you run it.

Revision is expected — the practicals were written toward feedback, the paper toward a reader. But you're assembling and polishing, not starting over. Plagiarism rules govern other people's words. Your own prior work in this course is yours to build with.

Say this explicitly and out loud. Students ask it every single year, and the ones who don't ask sometimes rewrite everything from scratch for no reason.
