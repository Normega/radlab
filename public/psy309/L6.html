The question most of you missed

A researcher suspects that upper-year students both sleep less and score higher. She adds year of study to her regression, next to sleep, to see whether sleep still predicts exam scores once year is accounted for. What is year of study doing?

30 of 41 of you said moderator. The answer is covariate.

If the third variable is……the question it answers isModel
a covariateDoes X still predict Y once this is held constant?Y ~ X + C
a moderatorDoes the X–Y link change from one level of it to the next?Y ~ X * M
a mediatorIs it the route by which X changes Y?Y ~ X + M

The tell is the verb. “Still holds once accounted for” is control. “Stronger for” or “only among” is moderation. And year can’t be a mediator: sleeping more can’t change what year you’re in.

Item T1-L2-010 (Dr. Snoozewell). 5 of 41 chose the key; 30 chose moderator. Its discrimination was negative (−.28): the students choosing moderator averaged 33.0 of 40 on the other items, against 29.8 for those choosing covariate. That is the signature of an item that misleads strong students rather than one that catches weak ones. Decide the scoring before class (credit everyone, or drop it from the 40), and say the decision here; it costs nothing and the room will hear that the item analysis is taken seriously. The covariate row is new beside the L4 cheat sheet, which only had “confound”; a covariate is the measured stand-in you put in the model to hold a suspected confound constant. Practical 3 uses “covariate” in the looser sense too (a measured variable related to the DV), so this is the moment to join the two.

Part one

Describe before you analyse

Shape first

Plot it before you summarize it. Every summary statistic you are about to compute assumes you already know the shape.

Bimodal is the one to notice — two humps usually mean two populations wearing one label, and averaging them describes nobody.

Ask what the mean would be for the bimodal panel, and who it describes. Answer: a value almost no one in the data has.

Then centre, then spread

Mean is a balance point — one four-hour commute drags it.

Median ignores how extreme the extremes are, which is why incomes and house prices are reported this way.

Mode is the only centre available for categories.

The gap between mean and median is itself information: a mean well above the median announces a right tail before you have plotted anything.

Variability is not noise around the real answer

Two classes both average 72%. One spans 70–74. The other spans 40–100.

These are utterly different courses. Spread is half the description, not an afterthought.

  • SD — roughly the typical distance from the mean, in the variable's own units
  • z-score — subtract the mean, divide by SD: position in your own distribution

That z-score move is about to reappear twice: as Cohen's d, and as a standardized beta. One idea, three costumes.

Effect size: the question significance doesn't answer

"The difference was statistically significant" doesn't say how big it was.

Cohen's d — the difference between two means, in SD units.

0.2 small · 0.5 medium · 0.8 large. Most published psychology: d = 0.2 to 0.6.

r and r² — for associations. η² — variance explained by a factor.

Benchmarks are conversation-starters, not verdicts. A "small" d = 0.2 on university retention, applied to every student in Ontario, is enormous.

Every effect size on this slide is the same fraction: signal over noise. How big the difference is, relative to how much people vary anyway — which is why even a “large” effect leaves the distributions mostly overlapping.

Confidence intervals: how sure, not just how much

r = .32, 95% CI [.21, .43]  is knowledge.
r = .32, 95% CI [.02, .62]  is a shrug in APA format.

  • Width is precision. A wide interval means the study genuinely cannot tell you whether the effect is trivial or substantial.
  • Zero matters. An interval excluding zero is significance, read off the interval.
  • It brackets the population value, not the people. 95% CI ≠ 95% of participants.

Precision improves with √n: quadrupling your sample halves the interval. Which is why precision gets expensive fast.

Part 3 runs 10,000 studies of the same world. Two numbers come out.

  • Power at n = 20 is about 25%. In a world where the effect is definitely real, three quarters of these studies conclude "no effect."
  • Among the studies that reached significance, the mean r is about .54 — nearly double the truth.

Significance filtering doesn't just lose real effects. It exaggerates the ones it keeps.

Only the orange bars count as “significant” — and their average is r = .54 against a truth of .30.

Reveal one at a time. The second number is the winner's curse, and they derived it — do not let it go by as a bullet. Three things you now know from your own data

  1. A single non-significant result is nearly uninformative when power is low. "We found no effect" often means "we ran a study that couldn't find effects."
  2. Published effects run hot. Remember week 1: replications average about half the original effect size. You just explained why.
  3. This is why you do a power analysis before collecting data — from an effect size the literature supports, not a hopeful guess. That is Practical 5, and your Practical 3 pilot is where you start choosing it.

Part three

Two ways to be wrong

Cry wolf, or sleep through it

There IS an effectThere is NO effect
You say yesCorrect — powerType I — false positive
You say noType II — false negativeCorrect

Alpha (.05) = your tolerance for crying wolf. Power (aim ≥ .80) = your chance of catching a real wolf.

Power rises with effect size, sample size, and less noise. You just watched all three.

Which error is worse? It depends what's at stake

Criminal trials are engineered against false positives. "Beyond a reasonable doubt" accepts that some guilty people walk, because convicting the innocent is judged the worse error.

Airport security runs the other way — a thousand flagged water bottles rather than one missed threat. So does screening for treatable disease.

Your research has stakes too, quieter ones: a false positive sends a literature chasing a phantom; a false negative buries a real phenomenon.

Asking which error is the greater danger in your study is a sign of methodological adulthood. Practical 5 asks you directly.

You have been preregistering all term

Across the practicals you choose a question, define measures, name a manipulation, predict an effect size from literature, and specify your analysis — all before any data exist.

In modern research that document has a name.

  • It doesn't solve fraud. It solves flexibility in honest hands — the dozens of defensible choices made after seeing results.
  • The line it draws: confirmatory (you predicted it) vs exploratory (you noticed it). Both are legitimate. Dressing the second as the first is not.

In November you'll see exactly how much that flexibility is worth. Bring a strong stomach.

Practical 3 · Part 3 meets today

Choosing your pilot’s numbers

“Make the difference between conditions the size you actually expect.” That’s an effect size, so choose it as one.

Neutral   <- rnorm(100, mean = 50, sd = 10)
Gratitude <- rnorm(100, mean = 55, sd = 10)   # expected +5
  • d = (55 − 50) / 10 = 0.5. A medium effect: you just wrote a prediction about size.
  • SE = SD / √n = 10 / √100 = 1. That’s the column in your table, and why it shrinks as n grows.
  • Is 100 per group enough? If d = 0.5, you’d detect it 94% of the time. If d = 0.2, only 29%. Your Part 4 answer depends on the d you chose.

The numbers are the course's worked example (Practical3_2026.Rmd: optimism, gratitude vs neutral journaling, seed 2026). Power from power.t.test in R: d = 0.5 with 100 per group gives .94; d = 0.2 gives .29 (394 per group needed for .80); d = 0.8 needs 26. Students choosing a d above 1 should be asked where in the literature an effect that large lives; the effect-sizes slide said most of psychology sits between 0.2 and 0.6.
