Part one

Describe before you analyse

Shape first, then centre, then spread

Shape. Symmetric? Skewed with a long tail? Bimodal — two humps, usually two populations wearing one label?

Plot it before you summarize it. Every summary statistic assumes you know the shape.

Centre. Mean is a balance point — one four-hour commute drags it. Median ignores how extreme the extremes are. Mode is the only centre for categories.

The gap between mean and median is information: a mean well above the median announces a right tail before you've plotted anything.

Variability is not noise around the real answer

Two classes both average 72%. One spans 70–74. The other spans 40–100.

These are utterly different courses. Spread is half the description, not an afterthought.

  • SD — roughly the typical distance from the mean, in the variable's own units
  • z-score — subtract the mean, divide by SD: position in your own distribution

That z-score move is about to reappear twice: as Cohen's d, and as a standardized beta. One idea, three costumes.

Effect size: the question significance doesn't answer

"The difference was statistically significant" doesn't say how big it was.

Cohen's d — the difference between two means, in SD units.

0.2 small · 0.5 medium · 0.8 large. Most published psychology: d = 0.2 to 0.6.

r and r² — for associations. η² — variance explained by a factor.

Benchmarks are conversation-starters, not verdicts. A "small" d = 0.2 on university retention, applied to every student in Ontario, is enormous.

Every effect size on this slide is the same fraction: signal over noise. How big the difference is, relative to how much people vary anyway.

Confidence intervals: how sure, not just how much

r = .32, 95% CI [.21, .43]  is knowledge.
r = .32, 95% CI [.02, .62]  is a shrug in APA format.

  • Width is precision. A wide interval means the study genuinely cannot tell you whether the effect is trivial or substantial.
  • Zero matters. An interval excluding zero is significance, read off the interval.
  • It brackets the population value, not the people. 95% CI ≠ 95% of participants.

Precision improves with √n: quadrupling your sample halves the interval. Which is why precision gets expensive fast.

Round 3 — be the whole class by yourself

Part 3 runs 10,000 studies of the same world. Two numbers come out.

  • Power at n = 20 is about 25%. In a world where the effect is definitely real, three quarters of these studies conclude "no effect."
  • Among the studies that reached significance, the mean r is .55 — nearly double the truth.

Significance filtering doesn't just lose real effects. It exaggerates the ones it keeps.

Reveal one at a time. The second number is the winner's curse, and they derived it — do not let it go by as a bullet.

Three things you now know from your own data

  1. A single non-significant result is nearly uninformative when power is low. "We found no effect" often means "we ran a study that couldn't find effects."
  2. Published effects run hot. Remember week 1: replications average about half the original effect size. You just explained why.
  3. This is why you do a power analysis before collecting data — and why Practical 3 asks for a literature-derived effect size, not a hopeful guess.

Part three

Two ways to be wrong

Cry wolf, or sleep through it

There IS an effectThere is NO effect
You say yesCorrect — powerType I — false positive
You say noType II — false negativeCorrect

Alpha (.05) = your tolerance for crying wolf. Power (aim ≥ .80) = your chance of catching a real wolf.

Power rises with effect size, sample size, and less noise. You just watched all three.

Which error is worse? It depends what's at stake

Criminal trials are engineered against false positives. "Beyond a reasonable doubt" accepts that some guilty people walk, because convicting the innocent is judged the worse error.

Airport security runs the other way — a thousand flagged water bottles rather than one missed threat. So does screening for treatable disease.

Your research has stakes too, quieter ones: a false positive sends a literature chasing a phantom; a false negative buries a real phenomenon.

Asking which error is the greater danger in your study is a sign of methodological adulthood. Practical 5 asks you directly.

You have been preregistering all term

Across the practicals you choose a question, define measures, name a manipulation, predict an effect size from literature, and specify your analysis — all before any data exist.

In modern research that document has a name.

  • It doesn't solve fraud. It solves flexibility in honest hands — the dozens of defensible choices made after seeing results.
  • The line it draws: confirmatory (you predicted it) vs exploratory (you noticed it). Both are legitimate. Dressing the second as the first is not.

In November you'll see exactly how much that flexibility is worth. Bring a strong stomach.
