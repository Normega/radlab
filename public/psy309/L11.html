Part one

One study is one study

Three kinds of "again"

  • Direct replication — same method, new participants. Tests one thing: was it real, or noise? This is the kind with teeth.
  • Conceptual replication — same hypothesis, different operationalizations. Success is strong construct-validity evidence. Failure is ambiguous — wrong theory, or wrong translation?
  • Replication-plus-extension — confirm the base camp, push one ridge further. The workhorse of normal science.

A literature full of "supportive" conceptual replications and zero direct ones can keep a weak finding alive indefinitely. Nobody ever re-checked the original.

What happened when psychology checked

100 published studies from top journals, directly replicated with original materials and decent power.

  • About a third produced a significant effect in the same direction.
  • Those that did were, on average, about half their original size.

Open Science Collaboration (2015), Science

You saw both numbers in week 1. In October you derived the second one yourselves — significance filtering exaggerates what it keeps.

Multi-lab projects filled in the texture: lab-to-lab variation is usually smaller than the gap between original and replication. So the shortfall isn't mostly context sensitivity. The originals ran hot.

Put the L6 winner's-curse histogram back up. The loop closing — week 1 quiz → October simulation → today's number — is the argument that this course was built as one thing.

Reading a failed replication

Calibration, not cynicism.

  • It doesn't prove the original false — but it moves the honest estimate toward zero.
  • "The replication must have been done wrong" is a hypothesis, not a rebuttal — especially when they used the original materials at triple the sample size.
  • Context sensitivity is real but must be argued: "the effect requires condition X" predicts a moderated pattern, which is testable.

The mature reading of any single exciting finding: interesting, pending replication.

Meta-analysis: the literature as a dataset

Treat each study as a data point. Extract every effect size, weight by precision, pool.

  • Gives an overall estimate with its own confidence interval
  • Measures heterogeneity — do these effects vary more than sampling error explains?
  • Chases that variance with moderator analyses — often the most informative table in the paper

The closest thing science has to seeing the whole web of evidence in one figure. Form opinions from reviews, not from single studies.

The file drawer

Meta-analysis can only pool what was published. And publication has a strong preference.

Significant, positive, novel results got in. Null results went into the drawer. Pool a literature filtered that way and the estimate is honestly computed and systematically inflated.

The case where we could open the drawer. Antidepressant trials registered with the FDA — where every trial legally exists on record.

Of the trials the FDA judged positive, nearly all were published. Of the negative ones, most were unpublished — or published spun as positive. The published literature said the effects were substantially larger than the full record did.

Turner et al. (2008), NEJM. Same drugs, same trials. The difference was which studies you were allowed to see.

So what did your p < .05 actually mean?

Three things to be clear about

  1. Nobody in this room cheated. Every move on that list is defensible in isolation, and every one appears in published papers.
  2. The problem is the bookkeeping, not the morals. Choices made after seeing the data detach the p-value from what it claims to mean.
  3. You now know the feeling from the inside — the small pull toward the analysis that worked.

That feeling is what a preregistration protects you from. And you're about to write one.

The names, and the antidotes

p-hacking — trying analytic paths until one crosses .05, then reporting that path as the only road contemplated.

HARKing — hypothesizing after results are known; writing an unexpected pattern up as the prediction all along.

The exploratory finding is fine. Laundering it into a confirmation is the crime.

Preregistration — locks the path before data exist

Open data and materials — flexibility can't hide when anyone can re-run it

Registered reports — acceptance before results; nothing to steer toward

Effect sizes and intervals instead of the p-threshold fetish

None of these requires anyone to become more virtuous. They re-engineer the situation so ordinary humans produce honest bookkeeping — the same philosophy as blinding.

Part two

Whose psychology is it?

WEIRD

Psychology's evidence base is built overwhelmingly on people from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic societies — and disproportionately on the strangest subpopulation within them: university undergraduates in psychology courses.

And on many measured dimensions, WEIRD people are not a neutral default. They're a global outlier. (Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan, 2010)

Not even perception is safe

The Müller-Lyer illusion — two equal lines, arrow-tails making one look longer — feels like raw hardware.

Its strength varies dramatically across societies: strong among people raised amid carpentered right angles, nearly absent in some other visual environments.

If line-length illusions are culturally tuned, the burden of proof shifts for everything above them.

"Human psychology" claims built on Ontario undergraduates are, until checked, claims about Ontario undergraduates.

Two modes — the distinction that resolves it

Generalization mode. The study exists to estimate a fact about a population. Representative sampling is the whole ballgame — the claim is "this holds out there."

Frequency claims live here permanently. So does anything feeding policy.

Theory-testing mode. The study exists to put a theory at risk: if mechanism M is real, effect E should appear under conditions C.

The sample needs to be a fair arena for the mechanism, not a miniature of humanity. The lab's artificiality is the feature.

Most unfair criticism — and unearned praise — comes from grading a study against the wrong mode's contract.

"Only 40 undergraduates in a lab!" is devastating for a prevalence estimate and often beside the point for a mechanism test.

The fine print on theory-testing mode

It licenses testing the theory on a narrow sample. It does not license quietly universalizing the result.

And if the theory itself claims universality, then whether the mechanism appears elsewhere becomes a theory-relevant prediction — so cross-population checks stop being courtesy and become the next test.

Modern journals increasingly ask for a constraints on generality statement: who, where, when this should hold. Your final paper's discussion is exactly that.
