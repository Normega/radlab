Part one

Two things that travel together

Plot it before you compute anything

Everything the correlation summarizes is visible in the scatterplot — and some things it hides are visible only there.

  • Positive — uphill drift. More commute, more fatigue.
  • Negative — downhill. More sleep, less irritability.
  • Zero — a shapeless cloud.

Plot first. Always. It's how you catch the pathologies before they catch you.

The deflator

What r = .30 actually buys you

Carry r² with you. It is the fastest way to keep a real finding in proportion — and the reason "explains X% of the variance" appears in every careful Results section.

Ask what fraction they expected before revealing the sliver. Most guess far higher. This slide is the antidote to both over-claiming and to disappointment at normal effect sizes.

Reading r

Sign = direction. Magnitude = strength. They are independent.

r = −.45 is stronger than r = +.20.

Students lose easy marks reading negative correlations as weak ones. Don't be in that scatterplot.

Calibration: ~.10 small · ~.30 medium · ~.50 large

Real published psychology lives mostly between .10 and .40.

r² — the deflator. A genuine, medium r = .30 explains 9% of the variance.

Interrogating an association

Three validities apply in force. The fourth waits by the door.

  • Construct — twice. Two measured variables, two sets of questions. And unreliability shrinks observed correlations: noise in either measure drags r toward zero.
  • Statistical. How strong? How precise? Outliers? Restriction of range? Linear at all?
  • External. An association in first-year volunteers is, so far, an association in people like that.
  • Internal — the moment the language drifts causal. "Linked to" becomes "may fuel" becomes "is driving," and now you owe direction and third variables.

Three ways a correlation lies to you

Outliers. In a small sample, one extreme point — extreme on both variables — grabs the line like a lever. Detectable exactly one way: looking.

Restriction of range. Select on one variable and you throw away the variation that carried the relationship. The r looks weak because of who you selected.

Nonlinearity. r sees straight lines only. Stress and performance rise, plateau, reverse — a story that can flatten to r ≈ 0 in the statistic while being obvious in the plot.

All three arguments end the same way: look at your data.

Exercise A · 10 minutes

Guess the correlation

guessthecorrelation.com

Five rounds, called out loud, before the reveal.

Most people systematically overestimate how strong a visible relationship is — which is exactly why r = .30 sounds disappointing when it's a perfectly normal finding.

End by putting a real published scatterplot on screen and asking for a guess. It will look like noise. It is r ≈ .35. That moment recalibrates the rest of the term.

Exercise B · 20 minutes

Spurious pairs

Six real correlations. For each: what third variable could produce this, without either causing the other?

Teams propose; class votes on the most plausible.

Check-in position 2. Do NOT warn them that two of the six have no third variable at all. That discovery is the exercise.

The six

  1. Ice cream sales & drowning deaths
  2. Shoe size & reading ability (children 4–12)
  3. Firefighters sent to a fire & damage caused
  4. National chocolate consumption & Nobel laureates per capita (r ≈ .79, ~20 countries — a real paper)
  5. US margarine consumption & the divorce rate in Maine (r ≈ .99)
  6. Films Nicolas Cage appeared in & swimming-pool drownings (r ≈ .67)

1–4 have a third variable — summer, age, fire size, national wealth. (#4 also: r = .79 on 20 points is far less impressive than it sounds.)

5 and 6 have nothing. Both series happen to drift; with tens of thousands of time series, near-perfect correlations appear by chance.

Don't accept the first laugh on 5 and 6. Push until someone says: nobody proposed a mechanism, they searched thousands of pairs until something lined up. Then name it — with enough comparisons, impressive correlations appear from nothing. That's the multiplicity problem, and it's the same mechanism as p-hacking in November.

Exercise C · 30 minutes · cloud R

Manufacture a confound

Build a world where A and B have nothing to do with each other — then watch a strong, significant correlation appear between them.

C <- rnorm(n)
A <- 0.65 * C + sqrt(1 - 0.65^2) * rnorm(n)
B <- 0.65 * C + sqrt(1 - 0.65^2) * rnorm(n)
# There is NO arrow from A to B. You just built it.

Open L4_confound.R in the cloud project.

Check-in position 3. Expected: r(A,B) ≈ .40, p < .001. Then lm(B ~ A + C) and A's estimate collapses to ~0, non-significant. Both on the fallback slide if the cloud misbehaves.

What happened

  • r(A, B) ≈ .40, p < .001. If you found this in real data, you'd write a paper.
  • Control for C: A's estimate collapses to about zero, non-significant.

The part that should worry you. Regression removed the confound because you knew to measure C.

In real correlational data, how would you know a C exists? And what could you do at the design stage — before any data — that handles every possible C at once, including the ones nobody has thought of?

Hold that question. The answer arrives in two weeks.

Part three

Three jobs a third variable can hold

The cheat sheet

RoleWhere it sitsQuestionModel
ModeratorBeside the relationshipWhen / for whom?Y ~ X * M
MediatorOn the path X → YWhy?Y ~ X + M
ConfoundOutside, driving bothIs it real at all?Y ~ X + C

Moderation = when. Mediation = why. Confound = controlling for.

The arrows are the whole game: moderators receive no arrow from X; mediators do; confounds send arrows to both and receive none.

Moderation: the relationship has boundaries

Social media use and anxiety: r = .35 in first-years, r = .05 in fourth-years.

Not a contradiction to be averaged away — a finding.

  • The test: can you honestly say "the relationship between A and B depends on M"?
  • Seen as: non-parallel slopes when you plot each group separately.
  • External validity, made quantitative — "does this generalize?" is "is population membership a moderator?"

Test enough subgroups and something will "moderate" by luck. A moderator predicted in advance is worth many discovered after the fact.

Mediation: the mechanism

Exercise predicts lower stress. Why? Perhaps exercise improves sleep, and sleep is what lowers stress.

Three paths:

c — total effect (X → Y)
a — X → M
b — M → Y, holding X constant
indirect = a × b · direct = c′

Don't control your mechanism away. Adding covariates is virtue against confounds and vandalism against mechanisms.

Whether a variable is off-path (control it) or on-path (analyse it) is a theory decision made before the model — not something the output announces.

Expect partial mediation. A mechanism carrying 40% of an effect is a win, not a failure to reach 100%.

When you can't experiment, recruit time

  • Cross-sectional — everyone measured once. A snapshot; every correlation in it is directionless.
  • Longitudinal — the same people, repeatedly. Now you can order events.
  • Cross-lagged panel — does phone use at T1 predict sleep problems at T2 more strongly than the reverse?

Autocorrelation is the elephant. Each variable's best predictor is itself, earlier. An analysis that doesn't hold each variable's own stability constant hands the cross-lags credit that belongs to persistence.

Time strengthens the causal case. Only random assignment closes it.

Test 1 prep · a worked short answer

Earn all five marks

Dr. Dowling surveys patients at all five inpatient mental-health facilities in her state (4,307 patients total; her sample is 1,369). She finds 27% (±3%) report having been arrested, 13% (±3%) convicted. A journalist suggests that with only ~4,000 patients she should have studied everyone for the study to really tell us something. How should she respond?

  • Mark 1: the sample is already very large — over 1,000 people and nearly a third of the population.
  • Mark 2: no reason to think it unrepresentative — more people wouldn’t make it more valid.
  • Mark 3: the margin of error is already small (±3%); a census barely narrows it.
  • Mark 4: we’re already confident of the ceiling — at most ~30% arrested, ~16% convicted.
  • Mark 5: so: dismiss the suggestion — it spends resources without changing the conclusion.

Notice what earned each mark: a named concept — sample size, representativeness, margin of error, confidence bound — applied to the numbers in the question. Not vibes. Sources.

Reveal one mark at a time and ask the room for the next one before showing it. This is the exact drill the short answers reward, on an item from a previous year's test.
