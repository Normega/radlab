Does background music improve studying?

Participants

60 students were recruited from a third-year psychology course. Students signed up through a shared online sheet listing two available sessions and chose whichever suited their timetable. The Tuesday 9 a.m. session became the music group (n = 31); the Thursday 4 p.m. session became the silence group (n = 29).

Procedure

The music group studied in the department’s study lounge, a comfortable room with sofas and natural light, with instrumental music playing. The silence group studied in a standard classroom in silence. Both groups were given the same textbook chapter and 30 minutes to study it.

Measure

After studying, all participants completed a 10-question multiple-choice quiz on the chapter, written by the researcher. Scores were high overall: the mean was 9.1 out of 10, and 22 participants scored a perfect 10.

Administration

The researcher, an undergraduate thesis student, personally ran both sessions, greeted participants, explained the study’s hypothesis that “music helps you focus,” and marked the quizzes.

Result

The music group scored significantly higher (M = 9.4) than the silence group (M = 8.8), p = .04. The researcher concludes that background music improves studying and recommends that the university install speakers in study spaces.

Your job — 20 minutes

The study has five planted faults, and a few bonus ones. For each problem you find:

  1. Name it precisely (the technical term).
  2. Say which validity it threatens.
  3. Say how you would fix it.

Scoring: 1 point per fault found, 2 if named precisely. “Something’s off about the groups” = 1. “Selection effect from self-selected sessions” = 2.
