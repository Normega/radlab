Liliana Study 3 — Analysis Plan (Consolidated Revision)

Version: consolidated draft, revision 2, 6 September 2026. Supersedes: the “Analysis Plan” doc as of 25 August 2026. Status: for Liliana’s review; not yet registered.

This revision merges the 25 August plan with the design review (liliana_study3_analysis_review.md) and the reproducible design specification (liliana_study3_design.md). Nothing has been removed. Section tags mark what moved: [KEPT] unchanged, [CHANGED] revised with the reason stated, [NEW] added.

Three structural changes are worth knowing before reading:

  1. Covariates, random effects, multiplicity and missing-data rules were repeated inside every hypothesis and had drifted apart. They are now stated once in §4 and referenced. Each hypothesis lists only what is specific to it.
  2. A derived-variables section (§3) now defines every computed quantity once, which settles an error where a within-person standardized variable was used as a between-group outcome.
  3. Exploratory aims are renumbered. The 25 August draft had two sections labelled E2 and a hypothesis labelled E5A under a heading marked E4.

1. Study design [CHANGED]

Two-phase randomized controlled trial, 31 nominal days, delivered online with one email-linked session per day.

Phase 1 (days 2–13). All participants rotate through three micro-interventions — Non-Reactivity, Reappraisal, and Self-Compassion — four consecutive exposures each. Block order is counterbalanced across participants using all six permutations, drawn at enrollment; order within a block is fixed.

Midpoint (days 14–16). Participants complete the assessment battery and rank all three interventions from #1 to #3. They are randomized to one of three conditions:

ConditionDatabase labelWhat they seePhase 2 intervention
Choice + Feedbackfeedback_choicePersonalized ranked summary of their own Phase 1 resultsFree choice
Choicecontrol_choiceControl display, no personal data Free choice
No-Choicecontrol_assignedControl display, identical to aboveAssigned 50/50 between the two non-rank-1 interventions

Phase 2 (days 17–28). Twelve daily sessions of the single intervention determined at the midpoint.

Final (days 29–31). Assessment battery and debrief.

Two corrections to the previous design summary. First, the third intervention is Self-Compassion, not Self-Appreciation. Second, and substantively: choice is free and independent of the stated ranking. Participants in both Choice conditions may select any of the three interventions, including their own rank-3, and this has been observed in testing. Describing Choice as “preferred” is therefore an assumption to be measured, not a design guarantee. Only No-Choice has a guaranteed relationship to preference — a guaranteed non-preferred one.

Calendar note. The assessment windows are catch windows, not waiting periods: Phase 2 begins the day after the midpoint is actually completed, so the study runs 29–31 days depending on the participant. Any calendar-indexed analysis must use the participant’s real study day, not the nominal day number.

2. Outcomes [CHANGED]

Primary. Perceived stress (BIPS) at the assessment level; raw delta_stress at the momentary level.

Secondary. Well-being (life satisfaction, SPANE-P, SPANE-N, PWB) and mental health (PHQ-8, GAD-7).

Cognitive-emotion mechanisms. Non-reactivity (MPoD-T); reappraisal (ERQ-CR, SRI-IP); self-compassion (SSCS-L).

Engagement. Self-reported effort (primary); objective engagement composite (secondary, see §4.7).

Instrument details: BIPS 9 items 1–5 sum; GAD-7 7 items 0–3 sum; PHQ-8 8 items 0–3 sum; SPANE 12 items 1–5 sum (P and N subscales of 6 each); PWB 8 items 1–7 sum; MPoD-T 15 items 1–7 mean; ERQ-CR 6 items 1–7 mean; SRI-IP 7 items 1–5 mean (item 7 reversed); SSCS-L 18 items 1–5 mean. Life satisfaction is a single-item 6-point scale, not a multi-item measure, and should be described as such.

The rule: no within-person standardized variable is ever used as a between-group outcome. Standardizing within person forces each participant’s mean to exactly zero, which removes precisely the between-person variance that Aims 2 and 3 test. A between-group comparison of such a variable is mathematically guaranteed to return null regardless of any real effect. z_delta_stress_P1 and composite_P1 exist to rank interventions within a person at the midpoint, and are confined to that role plus E1 and E6.

Where standardization is wanted for Phase 2 comparability, use delta_stress_rel_P1, which expresses Phase 2 relief relative to that person’s own Phase 1 baseline. It controls for individual differences in scale use while preserving between-person variance.

4. Analysis conventions [NEW]

These apply to every hypothesis unless a hypothesis overrides them explicitly.

4.1 Sample and exclusions

  • Test enrollments (is_test = true) are excluded from all analyses.
  • Participants failing Phase 1 adherence (<10 of 12 sessions) or not completing the midpoint within its window are withdrawn before the midpoint condition is drawn. This attrition therefore sits above the randomization step in the CONSORT diagram, and randomization balance holds only among those reaching the midpoint.
  • Aim 1 analyses use all participants with the relevant timepoints. Aims 2 and 3 are necessarily conditioned on Phase 1 completion.

4.2 Missing data and analysis population

  • Primary: intention-to-treat — all participants reaching Phase 2, analyzed by assigned condition, regardless of Phase 2 adherence.
  • Sensitivity: per-protocol — restricted to ≥10 of 12 Phase 2 sessions.
  • Mixed models retain partial cases under a missing-at-random assumption. MAR will be probed by testing whether missingness is predicted by condition or by prior-session stress; if it is, sensitivity analyses under alternative assumptions will be reported.

4.3 Covariates

Default set (all assessment-level models): age; gender; person of colour; stressful life events (Student Stress Scale total). Extended set (sensitivity): adds household income.

[DECIDED 6 Sep 2026] Race and ethnicity are coded as a single binary variable, person_of_colour, scoring 1 for any respondent selecting an identity other than White and 0 for a respondent selecting only White. The demographics instrument collects race and ethnicity as a multi-select over ten top-level categories with nested sub-options, which cannot enter a model as a factor at this sample size; collapsing to one binary contrast costs a single degree of freedom and is interpretable. Multi-select responses that include White alongside another identity score 1. Prefer-not-to-answer is coded missing rather than 0, so it does not silently join the White category.

Because the collapse reduces race to one degree of freedom, it moves into the default covariate set. Household income stays in the extended set: it has nine levels including prefer-not-to-answer, and its coding is still open (§10). Gender coding also remains open (§10) — it is likewise a multi-select, over nine options plus free text.

The default/extended split is deliberate. With roughly 100–200 participants, three conditions, a time factor and interactions, a long covariate list consumes more degrees of freedom than the design can afford.

Reporting note. The binary is an analytic simplification for statistical control, not a claim that the identities it collapses are interchangeable. The full multi-select distribution is reported descriptively in the sample-characteristics table, so nothing collected is hidden by the collapse.

Session-level models additionally include pre-session stress and sleep quality.

Aim 2 and Aim 3 models additionally include Phase 2 intervention (3 levels).

4.4 Random effects

Maximal justified structure, simplified in a fixed order on non-convergence.

  • Assessment-level models (2 timepoints): participant intercept.
  • Session-level models: participant intercept + random slope for session day.
  • H1D additionally: random slope for intervention type. Its variance component is itself a result — it estimates the extent to which different interventions suit different people, which is the study’s central premise and the assumption E1 depends on.
  • Simplification ladder: remove correlations between random terms; then the day slope; then the intervention slope. The final fitted structure will be reported.

4.5 Multiplicity

Holm–Bonferroni within family. Holm is uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni at the same family-wise error rate. Note it is a step-down procedure and stops at the first failure — every test below that point is non-significant regardless of its own p-value, so family membership is a substantive decision.

Families are defined within aim: the well-being family in Aim 1 is corrected separately from the well-being family in Aim 2. Exploratory analyses are reported uncorrected and labelled exploratory.

4.6 Time and session coding

  • Assessment models: Time is a two-level factor. Aim 1 uses baseline → midpoint. Aims 2 and 3 use midpoint → final.
  • Session models: session index 1–12 within phase, with Phase as a separate factor. This makes the Phase 1 and Phase 2 day effects directly comparable.
  • Calendar-indexed variables use the participant’s real study day, since study length varies 29–31 days.

4.7 Objective engagement composite

Self-reported effort is the pre-registered engagement outcome, but having chosen one’s intervention is exactly the sort of thing that biases self-report. The platform additionally records, per session: video watch percentage and completion; tab focus losses during video (count and duration); per-step time on task; session duration; and the presence and length of written reflections. A composite of these is pre-registered as a convergent secondary outcome wherever effort is tested (H2E, H3E). Composite construction will be fixed before unblinding and reported in full.

5. Aim 1 — Efficacy of the micro-interventions (Phase 1) [KEPT]

Aim. To understand the efficacy of three brief micro-interventions (four days each) on perceived stress, well-being, and cognitive-emotional processes.

RQ1. Do three brief micro-interventions improve perceived stress and well-being from baseline to the end of Phase 1, and do participants improve within individual sessions?

Design caveat, to be stated in limitations. Aim 1 is single-arm pre-post: every participant receives active intervention in Phase 1 and the study contains no waitlist or no-treatment control. H1A–C are therefore confounded with the passage of time, repeated assessment, and regression to the mean. This does not affect Aims 2 and 3, which are between-group.

H1A. Perceived stress will decrease from baseline to the end of Phase 1.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model
  • Outcome: BIPS
  • Predictor: Time (0 = baseline, 1 = midpoint)
  • Conventions: §4.3 default covariates, §4.4 assessment-level random effects

H1B. Well-being and mental health will improve from baseline to the end of Phase 1.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model, one per outcome
  • Outcomes: life satisfaction, SPANE-P, SPANE-N, PWB; PHQ-8, GAD-7
  • Predictor: Time (0 = baseline, 1 = midpoint)
  • Correction: Holm within family (well-being 4; clinical 2)

[CHANGED] Regression-to-the-mean handling for PHQ-8 and GAD-7. These two instruments are administered in the screener, which runs immediately before the baseline battery as one continuous Day-1 session — so they are genuine baseline measurements, and re-administering two-week-recall instruments minutes later was correctly rejected. However, eligibility is defined on them: max(GAD-7, PHQ-8) ≥ 10, GAD-7 ≤ 14, PHQ-8 ≤ 19. Participants are therefore admitted partly because at least one score was high on the day measured, and those scores carry measurement error, so they will drift toward the mean at re-measurement even under zero intervention effect. Two additions:

  • An expected-shift benchmark will be pre-registered, computed as approximately (1 − r) × (baseline deviation from the population mean), using published short-interval test–retest reliability for each instrument. Observed change is reported against that benchmark rather than against zero.
  • Binding-constraint split. Eligibility is a disjunction — only one score need reach 10. A participant with GAD-7 = 12 and PHQ-8 = 4 was not selected on PHQ-8, and no regression to the mean is expected there. Analyses are therefore split by which instrument was the binding constraint: regression to the mean predicts shrinkage on the binding score only, whereas a genuine treatment effect should appear on both.

Baseline distributions of both instruments are truncated at both ends by the eligibility rule and are non-normal by construction; residuals will be inspected rather than assumed.

H1C. Cognitive-emotion mechanisms will improve from baseline to the end of Phase 1.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model, one per outcome
  • Outcomes: MPoD-T, ERQ-CR, SRI-IP, SSCS-L
  • Predictor: Time (0 = baseline, 1 = midpoint)
  • Correction: Holm within the mechanisms family

H1D. Participants will show within-session reductions in perceived stress.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model
  • Outcome: delta_stress
  • Predictors: intervention type (3 levels); session day (1–12)
  • Covariates: pre-session stress; sleep quality
  • Random effects: participant intercept + random slope for session day + random slope for intervention type

[CHANGED] Pre-session stress is added because delta_stress is mechanically bounded by it — a participant starting at 1 on a six-point scale cannot show relief. The random slope for intervention type is added because the intercept-only specification assumes each intervention affects everyone identically, which contradicts both the study’s premise and E1. If a substantial share of sessions begin at the floor, an ordinal or censored alternative will be reported.

6. Aim 2 — Impact of choice (Phase 2) [CHANGED]

Aim. To determine whether practising a chosen intervention improves outcomes relative to being assigned a non-preferred one.

RQ2. Does choosing one’s intervention (with or without feedback) improve stress, well-being, mechanisms and engagement relative to being assigned a non-preferred one?

[DECIDED 6 Sep 2026] Aims 2 and 3 are estimated in a single model over all three conditions, using two pre-specified orthogonal contrasts. This replaces the two separate pooled two-group models in the 25 August draft. Same two questions, one model, no pooling decision, and better power because every participant contributes to both contrasts.

ContrastNo-ChoiceChoiceChoice + FeedbackEstimates
C1 — choice−1+1/2+1/2mean(Choice, Choice+Feedback) − No-Choice
C2 — feedback0−1+1Choice+Feedback − Choice

The two are orthogonal: (−1)(0) + (1/2)(−1) + (1/2)(+1) = 0.

Corrected 7 Sep 2026. Revision 2 published these as (−2/3, +1/3, +1/3) and (0, −1/2, +1/2). Those are orthogonal and give identical test statistics, p-values and power — contrast scaling cancels in the t-ratio — but the coefficients come out rescaled: C1 returned 2/3 of the intended group difference and C2 returned half of it. Since a pre-registration reports and interprets effect magnitudes, the weights above are the ones to use: they make each coefficient read directly as the difference between group means. Nothing else about the design or the analysis changes.

Model form for every assessment-level outcome:

Outcome ~ (C1 + C2) × Time + covariates + (1 | participant)

Aim 2 is the C1 × Time interaction; Aim 3 is the C2 × Time interaction. The H2 and H3 hypothesis labels below are retained, and each now names which contrast tests it. Both are read from the same fitted model, so the two aims are no longer separate analyses over overlapping subsets.

Three consequences worth stating explicitly:

  • “Among choosers” is now structural rather than a subsetting step. No-Choice carries weight 0 on C2, so the feedback contrast is by construction a comparison of the two choice conditions only. Aim 3 no longer requires restricting the sample, which was previously its own small source of analytic flexibility.
  • The pooling decision disappears. The 25 August draft pooled the two choice conditions for Aim 2, which assumed no feedback effect while Aim 3 tested for one. Orthogonal contrasts estimate both simultaneously without either assuming the other away.
  • Multiplicity is unchanged. Two contrasts on the same outcome are two tests, and the families in §4.5 apply to each contrast separately, exactly as they did to the two aims.

[CHANGED] One framing correction that the contrast coding does not fix. C1 estimates choice and preference-match together, not choice alone. No-Choice participants receive a non-rank-1 intervention by construction; choosers usually, though not always, receive one they wanted. The two differ on agency and on whether they got what they preferred. This is a property of the design, not of the model, so it must be described accordingly in the write-up. H2A-sens below is the partial remedy.

H2A. Choosers will show greater stress reduction than No-Choice participants.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model
  • Outcome: BIPS
  • Predictors: C1, C2, Time (0 = midpoint, 1 = final), and their interactions. Aim 2 is tested on C1 × Time.
  • Covariates: §4.3 default + Phase 2 intervention

H2A-sens. [NEW] Restricted to participants practising a non-rank-1 intervention.

  • Same model as H2A, restricted to participants whose Phase 2 intervention was not their stated rank #1
  • Purpose: holds preference-match roughly constant and isolates agency. Expected to be underpowered; reported as a pre-registered sensitivity analysis, not a confirmatory test.

This contrast exists only because choice is genuinely free (§1). The number of choosers selecting a non-rank-1 intervention is itself an informative descriptive result and will be reported whether or not the sensitivity analysis is interpretable.

H2B. Choosers will show greater improvement in well-being and mental health.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model, one per outcome
  • Outcomes: life satisfaction, SPANE-P, SPANE-N, PWB; PHQ-8, GAD-7
  • Predictors: C1, C2, Time (0 = midpoint, 1 = final), and their interactions. Aim 2 is tested on C1 × Time.
  • Covariates: §4.3 default + Phase 2 intervention
  • Correction: Holm within family (well-being 4; clinical 2)

The regression-to-the-mean concern in H1B does not apply here: all conditions were selected by the same rule, so it operates equally in each and cancels in the interaction.

H2C. Choosers will show greater improvement in cognitive-emotional mechanisms.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model, one per outcome
  • Outcomes: MPoD-T, ERQ-CR, SRI-IP, SSCS-L
  • Predictors: C1, C2, Time (0 = midpoint, 1 = final), and their interactions. Aim 2 is tested on C1 × Time.
  • Covariates: §4.3 default + Phase 2 intervention
  • Correction: Holm within the mechanisms family

H2D. Choosers will show larger within-session stress drops across Phase 2.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model

  • Predictors: C1, C2; session day (1–12 within Phase 2). Aim 2 is tested on C1.
  • Covariates: pre-session stress; sleep quality; Phase 2 intervention
  • Random effects: participant intercept + random slope for session day
  • Sensitivity: repeated with delta_stress_rel_P1

7. Aim 3 — Impact of feedback (Phase 2) [CHANGED]

Aim. To determine whether receiving personalized feedback before choosing improves outcomes relative to choosing without it, among participants who chose.

RQ3. Among choosers, does personalized feedback improve stress, well-being, mechanisms and engagement?

[DECIDED 6 Sep 2026] Aim 3 is the C2 × Time contrast in the single three-condition model defined in §6 — not a separate analysis on a restricted sample. Because No-Choice carries weight 0 on C2, “among choosers” is enforced by the contrast itself. The hypotheses below are therefore read from the same fitted models as Aim 2.

One exception. H3-MC is a logistic model on a binary participant-level outcome and genuinely is restricted to the two choice conditions — a participant with no choice has no choice to be congruent with. It is not part of the contrast model.

H3-MC. [MOVED FROM E2] Manipulation check — feedback will shift which intervention is chosen.

Participants who received feedback will be more likely to choose the intervention that reduced their stress most in Phase 1.

  • Analysis: logistic regression (Fisher’s exact if cell counts are small)
  • Outcome: congruent — Phase 2 intervention equals data_best (yes/no)
  • Predictor: Condition (Choice vs. Choice with Feedback)

This is tested and reported before H3A–E. Feedback can influence Phase 2 outcomes only by changing what is chosen, so without it a null on H3A–E cannot distinguish “feedback does not help” from “the manipulation did not take.” The test is possible because data_best is computed and stored for every participant including those who never saw it — the design deliberately banks that counterfactual.

H3-MC2 (secondary). Feedback will shift the stated ranking, not only the selection. The feedback group ranks after seeing their data; the Choice group ranks without it. Rank concordance with data_best is compared across the two conditions.

H3A. Among choosers, those who received feedback will show greater stress reduction.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model
  • Outcome: BIPS
  • Predictors: C1, C2, Time (0 = midpoint, 1 = final), and their interactions. Aim 3 is tested on C2 × Time.
  • Covariates: §4.3 default + Phase 2 intervention

H3B. Among choosers, those who received feedback will show greater well-being and mental-health improvement.

  • Outcomes: life satisfaction, SPANE-P, SPANE-N, PWB; PHQ-8, GAD-7
  • Predictors and covariates as H3A (tested on C2 × Time)
  • Correction: Holm within family (well-being 4; clinical 2)

H3C. Among choosers, those who received feedback will show greater mechanism improvement.

H3D. Among choosers, those who received feedback will show larger within-session stress drops.

  • Outcome: delta_stress (raw); sensitivity with delta_stress_rel_P1
  • Predictors: C1, C2; session day (1–12 within Phase 2). Aim 3 is tested on C2.
  • Covariates: pre-session stress; sleep quality; Phase 2 intervention

8. Exploratory aims [RENUMBERED]

All exploratory analyses are reported uncorrected and labelled exploratory.

E1. Machine-learning prediction of the best-fitting intervention [KEPT, with additions]

To determine whether baseline characteristics alone can predict which micro-intervention works best for a given participant.

E1A. Baseline characteristics predict which intervention produced the greatest within-session stress reduction.

  • Analysis: supervised 3-class classification (e.g. random forest), nested cross-validation
  • Label: intervention with highest mean Phase 1 delta_stress
  • Features: reduced pre-specified set (see below)
  • Report: cross-validated accuracy and Cohen’s κ, against both chance (0.33) and the label-noise ceiling from E6; feature importance (SHAP or permutation)

E1B. The same pipeline with the label defined by composite_P1, compared against E1A to see which definition of “best” baseline traits recover more accurately.

[NEW] Three specification changes. The original feature list — baseline demographics plus twelve scales plus BFI-2 facets — is on the order of 40–60 predictors before demographic dummy coding, against perhaps 100–200 Phase 1 completers, predicting a three-class label that is itself substantially noisy. Accordingly: (i) a reduced feature set is pre-specified before unblinding; (ii) nested cross-validation is used so reported accuracy is not optimistically biased by tuning; and (iii) results are interpreted against the E6 ceiling. A null result is a likely outcome under these constraints and should not be read as evidence against personalized matching — it may simply reflect that the label is too noisy to learn from at this sample size.

E2. Efficacy differences by intervention type [KEPT, extended]

Whether the three micro-interventions produce comparable within-session stress reductions.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model
  • Outcome: delta_stress
  • Predictor: intervention type (3 levels)
  • Covariates: pre-session stress; sleep quality; session day
  • Random effects: participant intercept + random slope for intervention type

[NEW] Run in Phase 1 and Phase 2 separately. Phase 1 is the clean comparison — within-person and counterbalanced. Phase 2 is confounded with condition, since choosers self-select, but the comparison is still worth reporting because it bears directly on how much of any Aim 2 or Aim 3 effect is attributable to intervention efficacy rather than to choice or feedback. The condition × Phase 2 intervention crosstab is reported alongside.

E3. Intervention-specific mechanism change [NEW]

Each intervention targets a specific mechanism with a matched instrument: Non-Reactivity → MPoD-T (particularly its Non-Reactivity subscale); Reappraisal → ERQ-CR and SRI-IP; Self-Compassion → SSCS-L. Aims 1–3 test whether mechanisms improve in general; this tests whether practising intervention X specifically improves mechanism X.

  • Analysis: linear mixed model, one per mechanism
  • Outcome: mechanism score
  • Predictors: Phase 2 intervention (3 levels); Time (midpoint, final); Intervention × Time
  • Prediction: the interaction is carried by the matched intervention
  • Secondary: repeated at subscale level (MPoD-T Non-Reactivity; SSCS-L facets)

Phase 2 is the ideal setting — twelve consecutive days of a single intervention. This also provides the only manipulation check on the interventions themselves, which the plan otherwise lacks.

E4. Baseline-dependent outcomes [KEPT, respecified]

Whether participants who begin Phase 1 worse off improve more.

  • E4A stress: outcome BIPS at midpoint, predictor baseline BIPS
  • E4B well-being and mental health, each with its own baseline as predictor
  • E4C mechanisms, each with its own baseline as predictor
  • Covariates: §4.3 default
  • Correction: Holm within each family

[CHANGED] Regression-to-the-mean specification. As originally written these analyses are close to a direct test of regression to the mean: under pure measurement error, baseline is negatively correlated with change, and an ANCOVA coefficient below 1 is regression to the mean rather than evidence of differential response. A method built for the question — Oldham’s method, or an explicitly reliability-corrected slope — will be used, and the expected result under the null stated in advance. For PHQ-8 and GAD-7 this compounds with the selection effect in H1B, and E4B for those two outcomes is reported as descriptive only.

E5. Trait negative emotionality as a moderator [REPAIRED]

Whether change over Phase 1 depends on trait negative emotionality (BFI-2-S Negative Emotionality domain, 6 items).

  • Analysis: linear mixed model, one per outcome family
  • Outcomes: BIPS; well-being set; mechanism set
  • Predictors: Time; Negative Emotionality (continuous, centred); Time × Negative Emotionality
  • Covariates: §4.3 default

Direction. The 25 August draft stated this as “will experience greater improve more or less,” which specifies no direction. A direction should be committed to before registration, or the aim explicitly labelled two-sided and exploratory. Recommended: two-sided and exploratory, since plausible arguments run both ways — higher negative emotionality means more room to improve, but also predicts poorer engagement.

E6. Robustness of the intervention-ranking metric [NEW]

To quantify how much the choice of quality metric, and the small number of sessions per intervention, affect which intervention is identified as a participant’s best — and what that implies for the feedback manipulation and for E1.

Background. This was originally scoped as a pre-launch decision (“metric bake-off”) to choose between two metrics before recruiting. It is now exploratory, for three reasons: the shipped metric (mean delta_stress) is pre-specified in the methods document and is what participants are shown, so changing it after seeing data would alter the manipulation rather than merely the analysis; a pretest would not be powered to distinguish the metrics; and the midpoint snapshot already stores both composites for every participant in every condition, so the comparison is recoverable at full sample size afterwards.

E6A. Metric disagreement. The proportion of participants for whom the two metrics identify a different top intervention, with 95% CI; Kendall’s τ between the two full rankings per participant; both stratified by whether any intervention was flagged as resting on fewer than two usable sessions.

Because within-person z-scoring is a monotone transform, ranking by mean z(delta_stress) is identical to ranking by mean delta_stress. The composite metric is therefore not a different measure but the same one plus an appraisal term at equal weight, and the two disagree on a pair exactly when the standardized appraisal gap outweighs and opposes the standardized stress gap. For approximately bivariate-normal gaps with correlation ρ and relative scale λ:

P(disagree) = ½ − arcsin(r)/π,   r = (1 + ρλ) / √(1 + 2ρλ + λ²)

Even under a strong correlation between how much an intervention relieves stress and how much it is liked, roughly one participant in ten would be told a different intervention is their strongest under the alternative metric. E6A estimates ρ and λ from the observed data and reports the realised rate against this benchmark.

E6B. Ranking stability. Leave-one-session-out resampling within participant: the proportion whose top-ranked intervention changes when any single session is dropped, plus the observed within-person session-level SD of delta_stress and the observed between-intervention separation — the two quantities that determine stability. Reported against rank_gap and the low-data flag.

E6C. Label-noise ceiling for E1. The maximum attainable classification accuracy implied by the E6B instability estimate, against which E1A and E1B accuracy are reported. E1 predicts a label that is itself estimated with error; if the ranking recovers the true best intervention only about 60% of the time, no classifier can exceed roughly 60% accuracy against ground truth however good its features. Reporting E1 against a 33% chance baseline alone risks reading a null as “baseline traits do not predict fit” when the correct reading may be “the label is too noisy to learn at this sample size.”

Implication for Aim 3, stated as a limitation rather than tested. E6A and E6B together characterize how much of “your strongest practice” is signal. This does not threaten Aim 3’s primary contrast — the manipulation is receiving personalized feedback, and the feedback is honestly the participant’s own data whatever its precision. It does attenuate any mediational claim that feedback helped because participants chose a better-matched intervention, since that mediator is measured with substantial error. rank_gap is therefore pre-registered as a moderator of the feedback effect: if feedback works through match quality, its effect should be larger for participants whose ranking was clear-cut.

E7. Order and recency effects in Phase 1 [NEW]

Phase 1 block order is counterbalanced across all six permutations and recorded, but was previously unanalyzed. Two questions:

  • E7A. Do outcomes differ by block position? Intervention practised first has twelve more days of elapsed time before the midpoint than the one practised last.
  • E7B. Is the most recently practised intervention over-represented at rank #1 — in the stated preference ranking, in the computed delta_stress ranking, or in both? Tested against the uniform expectation implied by counterbalancing.

E7B is a validity check on the feedback manipulation: if recency drives the ranking, then what is fed back is partly an artefact of order rather than of fit. Because order is randomized, this is cleanly testable.
