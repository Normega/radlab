# Standardize on stacked pages, freeze running studies

Your two renderers differ much less than the size of the drift suggests. In the survey-methods literature, the format that reliably harms data quality is the **grid with a shared header**. One item per screen and stacked items that each carry their own labels are both "item-by-item" formats, and on PCs and phones alike they behave much more like each other than like a grid. Between those two formats the trade-off is real but modest. One item per screen gives the least straightlining and the fewest accidental skips. Stacked pages are roughly 30–50% faster, show equal or lower breakoff in long surveys, and in the largest head-to-head experiment produced the most stable substantive estimates. Mean scale scores barely move across either format. What does move is the *covariance structure*: inter-item correlations, alpha and susceptibility to context effects. That matters most for the lab's scale-development work and for any pooling of instruments across studies. The recommendation is to standardize on the newer stacked-card renderer for new studies, with a few specific settings: Next only at the foot of the page, an answer or explicit "Prefer not to answer" required before advancing, fixed published order for validated instruments, and per-answer timestamps. Leave every in-progress study, especially the multi-week longitudinal ones, on the legacy renderer until it closes. Record the presentation format on every response row so the drift can be modelled rather than guessed at. The evidence is thinnest exactly where the lab sits. No study has compared these two formats on Prolific or SONA samples, and none has run a full invariance test of a psychological scale across them, so several conclusions below are inferences from attitude-battery experiments rather than direct tests.

## The real dividing line is the grid, and neither renderer uses one

One terminology warning applies to everything that follows. "Item-by-item" means one item per page in some papers (Couper 2001, Tourangeau 2004, Toepoel 2009, Roßmann 2018, DeBell 2021). In others (Mavletova 2018, Revilla 2017, Vehovar's "scrolling" arm) it means items stacked on one page, each with its own response options. That second meaning is exactly the lab's composable renderer. Many headline "grid vs. item-by-item" results are therefore evidence *for* the stacked card format, not against it.

The case against grids is consistent across studies. Mavletova, Couper and Lebedev crossed format with device in a two-wave Russian panel (N = 1,678 and 1,079). In their design the "item-by-item" arm was a stacked scrolling page. Grids raised straightlining (negative-binomial **OR 1.34 and 1.38**) and lowered concurrent validity on both PC and phone, with correlations such as **.47 vs .60 and .43 vs .57** ([ESRA 2017 slides](https://hse.ru/data/2017/10/15/1159283029/Mavletova%20et%20al_Grids_item-by-item_ESRA_2017.pdf); [abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317735307)). Vehovar, Couper and Čehovin randomized 4,644 respondents to a grid, a stacked scrolling layout with options "repeated for each item", an accordion, horizontal scrolling, or one item per page. They found the grid had no speed advantage over stacked layouts and performed worse on straightlining and nonresponse. Their conclusion was that grids "can be safely replaced by scrolling or unfolding" layouts "not only on mobile devices but also on PCs" ([Vehovar et al. 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)). On unadapted phone matrices, Liu and Cernat measured item nonresponse of **29.9% vs 2.8%** for a stacked page at five response options ([Liu & Cernat 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0894439316674459)). Toepoel's 40-item experiment ran its multi-item arms with either one shared header or labels repeated at every item. It found "no differences" between the two and pooled them ([Toepoel et al. 2005 working paper](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download)). The lab's decision to give every card its own labels therefore avoids the grid's known harms, but it does not by itself remove the effects of having several items on screen together.

That distinction matters because the "near means related" heuristic belongs to *co-presence on a screen*, not to the shared header. Tourangeau, Couper and Conrad found inter-item correlations "highest when they were all on the same screen" ([POQ abstract](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/68/3/368/1846396)). Their eight-item battery's alpha was **.62 in one grid, .56 on two screens, and .51 one item per screen** ([Callegaro et al. 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)). In Neuert, Roßmann and Silber's eye-tracking lab study, **16–29% of respondents went back and changed an earlier answer** after reading later items on the same page ([JOS 2023](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/jos-2023-0004.pdf)). Stacked cards presumably invite the same look-around, though probably less than a grid. No study reports a separate correlation estimate for the stacked-with-labels arm, so that remains an inference.

## Between the lab's two formats, the trade is speed against differentiation

Set grids aside and compare one item per screen with stacked pages directly. The two formats split the data-quality outcomes between them, and none of the differences is large.

| Outcome | One item per screen (legacy) | Stacked cards, several per page (composable) | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Completion time | Slowest: 29.1 vs 19.6 min median (Vehovar); ~30% longer (Lozar Manfreda) | Fastest of the non-grid formats | Strong, consistent |
| Straightlining | Lowest; ~30% better than average on PC (Vehovar) | Slightly more than paging, much less than grids | Moderate; one large direct test |
| Item nonresponse (optional answers) | Lowest; 10% of respondents skip ≥1 item at 1/screen vs 19% at 40/screen (Toepoel) | Rises with items per page; closes when answers are prompted | Strong gradient, design-dependent |
| Breakoff | Worse in long surveys (+9% PC, +25% phone vs average, Vehovar) | Equal or slightly better | Mostly null on short surveys |
| Alpha / inter-item correlation | Equal or lower | Equal or slightly higher; largest for short, uniformly keyed sets | Inconsistent, small |
| Substantive estimates | 5 of 40 significant deviations from the average (Vehovar) | 0 of 40 (Vehovar) | One large study |
| Context / priming effects | Priming from a preceding item appears only here (Stefkovics & Kmetty) | Proximity assimilation instead | One preregistered study |
| Respondent ratings | Higher on PC (Toepoel layout rating 7.67 → 7.06 as items/screen rose; Thorndike preference) | Higher on phones (Mavletova & Couper 2014) | Device-dependent |

The time evidence is the most robust. Lozar Manfreda and colleagues measured a one-question-per-page design at **466 s vs 368 s** for a scrolling page ([JCMC 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/7/3/JCMC731/4584218)). Vehovar's paging arm took about 50% longer than the non-paging layouts and broke off more often as a result ([Vehovar et al. 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08944393221132644)). For a lab that pays Prolific participants by the minute, that cost compounds across every instrument.

Item nonresponse is the clearest point in favour of one item per screen, but it depends on design rather than being fixed to the format. In Toepoel's probability panel, the share of respondents with any missing item rose from **.10 at one per screen to .19 at forty** ([Toepoel WP](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/bitstreams/9c502704-b5b1-4318-b73f-ef64926b50e0/download)). Nosek, Sriram and Umansky found missing data roughly halved in their one-at-a-time condition, **13.8% vs 7.2%**, with equal attrition and essentially equal criterion validity ([PLoS ONE 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3355147/)). Nosek's one-at-a-time interface, however, required an explicit "Decline to answer" click to skip. Mavletova made answers obligatory and Vehovar used soft reminders, and in both the gap largely disappears. A follow-up of Vehovar's work still found higher nonresponse on scrolling pages than on paging (p < .001), but that result exists only in [conference slides](https://www.europeansurveyresearch.org/conf2023/uploads/320/2081/4/ESRA2023_cehovin_final.pdf). The composable renderer already has the mechanism that closed the gap in Nosek's design: a per-item "Prefer not to answer". Making every card require either an answer or that explicit decline before Next activates turns accidental skips into recorded choices.

The alpha evidence is genuinely mixed, and it is a poor yardstick in any case. Roßmann, Gummer and Silber found grid alpha higher than single-item pages (**.60 vs .41**). In the same grid condition, straightlining was **10% vs 4%** and "don't know" answers **6% vs 1%** ([JSSAM 2018](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/6/3/376/4562352)), so the higher alpha came with worse responding. With long or mixed-keyed scales the difference disappears or reverses. Toepoel's 40-item scale had alpha **.880 to .887** across 1, 4, 10 and 40 per screen. Nosek's Moral Foundations subscales were *higher* one at a time (**.705 vs .679**). Peytchev's SEM reanalysis of Tourangeau's data found validity coefficients rising from **0.82 to 0.94** as items were separated onto different screens ([Callegaro et al. 2009](http://www.asasrms.org/Proceedings/y2009/Files/400045.pdf)). Taken together, a format that raises alpha partly by making answers more alike has not improved measurement. When comparing formats, validity coefficients and reverse-keyed item behaviour are the outcomes to trust.

The least appreciated finding is that each format carries its *own* context artifact. In Stefkovics and Kmetty's preregistered experiment, a priming item shifted the target attitude only when items appeared on separate pages (B = 0.38 vs −0.00 in a grid) ([MISS 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42409-022-00036-z)). Co-displayed items absorb context as proximity correlation; one-per-screen items absorb it as sequential priming. Neither format is context-free. Switching between them changes *which* artifact a dataset carries, and this is the mechanism by which the lab's drift could matter even when means agree.

## Phones raise breakoff for every format, and navigation details matter more than layout

Many participants complete on phones, and phones cost about **1.5–2× the breakoff of PCs regardless of format**. The figures are 15.2% vs 10.0% in Mavletova's panel ([slides](https://hse.ru/data/2017/10/15/1159283029/Mavletova%20et%20al_Grids_item-by-item_ESRA_2017.pdf)), 16% vs 10% in Statistics Netherlands' 2025 experiment ([CBS 2025](https://www.cbs.nl/-/media/_pdf/2025/42/experiment-smartphone-first-vragenlijstopmaak.pdf)), and about 13% vs 5% averaged over 13 studies ([via Clement et al. 2020](https://surveyinsights.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/device-effects-on-survey-response-quality.pdf)). Under random assignment to device, however, measurement quality is close to equal. Antoun, Couper and Conrad's crossover in the LISS probability panel found phone respondents "at least as likely to provide conscientious and thoughtful answers" ([Antoun et al. 2017](https://api.isr.umich.edu/?p=215521)). Tourangeau and colleagues found "few effects of the type of device on data quality" ([SSCR 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317719438)). Device gaps in observational data mostly reflect who chooses phones ([Lugtig & Toepoel 2016](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/13552/1/Lugtig%20and%20Toepoel%20(prepublication).pdf)).

On phones, the stacked card layout *is* the standard mobile item-by-item format. Every responsive-design grid study that collapses rows into cards finds less straightlining and missing data than the desktop grid ([Richards et al. 2016](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/2810.pdf)). Scrolling pages on mobile were significantly faster than paging, with non-significantly lower breakoff and higher respondent ratings ([Mavletova & Couper 2014](https://publications.hse.ru/en/view/100571784)). Moving from 5 to 30 items per page cut mobile breakoffs by nearly a third (n.s.) but raised item nonresponse ([Mavletova & Couper 2016](https://publications.hse.ru/en/view/133861417)). Most of the extra time phones take is within-page scrolling ([Couper & Peterson 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439316629932)).

Two navigation details outweigh the layout choice itself. First, in Revilla and Couper's seven-group experiment, "the most striking difference" was that an always-visible Next button on stacked phone pages made item missing data "substantially higher" ([Revilla & Couper 2018](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0894439317715626)). CBS forces respondents to scroll to the bottom for the same reason ([CBS 2025](https://www.cbs.nl/-/media/_pdf/2025/42/experiment-smartphone-first-vragenlijstopmaak.pdf)). The composable renderer's Next must sit only at the foot of the page, never in a sticky footer. Second, auto-advance helps less on phones than its reputation suggests. It saved time on PCs and tablets but **not on smartphones** in Selkälä and Couper's experiments ([U. Lapland record](https://research.ulapland.fi/fi/publications/automatic-versus-manual-forwarding-in-web-surveys/)). It cut answer changes by about 45–48%, and **more than a third** of auto-advance users wrongly believed they could not revise answers ([Giroux et al. 2019](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/6381-impacts-of-implementing-an-automatic-advancement-feature-in-mobile-and-web-surveys)). Hays and colleagues found no cost of auto-advance to missing data, reliability or means, and recommended pairing it with a Back option, which reduced sub-2-second responders ([Hays et al. 2010](https://erasmusmc.mijn.bsl.nl/the-impact-of-next-and-back-buttons-on-time-to-complete-and-meas/271924)). Auto-advance on multi-item pages misbehaves: it generated extra unnecessary Next clicks in Statistics Netherlands' trial ([Bakker et al. 2022](https://majournals.bib.uni-mannheim.de/mda/article/download/2021.10/312)). The coherent pairings are therefore one item per screen with auto-advance plus Back, or stacked cards with a manual Next. The lab's two renderers already follow those pairings, which is a reason not to build a hybrid.

Device-level measurement equivalence mostly holds, with two caveats relevant to a unified design. Menold and Toepoel's randomized experiment (N = 5,077) found "full data comparability for devices and formats" except visual analogue scales. They also found lower reliability on phones and with 5-point scales, and recommended "a unified design for mixed-device surveys" ([SMR 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/00491241221077237)). Long horizontal scales (9–11 points) in matrices shift answers leftward on phones ([Liu & Cernat 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0894439316674459)). Both point to one rule: render the same vertically stacked option list on every device, which the composable renderer already does on phones. The newest work finds that mobile-first layouts applied to everyone reduce item nonresponse without raising breakoff or satisficing ([Schwerdtfeger et al. 2026](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/14707853261483760)).

## Norms survive a format change; covariance structure is less certain

For validated instruments whose norms come from paper, the reassuring news is substantial. Faithful paper-to-electronic migration leaves scores nearly unchanged. Gwaltney, Shields and Shiffman found mean differences averaging **0.2% of the scale range** across 278 scales, with a weighted cross-mode correlation of **0.90** ([Gwaltney et al. 2008](https://www.ispor.org/publications/journals/value-in-health/abstract/Volume-11--Issue-2/Equivalence-of-Electronic-and-Paper-and-Pencil-Administration-of-Patient-Reported-Outcome-Measures--A-Meta-Analytic-Review)). Muehlhausen and colleagues pooled a standardized mean difference of **0.037** across 307 studies ([HQLO 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4597451/)). The ISPOR ePRO task force classes "going from multiple items per page to one item per screen" as a **minor** modification needing only cognitive interviewing and usability testing. It classes "changing the order of item presentation" and requiring a scroll bar to see an item's text or options as **moderate** changes that call for a quantitative equivalence study (Coons et al. 2009, quoted in [Muehlhausen et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4597451/)). ISPOR's 2023 update says further comparability testing is unnecessary when evidence exists and faithful migration is followed ([O'Donohoe et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2023.01.001)).

A paper questionnaire is itself a multi-item page with every item labelled. The stacked card format is therefore arguably the *more* faithful migration of paper-normed instruments, and one item per screen is the larger (if still "minor") departure. The caveat is that this literature measures means and ICCs, not factor structure. It is silent on exactly the covariance effects where the survey-methods literature finds format sensitivity. Item order is the bigger risk for normed instruments. Item-blocking the SCL-90-R changed its means, reliability and validity ([Franke 1997](https://doi.org/10.1027/1015-5759.13.2.67)). Randomizing the Personal Wellbeing Index lowered means ([Weinberg et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-016-9822-1)). Reordering questions raised bullying prevalence by **45%** ([Huang & Cornell 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164415622664)). Personality inventories, by contrast, were unaffected by randomization ([Schell & Oswald 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886913001281); [Buchanan et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41237-018-0055-y)). For clinical and wellbeing instruments, the published order is part of the instrument.

The scale-development study is well designed on this score, with one obligation attached. Grouping same-facet items inflates alpha through positive lag-1 dependency between consecutive answers ([Shimada & Katahira 2023](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-022-01943-z)). Per-participant facet-interleaved randomization avoids building that into the EFA. It also spreads page co-presence evenly: by simple arithmetic, any two of the 32 items share an 8-item page for roughly 7/31 ≈ 23% of participants, so proximity correlation becomes diffuse noise rather than a spurious facet. Format effects also carry forward: a grid raised inter-item correlations in the *following* block of questions too ([Silber et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/13645579.2017.1381478)). The obligation is that the CFA sample must run under exactly the same regime as the EFA sample: same renderer, items per page, randomization scheme and attention checks. Otherwise a CFA misfit cannot be separated from a format change. The resulting norms will belong to that regime. Liu and Cernat and Mavletova found formal non-equivalence between formats that grew with response-scale length, though only for grids versus stacked items ([Liu & Cernat](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439316674459); [Mavletova et al.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439317735307)). Formal invariance failures can coexist with trivial practical differences: the CAPE failed metric invariance online vs paper with a mean SIDS of only −0.11 ([Vleeschouwer et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0084011)). Anyone later administering the new scale one item per screen should treat that as a change needing an invariance check, not assume equivalence.

The *Standards* put the burden of documentation on the lab. Test specifications must define "the item formats … and the ordering of items and sections" (4.2). Directions must let others "replicate the administration conditions under which the data on reliability, validity, and … norms were obtained" (4.15). Permitted variations must be identified and justified (4.5). And "those who allow lack of standardization are responsible for providing evidence that [it] did not affect … the comparability of the scores" ([AERA/APA/NCME 2014](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)). The ITC guidelines add that computerized versions should preserve "equivalent test-taker control (such as the ability to skip or review items)" ([ITC CBT Guidelines](https://www.intestcom.org/files/guideline_computer_based_testing.pdf)). That requirement favours keeping Back available and keeping the decline option explicit.

## A concrete standard: what to adopt, what to freeze, what to report

**Adopt the composable stacked-card renderer as the platform default for new studies.** It is faster, has equal or lower breakoff, sits closest to how paper norms were collected, and is the layout mobile-first guidance converges on ([Antoun et al. 2018](https://www.popcenter.umd.edu/mprc-associates/antoun/christopher-antoun-publications/articlereference.2019-03-29.9422701861); [Pew 2015](https://www.pewresearch.org/methods/2015/06/11/tips-for-creating-web-surveys-for-completion-on-a-mobile-device/)). Most importantly, the scale-development study's norms are being built in it. Five settings carry most of the benefit. First, keep Next only at the foot of the page. Second, require an answer or an explicit "Prefer not to answer" on every card before Next activates, highlighting any card still unanswered. Third, keep pages at about 5–10 items; the current 8 is fine, since Grady's grid experiments found dropout rising with rows per page and nothing supports changing it ([Grady et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439318773733)). Fourth, keep Back available. Fifth, log a server-side timestamp for every answer event, including changed answers, which the platform's append-only response rule already preserves. For validated instruments, use the published item order, never randomize, keep reverse-keyed items where the authors placed them, and break pages at instrument boundaries so two instruments never share a page. Split long instruments into roughly equal chunks rather than leaving a stray single item on the last page, and never split an item from its options. A progress bar should not be added as a retention device. Constant-speed bars did not reduce breakoff across 32 experiments, and they *increased* it where a small incentive was promised ([Villar et al. 2013](https://research.google/pubs/where-am-i-a-meta-analysis-of-experiments-on-the-effects-of-progress-indicators-for-web-surveys/)), which describes Prolific and course-credit samples.

**Do not change anything in a running study.** A format switch in a multi-week longitudinal study confounds within-person change with an administration change, and within-person change is the effect those studies exist to measure. Keep the legacy renderer frozen for every in-progress study and any planned extension or replication meant to be compared with it. Retire it only when the last such study closes. If a switch is ever unavoidable, for example a blocking bug, make it at a wave boundary for all participants at once, never per participant, and model format as a covariate. Whatever the timing, add a presentation-format or renderer identifier to every response row now. This is the same "record where it came from" principle the platform already applies to schedule IDs, and it turns any future cross-study pooling of an instrument from guesswork into a moderator analysis.

**Recalibrate speeder screens by format; do not port one cutoff across both.** The standard 2-seconds-per-item page-time index was developed and validated on multi-item pages with manual navigation ([Huang et al. 2012, via Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.784375/full); [Bowling et al. 2023](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356814910_The_Quick_and_the_Careless_The_Construct_Validity_of_Page_Time_as_a_Measure_of_Insufficient_Effort_Responding_to_Surveys)). It fits the composable renderer directly: page time divided by items on the page. Legacy auto-advance latencies exclude the roughly 2 seconds per item a Next click adds ([Hays et al. 2010](https://erasmusmc.mijn.bsl.nl/the-impact-of-next-and-back-buttons-on-time-to-complete-and-meas/271924)), so the same cutoff applied per item there flags more people than its validation supports. For legacy data, apply the rule to the instrument's summed time divided by item count. Alternatively, use a word-count threshold such as Zhang and Conrad's 300 ms per word ([SRM 2014](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/5453/5344)). Speeding is a stable trait concentrated among younger respondents: over 40% of 18–34-year-olds were persistent speeders in LISS, versus under 5% of those 65 and over. Student pools are therefore the high-risk sample whichever format is used. Platform choice outweighs format here. Prolific and CloudResearch beat MTurk, Qualtrics panels and SONA on attention and reading time ([Douglas et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0279720); [Peer et al. 2022](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/data-quality-of-platforms-and-panels-for-online-behavioral-resear-2/)).

**Report the administration format in every methods section** as a specification, not an adjective. The table below lists what the Standards (1.10, 4.2, 4.15) imply a reader needs to judge comparability.

| Report | Example wording |
|---|---|
| Items per page and page breaks | "8 items per page across 4 pages; page breaks fixed" |
| Item order | "Published order" or "randomized per participant, facets interleaved (scheme in supplement)" |
| Response-option layout | "Each item displayed with its own fully labelled 5-point options, vertical on phones; no shared header" |
| Navigation | "Manual Next at page foot; Back available" or "auto-advance on selection; Back available" |
| Missing-data handling | "Answer or 'Prefer not to answer' required before advancing; PNA treated as missing" |
| Device mix | Share of phone / PC completions, and whether results were checked by device |
| Timing screen | Rule, unit (per page vs per item) and cutoff |
| Deviation from the validated administration | e.g., "normed on paper; administered as stacked web items (an ISPOR 'minor' modification)" |

Be explicit about the limits of this standard. Every format comparison here comes from survey panels answering attitude, health or personality batteries. None was run on Prolific or SONA. No study has run a configural-to-scalar invariance test of a psychological scale between one item per screen and stacked labelled items. The closest is Thorndike's clinical crossover, which found the "same construct" and equivalent factor means ([Thorndike et al. 2009](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/111/paper-detail/72164)) and is verified only through a summary. No experiment has tested whether mixing formats *across* instruments in one session hurts data. The case for one format per session rests on carryover findings and ITC usability guidance, not on a direct test.

## Conclusion

The drift matters less for what the lab's numbers *say* and more for what they can later be *compared with*. Scale means are robust to this choice. Correlations, factor loadings and context effects are not, and each format carries its own artifact: proximity assimilation on stacked pages, sequential priming one item at a time. So the important decision is less which format to choose than committing to one for each study and keeping it unchanged for that study's lifetime. Recording it per row is what makes cross-study pooling defensible. Stacked cards win the default on efficiency, mobile fit, closeness to paper norms, and the fact that the lab's newest norms are being built in that format. Under answer-required rules, one item per screen would win on per-item paradata and straightlining by only a small margin.

The lab is also unusually well placed to close the gap in the evidence. It owns the renderer, recruits from the very platforms the literature has not studied, and already logs responses append-only. Randomizing format (one item per screen vs stacked cards) within one cheap single-session Prolific study of a few established scales would cost a few hundred participants. It would produce something no published source offers: a direct invariance test, speeder-rate comparison and phone-versus-PC breakdown for exactly this sample and these formats. Run before the legacy renderer retires, it would also tell the longitudinal studies whether their eventual successors can be compared with them directly or need a format covariate.
