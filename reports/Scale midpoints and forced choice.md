# Neither fence sitting nor forcing wins outright

No consensus has emerged on whether agree–disagree rating scales should offer a middle category, and the reason is not a shortage of studies. Different research traditions measure "better" by different yardsticks, and on those yardsticks they reach opposite answers. On the outcomes psychologists usually check, the midpoint barely matters. In the one large randomized trait study that posed the odd-versus-even question directly, adding or removing the midpoint had no detectable psychometric effect, and internal consistency, test–retest reliability and criterion validity plateau somewhere between 5 and 7 points whether the count is odd or even. On the outcomes survey methodologists and IRT modellers check, the midpoint matters a great deal. Offering it draws in roughly 10–20% of answers. It shifts response distributions, net agreement and extreme responding. A large share of the people who choose it, sometimes most of them, are not neutral: they mean "it depends", "I don't know" or "I'd rather not say". Removing it does not make those people disappear. It moves them, unequally, into the adjacent categories, where they can no longer be seen. The narrow points the field does agree on are these: too few points (2–3) are bad, fully labelled scales are better, the midpoint is a mixed category rather than a true scale centre, and odd and even versions of the same items are not interchangeable. The last point matters most for the lab. The Sense Foraging Scale's 6-point Prolific data and 7-point classroom data can be compared at the level of correlations and factor patterns with moderate confidence. They cannot be compared item by item, by distribution, or by thresholds without a calibration study, because format is fully confounded with population, setting and study design.

## A narrow consensus surrounds a genuinely open question

No professional body has issued a consensus statement on midpoints. The notes searched AAPOR, APA and ISPOR guidance and found none. The best-known psychometric experiment on the question opens by stating that "relatively little consensus exists" on the optimal number of options or on odd versus even ([Simms et al. 2019](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/does-the-number-of-response-options-matter-psychometric-perspecti/)). The meta-analyses that might have settled it, built from pre-existing survey items (Andrews 1984; Alwin & Krosnick 1991; Scherpenzeel 1995), "yielded inconsistent findings". Their authors' critics trace this to the analyses mixing subjective and objective items, and to "strong confounds between number of scale points and other item characteristics" ([Krosnick & Presser 2010, fn. 3](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)). The most systematic recent tally of response-scale design features classifies the neutral alternative as a characteristic that **demonstrably affects data quality, but in directions that differ across studies** ([DeCastellarnau 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5993837/)). That phrase is the most honest one-line summary of the field.

Four things do command broad agreement. **Two- and three-point scales perform worst, and gains level off around 5–7 points** ([Boateng et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2018.00149/full); [Lozano et al. 2008](https://portalinvestigacion.uniovi.es/documentos/5e78cab32999521b3d11f205?lang=en)). **Every point should carry a verbal label**, which raises reliability (secondary summaries of [Weng 2004](https://scholars.lib.ntu.edu.tw/handle/123456789/42130?locale=en) and [Krosnick & Presser 2009](https://benjaminhirt.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2024/01/2009_handbook_krosnick-1.pdf)). **The midpoint is not a clean "neutral"**: every study that has asked respondents what they meant by it finds a mixture of meanings (reviewed below). And **changing the format changes the data**, so scores from odd and even versions cannot be pooled as if nothing happened. Beyond that, the field splits.

The split is lopsided in an instructive way. Among the published *guidance* sources in the notes, the lean is toward offering a midpoint. Krosnick and Presser recommend it and call 7-point scales "probably optimal in many instances" ([Krosnick & Presser 2010](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)). The GESIS guidelines report that "most researchers recommend that a middle alternative should be offered" ([Menold & Bogner 2016, quoted in Wetzelhütter 2020](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484)). Weijters and colleagues advise "avoiding scales without a midpoint, unless particular, relevant reasons present themselves" ([Weijters, Cabooter & Schillewaert 2010](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)). No guidance source in the notes prescribes even scales as best practice. The *empirical* record, by contrast, contains well-designed studies favouring each side, and several finding no difference. Guidance has therefore converged further than the evidence beneath it. Scale-development textbooks such as DeVellis's present odd versus even as a trade-off rather than a rule, though that characterisation comes from secondary summaries, not a page-cited quotation ([McGill summary of DeVellis](https://blogs.mcgill.ca/crcfbrownbag/2014/05/10/devellis-r-1991-scale-development-theory-and-applications/)).

## Reliability and validity barely register the midpoint in trait scales

Psychologists usually judge a scale by internal consistency, test–retest reliability and correlations with criteria. On those yardsticks, the odd-versus-even choice on a 4- to 7-point scale is close to inconsequential. Simms and colleagues randomly assigned **1,358 undergraduates** to complete the Big Five Inventory on anything from 2 to 11 options or a visual analogue scale. Precision was attenuated for 2–5 options, criterion validity "did not follow this pattern", and there were "no psychometric advantages… for any response scales beyond 6 options" ([Simms et al. 2019, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1037/pas0000648%22&resultType=core&format=json)). A later systematic review summarises the midpoint-specific result as "including (or removing) a middle point… did not show any psychometric effects" ([Jebb, Ng & Tay 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8129175/fullTextXML)). That summary is secondary, since the abstract poses the odd/even question but does not state the answer. The paper is also often cited for the opposite view. Its press release calls six "the magic number" and describes the midpoint as "an island of apathy" ([University at Buffalo 2019](https://www.buffalo.edu/news/releases/2019/04/035.html)), but that is author commentary, not a result. What the data show is that 6 points did as well as anything longer. They do not show that 6 beat 5 or 7 because it lacked a midpoint.

Other designs point the same way. A within-subjects study of two personality questionnaires found that as the number of alternatives rose, **internal consistency increased, convergent validity did not change, and model fit worsened** ([Maydeu-Olivares et al. 2009](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22effect%20of%20varying%20the%20number%20of%20response%20alternatives%20in%20rating%20scales%22&resultType=core&format=json)). An IRT simulation found 4-, 5- and 6-point scales nearly identical, with quality deteriorating only below 3 categories (Lee & Paek 2014, as summarised by [Sun, Schmidt & Henry 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02846); secondary). In the flagship survey experiment on midpoints, a 13,000-person Euro-Barometer split ballot, Cronbach's alpha was **.64 with the midpoint and .65 without**, and predictive validity was .17 versus .21, neither difference significant ([O'Muircheartaigh, Krosnick & Helic 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf)). Validity is consistently the property least sensitive to format. Substantive correlations with outcomes rarely hinge on the midpoint decision.

The disagreement appears when the yardstick changes. The same Euro-Barometer study found the midpoint mattered once acquiescence was modelled in a structural equation model. **14 of 18 factor loadings were stronger with the midpoint offered, and 15 of 19 error variances were larger without it**, with all nine individually significant differences pointing that way. The authors concluded that people who belonged in the middle "made essentially random choices" when it was removed ([O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf)). The paper is internally inconsistent on what this means for validity. Its abstract says the midpoint raises reliability "while not affecting the validity" ([RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/har/wpaper/0103.html)). Its discussion says omission "significantly decreased the validity". The handbook that built its recommendation on this study reports both reliability and validity as improved ([Krosnick & Presser 2009](https://benjaminhirt.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2024/01/2009_handbook_krosnick-1.pdf)). The reliability result is solid. The validity claim depends on whether stronger loadings count as validity. This matters because the most-cited pro-midpoint recommendation rests largely on this single study, which first circulated as a conference paper and a working paper.

The multitrait–multimethod (MTMM) tradition, which estimates "quality" as reliability times validity across many items and countries, leans the other way. Saris and Gallhofer's meta-analysis reports that **"not providing a neutral category improves significantly both reliability and validity"**. Scherpenzeel and Saris found no reliability effect and *higher* validity with an explicit midpoint. Alwin and Krosnick found that midpoints lower reliability, though they are more valuable on 7-point scales ([DeCastellarnau 2018, Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5993837/); effect sizes not verified). Alwin's later reliability work found 3-point scales significantly less reliable than 2-point ones and attributed this to "uncertainty induced by the introduction of a potentially ambiguous midpoint" ([Sturgis et al. 2014, secondary](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf); [Maitland 2009](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/2950-how-many-scale-points-should-i-include-for-attitudinal-questions)). Four MTMM experiments in the European Social Survey found that for agree–disagree items, **5 categories gave quality of .533, against .394 for 7 and .383 for 11**, with the loss coming almost entirely through validity ([Revilla, Saris & Krosnick 2014, working-paper version](https://www.upf.edu/documents/3966940/3986764/RECSM_wp005.pdf)). That study is often read as pro-midpoint, but it is not a midpoint test. Both the 5- and the 7-point versions had midpoints, and the 5-point version was also fully labelled and vertical while the 7- and 11-point versions were end-labelled and horizontal. It speaks to whole formats, not to the middle box.

| Study or tradition | Yardstick | Verdict on offering a midpoint | Verification in notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simms et al. 2019 (BFI, N = 1,358, randomized) | Alpha, test–retest, criterion validity | No effect | Abstract verified; midpoint null via review |
| Maydeu-Olivares et al. 2009 | Alpha, convergent validity, model fit | More categories: higher alpha, same validity, worse fit | Abstract verified |
| O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000 (Euro-Barometer, N ≈ 13,000) | Alpha and criterion validity / SEM loadings and error | No difference / midpoint better | Full text verified |
| Saris & Gallhofer 2007 (MTMM meta-analysis) | Reliability × validity | Omitting is better | Direction only, via review |
| Scherpenzeel & Saris 1997 | MTMM | Midpoint: same reliability, higher validity | Direction only, via review |
| Alwin & Krosnick 1991; Alwin 2007 | Cross-item reliability | Midpoints lower reliability (3-point worst) | Secondary |
| Revilla et al. 2014 (ESS, A/D items) | MTMM quality | 5 points beat 7 and 11 (labelling confounded) | Working paper verified |

The pattern in the table is the first reason the split persists. **When the outcome is a scale-level correlation, the midpoint is nearly irrelevant. When the outcome is item-level error structure, it matters, and the sign depends on the method.** Experiments that manipulate the midpoint on fixed items tend to favour offering it. Cross-item meta-analyses, which cannot separate the midpoint from everything else about an item, tend to favour omitting it.

## The middle box holds several different answers

The firmest finding in this literature is that the midpoint is a mixture. Several independent methods converge on it.

Direct probing gives the starkest numbers. Sturgis, Roberts and Smith randomised 3,113 British omnibus respondents to one of three 5-point items on low-salience topics: the European Commission, nanotechnology and GM foods. They then asked everyone who chose "neither/nor" what they meant. **Only 13–25% held a genuinely neutral view.** After coding open-ended explanations, "between three quarters and nine-tenths" of midpoint answers concealed nonattitudes ([Sturgis et al. 2014](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)). Reallocating these hidden don't-knows changed substantive conclusions. In an ordered-logit model of EC satisfaction, gender and political interest went from significant to non-significant. The hidden don't-knows were *more* politically interested than people who openly said "don't know", which the authors read as a face-saving social-desirability motive rather than effort-saving.

Cognitive and psychometric evidence from personality items tells a related but distinct story. In think-aloud protocols on 100 personality items, "the predominant respondent orientation when selecting the Likert middle category is *it depends*". Midpoint intentions were "more closely attributable to item rather than respondent characteristics" ([Kulas & Stachowski 2013](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/respondent-rationale-for-neither-agreeing-nor-disagreeing-person-/)). Midpoint endorsements show long response latencies and "a strong, negative relationship with item clarity" ([Kulas & Stachowski 2009](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/middle-category-endorsement-in-odd-numbered-likert-response-scale/)). Asked directly, respondents described the midpoint as "no opinion", "don't care", "unsure", "neutral", "equal/both" and "neither" ([Nadler, Weston & Voyles 2015, secondary](https://www.siue.edu/education/psychology/faculty/nadlerbio.shtml)). A mixed Rasch analysis of a personality questionnaire found two latent classes. For most people the probability of choosing the middle was "very close to zero", while a non-negligible minority chose it far more often, and **"the total scores from the 2 subpopulations were incommensurate"** ([Hernández, Drasgow & González-Romá 2004](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22functioning%20of%20a%20middle%20category%20by%20means%20of%20a%20mixed-measurement%20model%22&resultType=core&format=json)). The most recent model-based estimate comes from a response-mixture IRTree fitted to a 20-item Machiavellianism scale (N = 2,000). It classified **30.8% of middle-category responses as non-informative** ([Tijmstra & Bolsinova 2025](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11873858/fullTextXML)). That is one dataset and one model: a plausible order of magnitude, not a constant.

The 75–90% figure from Sturgis and the roughly 31% figure from Tijmstra and Bolsinova do not conflict. They describe different item types. Sturgis deliberately chose obscure policy topics on which about 29% volunteered "don't know" anyway. Nobody lacks information about their own personality, so the residual non-neutral midpoint on trait items is "it depends" and item ambiguity rather than ignorance. Another contrast supports this reading. In the higher-salience Euro-Barometer science battery, the same predictors explained **25% of the variance in "don't know" answers but only 2% of the variance in midpoint answers**, and the authors concluded the two are "not manifestations of the same underlying cognitive state" ([O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf)). **How far the midpoint is a hidden "don't know" depends on topic salience. How far it is a hidden "it depends" depends on item clarity.**

Ambivalence and indifference are a third contaminant. Attitude theory has long distinguished indifference (low positive and low negative evaluation) from ambivalence (high on both), and a single midpoint cannot tell them apart although they predict behaviour differently (Thompson, Zanna & Griffin 1995, discussed in [Priester & Petty 1996](https://msbfile03.usc.edu/digitalmeasures/priester/intellcont/1996-JPSP-PriesterPetty-1.pdf)). The forced-choice side of the debate assumes that people who would sit on the fence have a lean and will reveal it. The evidence suggests they do not reveal it neutrally: ambivalent respondents who are forced to choose "tend to react negatively" ([Weijters et al. 2010](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)). No study in the notes checks forced-choice answers from ambivalent respondents against a behavioural criterion. The claim that forcing a choice recovers a true lean therefore remains untested, not refuted.

Two popular accounts of the midpoint fare poorly. The first is satisficing, the idea that the midpoint is an easy way out for low-effort respondents. It explains "don't know" answers well but midpoint answers badly. Attraction to the middle was unrelated to education in four studies. It was greater among people with weaker attitudes or less interest in the topic, and Krosnick and Presser call the overall evidence "mixed" ([Krosnick & Presser 2010](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)). Two studies found offering a midpoint had the *most* impact on the *most* educated and cognitively skilled ([O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf)). The second is the claim that removing the midpoint pushes people toward the socially desirable pole. That has no consistent direction. In a New Zealand omnibus (n ≈ 450), dropping the midpoint **doubled "unimportant" answers from 8% to 16%**, which Garland read as the midpoint having sheltered a socially undesirable view. He notes an earlier study found the opposite shift, toward the positive pole, and concludes the effect "is content specific" ([Garland 1991](http://marketing-bulletin.massey.ac.nz/V2/MB_V2_N3_Garland.pdf)). Johns, working with British Election Study items, describes the midpoint as a "safe haven" for a "silent minority". He recommends offering it on obscure topics and omitting it on controversial ones ([Johns 2005, abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13689880500178849)). This evidence base is small: one small omnibus survey, one analysis known only from its abstract, and inference.

A separate "don't know" option does not cleanly fix this, and the two studies that tested it disagree. In face-to-face British interviews, putting "don't know" on the show card raised such answers by about 7 points but **did not reduce neither/nor answers at all**. The new "don't knows" came from the substantive categories instead ([Sturgis et al. 2014](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)). In an online experiment with 1,282 Austrian students rating unipolar satisfaction items, adding a "don't know" option **cut midpoint use from about 7 to about 4 answers per 20 items** (t = 9.41) ([Wetzelhütter 2020](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484-scale-sensitive-response-behavior-consequences-of-offering-versus-omitting-a-don-t-know-option-and-or-a-middle-category)). The difference in mode and item type is the obvious candidate explanation, but nobody has tested it. Separately, nine experiments found that explicit no-opinion filters raise abstention without improving over-time consistency or validity. The extra "don't knows" came disproportionately from the least educated and least effortful respondents ([Krosnick et al. 2002](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2002/2002%20The%20impact%20of%20no%20opinion%20response%20options%20on%20data%20quality-%20N.pdf)).

## Removing the midpoint relocates the problem rather than deleting it

The best-supported claim in this whole literature is that the midpoint's presence changes the shape of the data. Classic split-ballot experiments agree on the size of the pull. In the Euro-Barometer split, the offered midpoint drew **a mean of 14.6% of answers (range 6–23%) across 19 items**, comparable to Schuman and Presser's experiments ([O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf)). The classic experiments disagree about what happens to everyone else. Schuman and Presser found little change in the pro:con balance or in correlations with background variables. Kalton et al. and Bishop found changes that were real but small and "not in a consistent direction" ([Sturgis et al. 2014](https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/sturgisrobertssmith.pdf)). In the Euro-Barometer data, the agree:disagree ratio among substantive answers was 64:36 with the midpoint and 62:38 without it: small, significant, and tilted toward disagreement when the midpoint was removed.

The cleanest modern experiment makes that tilt larger and shows how it works. Weijters, Cabooter and Schillewaert randomised **1,207 Belgian online-panel respondents** to 4, 5, 6 or 7 points, crossed with full versus endpoint-only labelling. The design separates the midpoint (present or absent) from the number of gradations of agreement. **Including a midpoint significantly raised net acquiescence, lowered extreme responding, and lowered misresponse to reverse-keyed items.** The misresponse benefit was significantly larger when every point was labelled (Wald χ² = 13.31, p = .0003). The authors' account is that ambivalent respondents denied a midpoint move "disproportionately" toward disagreement and toward the extremes. The practical magnitude is large: **the share of respondents expressing positive intentions on the same items ranged from 22.6% to 60.6% across formats** ([Weijters et al. 2010, working paper](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf); [published version](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ijrema/v27y2010i3p236-247.html)). The 4-to-5 difference was larger than the 6-to-7 difference, so the effect shrinks when the scale already has three gradations per side. That bears directly on the lab's 6-versus-7 case. The sample was men only, Dutch-speaking, and rating consumer items, so generalisation to women, students or self-report items is untested. Wetzelhütter's student experiment found the same signature: omitting the middle category significantly increased extreme answers and decreased mild ones ([Wetzelhütter 2020](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/14484-scale-sensitive-response-behavior-consequences-of-offering-versus-omitting-a-don-t-know-option-and-or-a-middle-category)). A secondary summary of Nadler et al. reports that changing the response options **significantly shifted a quarter of item means** ([JMU summary](https://www.jmu.edu/pair/ads/surveys/continuing-survey/cssmethodschange.pdf)).

Two of the experiments in the notes disagree on acquiescence. Weijters found that the midpoint *raises* net agreement. In the Euro-Barometer study, acquiescence-factor loadings were identical with and without the midpoint (.33 versus .32) ([O'Muircheartaigh et al. 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://harrisschool.uchicago.edu/about/publications/working-papers/pdf/wp_01_3.pdf)). The two are less contradictory than they look. Weijters's net-agreement index is a ratio of agree to disagree counts, and the effect he found is that forced choice *deflates* agreement by pushing ambivalent people negative. That is not the same as the midpoint *creating* yea-saying. Neither study supports the folk claim that removing the midpoint cures acquiescence. Agree–disagree formats carry their own acquiescence cost, which is larger than the midpoint question: 10–20% of respondents agree with both a statement and its reversal, and balancing the keying only moves acquiescers to the centre of the score range ([Pasek & Krosnick 2010](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2009/2009%2003-Leighly-Chapter03.pdf)). Response styles bias scale means and inflate or deflate correlations between scales in predictable ways, depending on how many items are reverse-keyed and how far the true mean sits from the midpoint ([Baumgartner & Steenkamp 2001](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/response-styles-in-marketing-research-a-cross-national-investigat/)). Extreme response style appears regardless of scale length and behaves like a stable personal trait ([Kieruj & Moors 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/qualqt/v47y2013i1p193-211.html)). Removing the midpoint therefore swaps one style the analyst can see (midpoint responding) for heavier loading on one that is harder to see (extreme responding).

For factor analysis and IRT, the consequences follow directly. If some midpoint answers are non-informative, then "the assumption of ordinality of the item scores can no longer be maintained" ([Tijmstra & Bolsinova 2025](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11873858/fullTextXML)). That undermines the threshold model behind polychoric correlations and graded-response IRT. In Tijmstra and Bolsinova's simulations, treating non-informative midpoints as ordinal attenuated item discriminations and biased trait estimates. With 50 items and 20% non-informative midpoints, average absolute bias more than doubled. A mixture IRTree that models the midpoint as a separate decision cut absolute bias by 44–45% and mean squared error by 24–28%. Modellers now routinely distinguish a "midpoint primary process" for odd scales from an "agreement primary process" for even ones ([Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1003756/full)). Here is the part the forced-choice camp tends to miss. **The noise from a midpoint is a modelling problem, not an intrinsic property of offering one.** With the midpoint present, the ambiguity is visible: it can be counted per item, used to flag items for rewording ([Kulas & Stachowski 2009](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/middle-category-endorsement-in-odd-numbered-likert-response-scale/)), and modelled. With it absent, the same ambiguity sits inside "slightly agree" and "slightly disagree", where no model can find it. The case for forced choice is mirror-image and also real. If the analysis will be plain sum scores or polychoric EFA, a midpoint carrying 30% non-informative answers adds noise that the even format avoids. Whether forced respondents then split at random (O'Muircheartaigh) or lean systematically negative (Weijters) decides whether the even format gains discrimination or adds bias. No study in the notes estimates trait-score bias for the same people under both formats in an IRT linking design.

## Culture, careless responding and smartphones add less than expected

Cross-cultural evidence is the strongest practical argument for even scales, and it is weaker than often presented. East Asian respondents use the midpoint more than North Americans, and Americans use the extremes more. Within each culture, individualism predicts more extreme and less midpoint responding. Yet these style differences **"generally did not alter cross-cultural comparisons of item means"** in high-school students from Japan, Taiwan, Canada and the US ([Chen, Lee & Stevenson 1995](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.1995.tb00327.x/)). In managers, dropping the midpoint decreased the central tendency of Chinese, Japanese and Hong Kong respondents and increased their variance ([Si & Cullen 1998](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/eb028885/full/html)). That is a distributional change, not a demonstrated gain in comparability. A nursing study found construct validity was better with 4 options for Chinese and American respondents but with 7 for Japanese respondents ([Lee et al. 2002, via a secondary aggregator](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/l/likert+scale+questions.html)). On that evidence, no single format is culture-fair. A 26-country study found Spanish-speaking respondents more extreme and acquiescent, and Japanese and Chinese respondents "far less extreme" ([Harzing 2006, secondary](https://harzing.com/blog/2017/02/what-if-fully-agree-doesnt-mean-the-same-thing-across-cultures)). No study in the notes shows that forced choice improves measurement invariance or criterion validity across cultures. Removing the midpoint removes differential midpoint responding but leaves acquiescence and extreme-response differences in place. Given the Weijters result, it may convert some East Asian fence-sitting into differential disagreement, though that is an untested inference.

For careless responding, the key fact is an absence. **No randomized odd-versus-even experiment measuring attention-check failure, long-string runs or other careless-responding indices was found.** The careless-responding literature treats midpoint runs as a symptom to detect, not format as a manipulation. Partially random responders have means closer to the midpoint, which can make findings spurious or inflate them ([Stosic et al. 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/25152459241231581); search snippet only). One machine-learning study reportedly found "uniform midpoint response" to be the commonest careless pattern ([Bloy et al. 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/25152459251378420); snippet only, unverified). Neither shows that removing the midpoint reduces carelessness. A careless respondent facing an even scale can still straightline on "slightly agree". A midpoint-run index on its own also confounds low effort with a stable, culturally patterned style. It should be combined with response time and attention checks rather than used alone.

The online and mobile evidence is reassuring for the lab's Prolific work. A randomized crossover experiment in the Dutch LISS probability panel found **no device difference in non-differentiation (0.35 on mobile versus 0.34 on PC)**, and smartphone respondents were "at least as likely to provide conscientious and thoughtful answers" ([Antoun, Couper & Conrad 2017](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/81/S1/280/3091905)). Two Danish probability samples found **no significant device effect on midpoint responding** in either year, and "no systematic evidence of device effects" overall ([Clement et al. 2020](https://surveyinsights.org/?p=13585)). Nonprobability panels show more straightlining than probability panels, and an experimental change to the visual design of the midpoint had no significant effect in either ([Cornesse & Blom 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0049124120914940); abstract only). No study in the notes compares midpoint use on Prolific or MTurk with probability panels, or tests odd versus even within a crowdsourced sample. One design hazard is real but separate from response style: a slider whose handle starts at the centre records a respondent who never touched it as a midpoint answer.

## Five confounds keep the field from converging

The split persists because studies that look as if they test the same question are testing different ones. Five dimensions separate them, and each predicts which way a study will come out.

| Confound | Studies favouring a midpoint typically… | Studies favouring forced choice or finding no difference typically… |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome criterion | Measure item error variance, loadings, misresponse to reversed items, distributional distortion | Measure alpha, criterion correlations, or MTMM quality pooled across many items |
| Dimension type | Use bipolar items where neutrality is conceptually possible | Mix in unipolar items, where the middle is "moderately", not "neutral" |
| Question format | Study agree–disagree batteries, where ambivalence is common | Treat agree–disagree as given; item-specific formats outperform both ([Saris et al. 2010](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/handle/document/44197)) |
| Mode and topic | Use self-administered personality items, or salient topics | Use interviewer-administered, low-salience political items, where the midpoint hides "don't know" |
| Format bundling | Manipulate the midpoint alone (Weijters's decomposition) | Change number of points, labelling and orientation together (Revilla et al.) |

The criterion split is the deepest of the five, because each camp is right about its own yardstick. A scale-level correlation averages over items, so a midpoint that adds noise to some answers and a forced format that adds bias to others both wash out. That is why Simms's criterion validity was flat. Item-level error and distributional measures do not average out, so they detect the format. The dimension split dissolves much of the debate for frequency and intensity scales. On a unipolar "never" to "always" format, the middle category is a moderate frequency, not an abstention, so "fence sitting" does not apply. Practice reflects this: MAIA-2 uses a 0–5 frequency format, "Never" to "Always", with no neutral point by design ([Mehling et al. 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6279042/)).

The question-format split puts the whole debate in proportion. Randomized MTMM experiments found agree–disagree items "had much lower quality" than comparable item-specific questions ([Saris, Revilla, Krosnick & Shaeffer 2010](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/handle/document/44197)). A 2022 review recommends item-specific formats "for most purposes" ([Dykema et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8692311)). For an agree–disagree battery, the larger threat to validity is probably the agree–disagree format itself, and the midpoint is second-order next to it.

The mode-and-topic split explains why survey methodologists and personality psychologists talk past each other. Almost all of the hidden-"don't know", social-desirability and satisficing evidence comes from **interviewer-administered general-population surveys about political and policy attitudes**. The notes found little on self-administered ratings of personal experience. There, people always have *some* state, so a midpoint more plausibly means "moderate", "mixed" or "it depends" than "uninformed". Generalising the 75–90% hidden-"don't know" figure to psychological self-report is untested.

The bundling split warns against reading any "5 versus 7" or "4 versus 5" study as a midpoint test. The 4-versus-5 comparison changes the midpoint *and* the number of points. Revilla's 5-versus-7 comparison changed labelling and orientation as well. Only designs like Weijters's, which decompose the number of points into midpoint and gradations, isolate the midpoint itself.

## What this means for the Sense Foraging Scale

### The 6-point and 7-point datasets cannot be pooled as they stand

> **Correction, 2026-10-10.** This section was written as if both datasets already exist. Neither does yet: Sense Foraging Study 1 (6-point, Prolific) has not launched, and the PSY240 RCT (7-point) launches on 2026-10-17, and both hold only test runs. The cheapest remedy in this whole section is therefore still available: **give both studies the same response format before either collects data**, which removes the comparability problem rather than managing it. Everything below applies only if the formats are left different, or to any future study that uses a different format.

The lab's two Sense Foraging datasets differ in format: 6 points without a midpoint versus 7 points with one. They also differ in population (Prolific adults versus students), setting (unsupervised online versus classroom), and design context (a validation EFA versus an RCT). **Format is completely confounded with everything else, so no difference between the datasets can be attributed to format, and no analysis of the existing data alone can remove it.** That is the binding constraint, and it shapes what each kind of comparison can bear.

Within the RCT, nothing is compromised. Treatment-versus-control and pre-versus-post comparisons use one format throughout, so the format choice affects their precision and interpretation but not their internal validity. The risk lies entirely in cross-study comparison: importing the EFA's factor structure, item statistics or score distributions into the RCT, or setting the two samples' means side by side.

Correlational and structural comparisons are the safest cross-study use. The evidence that criterion validity is insensitive to format (Simms; Maydeu-Olivares; the Euro-Barometer alpha and validity nulls) supports comparing the *pattern* of loadings, subscale intercorrelations and correlations with MAIA-2 or PHQ-4 across the two datasets. Treating 6- and 7-point items as continuous is conventionally acceptable, since items with 5–7 categories and no strong floor or ceiling can be treated as continuous in CFA ([Boateng et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2018.00149/full)). On that basis, configural and metric (loading) invariance across datasets can be tested on the raw or rescaled items. The literature also predicts specific ways the factor structures might differ, which turns a vague worry into checks. If the scale has reverse-keyed items, the 6-point data should show more misresponse to them ([Weijters et al. 2010](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)), and so possibly a stronger method factor for reversed items, than the 7-point data. In the 7-point data, "it depends" midpoint use concentrates on ambiguous items ([Kulas & Stachowski 2013](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/respondent-rationale-for-neither-agreeing-nor-disagreeing-person-/)), so the lab should tabulate the midpoint rate per item in the RCT data. Items with high midpoint rates are the ones most likely to load differently, and the first candidates for rewording.

Mean-level comparison needs more caution. Linear rescaling to a common metric, such as percent of maximum possible (POMP), (x − min)/(max − min) × 100, is the conventional first step. A randomized split-sample comparison of 5-, 7- and 10-point formats reported approximately comparable rescaled means ([Dawes 2008, via secondary summary](https://scholars.nova.edu/en/publications/how-respondents-use-verbal-and-numeric-rating-scales-a-case-for-r/); details unverified). But linear stretching assumes respondents use categories the same way across formats, an assumption the scale-homogenisation literature specifically rejects ([de Jonge, Veenhoven & Arends 2014](https://personal.eur.nl/veenhoven/Pub2010s/2014g-ab.html)). The midpoint-specific evidence predicts a *direction* of bias. If the Weijters pattern holds for these items, the 6-point data will look less agreeing and more extreme than the same people would on 7 points. At the scale level the gap may be modest, because the effect is smaller at six versus seven than at four versus five. That makes POMP-rescaled means across the two datasets descriptive at best, and any difference between them uninterpretable without a calibration study.

Threshold-level invariance is not testable as the data stand. Standard categorical multigroup CFA needs the same number of categories in each group. Collapsing categories to force equality affects fit and costs information ([Elosua, secondary](https://www.uv.es/psicologica/articulos2.11/13ELOSUA.pdf)). Every way of forcing the 7-point data onto 6 points has a cost. Merging the midpoint into "slightly agree" assumes forced respondents lean positive, which Weijters contradicts. Merging it into "slightly disagree" assumes they lean negative. Splitting midpoint answers at random between the two assumes the O'Muircheartaigh random-scatter model. Each is an assumption the data cannot check. The lab could fit separate graded-response IRT models and link them through anchor items assumed to be invariant ([Kolen & Brennan, via sirt documentation](https://rdrr.io/pkg/sirt/man/linking.haberman.html)). Without common persons or random assignment, though, any linking rests on an untestable assumption about the two samples' latent distributions. Here that assumption is especially weak, because one sample is students in an RCT and the other is adults on Prolific. Changing a validated scale's response format changes its error structure and calls for re-validation rather than rescaling ([Sun, Schmidt & Henry 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02846)).

A small calibration study is the clean solution, and the literature supplies its design. In a random-groups design, one population is randomly assigned to the 6-point or the 7-point version. That supports observed-score (equipercentile) equating, which has been shown to work for harmonising instruments that differ in response format, including in online nonprobability samples ([Singh 2022](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/7916)). It also allows a direct invariance test of format with population held constant. A single-group design, where the same people answer both formats in counterbalanced order, gives more information per person but risks carry-over. Either design yields a crosswalk from 7-point to 6-point scores, or the reverse, that can be applied to the existing datasets. It also shows empirically where would-be midpoint choosers go when the midpoint is removed, for these particular items. Because the RCT population is students, a calibration sample drawn only from Prolific would leave population and format entangled for the crosswalk's intended use. Including a student arm, or at least checking that the crosswalk is stable across age groups, closes that gap. The 7-point arm can also be fitted with a midpoint IRTree ([Tijmstra & Bolsinova 2025](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11873858/fullTextXML)) to estimate how much of the scale's midpoint use is non-informative. That number is the most direct evidence the lab could gather on which format suits this instrument. The notes do not supply a sample-size rule for equating designs, so sizing the study is a separate power question.

The established instruments need no such decision. MAIA-2 (0–5) and PHQ-4 (0–3, as the lab administers it) are unipolar frequency formats, where the middle is "sometimes" rather than "neutral" and the odd/even debate does not apply. They should be administered exactly as published, since altering their format would void their validation and norms.

### A lab default for new agree–disagree items depends on five questions

The literature does not settle a universal default, and it would misstate the evidence to claim otherwise. It does settle what a defensible default looks like and what should override it. Whichever default the lab picks, three things are supported regardless of the midpoint. **Label every point with words. Stay within 5–7 points. Hold the format fixed for a given instrument across all its studies**, because once a scale is in use, consistency is worth more than the small differences between formats. The comparability problem above shows the cost of not doing so. For the Sense Foraging Scale specifically, the choice is less "which format is better" than "which dataset is the reference". The validation sample, with its published factor structure and norms, usually sets the format later studies should match, unless the calibration study shows the other format works better for these items.

For new bipolar agree–disagree items, five questions should drive the choice. They come from the variables that decide which way studies fall. First, **is neutrality a meaningful position on this construct?** If a respondent can genuinely be neither, as with attitudes, evaluations or mixed states, forcing a choice distorts, and ambivalent respondents drift negative ([Weijters et al. 2010](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)). If the dimension is really a tendency everyone has to some degree, the even format loses little. Second, **are the items clear and context-free?** If "it depends" is the likely reason for sitting in the middle, rewriting the item does more than deleting the midpoint ([Kulas & Stachowski 2013](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/respondent-rationale-for-neither-agreeing-nor-disagreeing-person-/)). Third, **are the items sensitive or socially loaded?** Here the midpoint can shelter undesirable answers ([Garland 1991](http://marketing-bulletin.massey.ac.nz/V2/MB_V2_N3_Garland.pdf); [Johns 2005](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13689880500178849)), which favours forced choice. Fourth, **does the scale use reverse-keyed items?** The midpoint reduces misresponse to them, especially when fully labelled ([Weijters et al. 2010](https://public.vlerick.com/Publications/74eb09c8-6aa9-e011-8a89-005056a635ed.pdf)), which favours offering it. Fifth, **what analysis is planned?** IRT with a midpoint-aware model gains from the visible midpoint. Plain sum scores and polychoric EFA absorb its non-informative share as noise. Two secondary considerations sit alongside these. Samples that mix cultures differ in midpoint use, but no format choice fixes that, and modelling or invariance testing is the better remedy. And because agree–disagree is itself the weaker format, converting items to item-specific wording ([Dykema et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8692311)) is a larger potential gain than either midpoint choice.

Applied to typical sense-foraging-style self-report items, which are clear, non-sensitive statements about one's own tendencies, these questions do not point decisively either way. That is the honest result. A fully labelled 7-point (or 5-point) scale with a midpoint follows the majority of published guidance and the best controlled evidence on response styles and reversed items. A fully labelled 6-point scale is backed by the largest randomized trait study, which found no psychometric cost, and it avoids a category that a non-trivial minority use as a non-answer. A lab could adopt either as a default and defend it in review, provided it states which considerations drove the choice and does not switch formats within an instrument.

## Conclusion

The question "should a scale have a midpoint?" turns out to be several questions, and the field has answered the pieces separately. For scale-level reliability and validity, the answer is that it barely matters. For item-level distributions, response styles and reversed items, it clearly matters, and offering a labelled midpoint looks better. For low-salience opinion items in interviews, the midpoint largely hides "don't know". For sensitive items it can shelter undesirable answers. The persistent "split" is mostly these answers being mistaken for rival answers to one question. The genuine open problem is narrower than the debate suggests. It concerns what forced respondents actually do on the ambiguous middle of a self-report scale: scatter at random, lean negative, or reveal a true tilt. That has never been checked against a behavioural criterion, and it is the evidence that would decide between the camps.

For the lab, the practical consequence is that the more expensive mistake, running one instrument in two formats in two non-comparable samples, is about to happen and can still be avoided: neither Sense Foraging study has collected real data (see the correction above). Aligning the two formats before launch matters more than which default the lab picks. A modest randomized calibration study would turn that mistake into an asset. It would give the Sense Foraging Scale a crosswalk between its two datasets, and an item-level estimate of how much of its midpoint use is genuine neutrality. Those are the two facts the literature cannot supply on the lab's behalf, and the second would make the format decision evidence-based for this instrument instead of an inherited convention.
